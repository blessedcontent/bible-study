What Love Does

A Portrait of LoveLesson 7Day 3 of 7

In brief

Ask a hundred people what love is and you will get feelings. Ask Paul and you get footage. Today he hands the Corinthians seven verbs you could catch on camera. Love waits without the sigh. Love does the kind thing first. Love shows up at somebody else's celebration and means it. Love carries, trusts, hopes, and outlasts. There is not a single emotion on the list, because Paul is describing how a person filled with the Spirit behaves, not how they feel. Love is not a feeling we wait for. It is a set of verbs we practice, and the practice becomes a life.

Yesterday

Yesterday ended with a cup of coffee going cold on a desk, love losing temperature one unnoticed degree at a time, and with Paul's arithmetic still ringing. Everything minus love equals nothing. Which raises the obvious next question. If love is the one ingredient that makes the rest count, what exactly is it? Today Paul answers, and he refuses to hand us a definition we can frame and forget. He hands us a work order. Seven verbs, each one something love does with its hands, each one something you could watch happen across a kitchen table.

Historical Context

Kindness Already Had a History

On Sabbath mornings in Corinth, before there was a church, there was a synagogue, and inside it a reader unrolling a Greek scroll. Most of Corinth's Jews, and the God-fearing Greeks who slipped in beside them, did not read Hebrew. They knew the Scriptures through the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, and in that translation one family of words kept surfacing whenever the Psalms talked about God. Kindness words. The Lord is good to everyone. His compassion rests on all He has made. Week after week, the congregation heard kindness described not as good manners but as the settled habit of the Almighty.

So when Paul's letter reached the church and someone read the words love is kind aloud, the verb did not arrive empty. Paul reached for that same word family, the one the Greek Psalms had spent centuries filling with the character of God. His converts had been singing about a kind God long before they were told to be kind people. That is the quiet force of today's list. Paul is not inventing virtues from scratch. He is taking the family traits of the God of Israel and writing them into the job description of everyone who claims to be His child. Kindness was God's reputation first. Now it is supposed to be ours.

Into the Word

Somewhere today a person will say I love you and mean I enjoy how you make me feel. Paul writes four verses that make that sentence impossible to leave alone. He takes love out of the heart, where nobody can check on it, and moves it into the daylight of behavior, where everybody can. What follows is the heart of the whole chapter, a portrait of love painted entirely in motion. Read it slowly. Every verb is aimed at somebody.

Keep walking the list and it gets more surprising. Love rejoices in the truth, and behind that phrase sits a very specific skill, the ability to be glad alongside someone else. It is the joy of the neighbors who celebrated with Elizabeth over her impossible baby (Luke 1:58), the shepherd shouting rejoice with me over one found sheep (Luke 15:6), the woman throwing a party over one found coin (Luke 15:9), the body where one member's honor makes every member glad (1 Corinthians 12:26), and Paul himself, chained and still rejoicing with his friends (Philippians 2:17, 18). Then come the four load-bearing verbs. Love bears, believes, hopes, endures, all things (1 Corinthians 13:7). Bearing and enduring are near twins, the same tough endurance Paul practiced when he chose to put up with anything rather than hinder the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:12). Believing all things means leading with the benefit of the doubt. Hoping all things means expecting good on the far side of hard. Love endures precisely by believing and hoping. It lasts because it refuses to stop expecting.

Street Level

The Butterflies Grew Up

At every fiftieth anniversary party, somebody finally asks the question. What's the secret? And the husband, who has been dreading the microphone all evening, gives an answer that disappoints the whole room. We just kept showing up, he says. I make her tea. She warms my side of the bed. The crowd wanted butterflies and he handed them chores. But watch that man for a week. He starts her car on cold mornings so the seat is warm before she gets in. He fills the pill organizer every Sunday night, her days and his, whether or not he feels anything in particular while he does it. Ask him which of those mornings he felt in love, and he honestly could not tell you. The feelings came and went a thousand times across fifty years, like weather. The verbs never missed a day.

That is what Paul is handing us in today's passage. Not a rush to wait for. Seven verbs to run today, patience and kindness and rejoicing on purpose, practiced on schedule through every kind of feeling-weather. In a love like that, the butterflies do not die. They grow up. They get jobs. And given enough mornings, the practice stops being something a person does and becomes someone the person is, which is exactly what the Spirit grows in anyone who keeps choosing the verb. Fruit does not appear in a day. It ripens on a schedule of small, repeated yeses.

Today’s World

The Feelings Followed the Verbs

In June 2025, researchers at the University of California, San Francisco published the results of a large-scale global well-being study. They called it the Big Joy Project, and the assignment could not have been humbler. For seven days, five to ten minutes a day, each participant performed one small prescribed act, and the daily list reads like Paul's portrait with a login screen. Do a kind thing for someone. Write down what you are grateful for. Celebrate another person's joy on purpose. Nobody was asked to feel anything first. The acts were scheduled, mood or no mood. In all, 48,789 people consented to take part, 17,598 completed the measures before and after the week, and 169 countries were represented. When the team analyzed the results, the week of tiny verbs had measurably raised emotional well-being and positive emotion, lowered stress, and improved self-reported health and sleep quality, with benefits comparable to programs that run ten or twelve weeks. The largest gains landed on the youngest and most disadvantaged participants.

Notice the order of operations, because it is the whole finding. The feelings were not the fuel. They were the exhaust. People acted first, on schedule, and the joy showed up behind the acting, which is precisely the architecture Paul built into today's passage. Love is patient. Love is kind. Love rejoices with. Verbs, all of them, available on days when the warm feeling never reports for duty. Scripture assigned these reps two thousand years before anyone measured them, and it also named what the researchers were watching grow. Fruit. A week of deliberate kindness does not make anyone a finished orchard, and no app can pour God's love into a heart. That is the Spirit's work. But the study caught the mechanism in daylight. Choose the verb, and the verb starts changing the person running it. The feelings were never the engine. The verbs were.

Christ at the center

Every verb on today's list, Jesus performed in sandals, on foot, at street level. Patience looked like three years of explaining the kingdom to twelve men who kept asking about thrones. Kindness looked like a hand laid on a leper who had not been touched in years. Rejoicing with looked like a wedding at Cana, where He kept the celebration alive with the best wine of the day. Bearing all things looked like a Teacher washing the feet of the man about to sell Him. And believing all things looked like breakfast on a beach, where He handed the ministry back to the friend who had denied Him three times, because He saw who Peter would become and not just what Peter had done. We will spend Wednesday gazing at this portrait in full. For now it is enough to notice one thing. Not one verb stayed theoretical. In Jesus, every line of the love chapter got up and walked.

Application

Pick the verb on Paul's list that comes least naturally to you and run it once today, on purpose. If it is patience, let the slow moment pass without the sigh or the phone. If it is kindness, do the unrequested helpful thing for the person you find hardest. If it is rejoicing with, find somebody's good news today and celebrate it like it happened to you, no comparison allowed. One verb, one deliberate rep, before you sleep tonight.

Discussion Question

"Which of Paul's seven verbs is hardest for you right now (patience with a slow situation, kindness to a difficult person, honest joy at someone else's success, holding on when trust has been bruised)? And since this love is the Spirit's fruit rather than raw willpower, what would it look like to ask God for that verb this week instead of just trying harder at it?"

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for refusing to leave love vague. We have hidden behind the vagueness, calling ourselves loving because we felt warm things while doing nothing. Today You showed us seven verbs, and we felt the list read us back. We confess the sighs that leak out of our patience. We confess the kindness we ration to people who can return it. We confess how much easier it is to weep with those who weep than to honestly rejoice with those who rejoice. Spirit of God, grow Your fruit in us, because we have tried manufacturing it and the plastic version fools no one. Give us one honest rep of the hardest verb today, and the strength to repeat it tomorrow, until Your love stops being something we admire and becomes something our families can feel. In Jesus' name, Amen.
