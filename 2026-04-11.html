Pride Versus Humility

Pride Versus HumilityLesson 3Day 1 of 7

In brief

Pride is the oldest spiritual sickness and the hardest one to diagnose in yourself.

  • ☩ Pride is not just arrogance; it is any posture that places self at the center where God belongs
  • ☩ The Bible identifies pride as the fundamental barrier to a deep relationship with God
  • ☩ In the ancient world, humility was considered weakness; Jesus made it the gateway to true greatness
  • ☩ This week explores how pride quietly damages our relationship with God and how Christ Himself models the cure

Last week

Last week we explored the character of the God we are trying to know: His holiness that makes every other attribute safe, His love that flows from who He is rather than how we perform, His creative power that spans galaxies yet kneels to form a single human being, and His incarnation in Jesus where every attribute converged in one Person. We ended the week staring at the fullness of God revealed in Christ. Now the question shifts. If this is who God is, what is the biggest obstacle keeping us from growing closer to Him? The answer Scripture gives, consistently and urgently, is pride. We all battle it. We all underestimate it. We all assume the person next to us has a bigger problem with it than we do. And that assumption is itself a symptom. This week we confront the one thing that stands most stubbornly between us and the God we have just begun to see clearly.

Historical Context

When Humility Was a Dirty Word

Nobody in the Roman Empire aspired to be humble. The entire social fabric of the first-century Mediterranean world was woven around honor and shame: a public scoreboard that determined everything from dinner invitations to political alliances to whether your children could marry well. Roman citizens spent fortunes on public banquets, stone inscriptions, and monuments to their own generosity, because visible status was the currency of influence. The Greek word tapeinophrosyne, which we translate as "humility," literally meant "lowliness of mind," and in secular Greek usage it was an insult. It described the posture of slaves, prisoners, and people who had been publicly defeated. No philosopher praised it. No orator aspired to it. To be tapeinos was to be crushed.

Into this world walked Jesus of Nazareth, who sat down at a Pharisee's dinner party and told the guests to stop scrambling for the best seats (Luke 14:7-11). He told them to take the lowest place and wait to be called higher. And He made this astonishing claim: the one who humbles himself will be exalted. His audience would have heard this as a direct assault on everything their culture valued. Jesus was not offering a minor social adjustment. He was inverting the entire honor system of the ancient world, replacing it with something His listeners had no category for: a God who considers voluntary lowliness to be the highest form of greatness.

That cultural revolution is the backdrop for this entire week. Every passage we will study, from the tight grip of worldly pride in 1 John 2:15-17 to the radical self-emptying of Christ in Philippians 2:3-8, only makes full sense against this background. The word "humility" has been softened by centuries of Christian influence until it sounds almost pleasant. In the first century, it was a scandal.

Christ at the center

As the week unfolds, every lesson will point toward the same Person. Sunday will show us what pride grips and refuses to release. Monday will force us to look honestly in the mirror. Tuesday will give us a human example of humility in Moses. Wednesday will confront us with pride's real cost before God. But Thursday is where the week arrives at its destination: Philippians 2:5-8, where we see the One who held every right to the highest seat in the universe and chose instead to take the form of a servant. Jesus did not merely teach humility. He became it. And that changes everything about what it means to follow Him.

Application

Before Sunday, take five honest minutes with a pen and paper. Write down three specific situations where pride tends to surface in your life: conversations you need to dominate, comparisons you quietly make, moments where you resist correction or refuse to admit you were wrong. Do not edit or soften them. Fold the paper and keep it somewhere you will find it on Friday. At the end of this week, you will revisit what you wrote and measure it against what Scripture has revealed.

Opening Question for the Week

"If someone who knows you well were asked where pride shows up most in your life, what would they say, and would you agree with their answer?"

Opening Prayer for the Week

Father, this week we study something uncomfortable: our own pride. We confess that we do not always see it clearly, and that blindness frightens us more than the pride we do recognize. Open our eyes through Your Word. Show us where pride has quietly taken root in our hearts, in our relationships, and in how we approach You. Give us the courage to choose humility, not as a sign of weakness, but as the posture that brings us closest to Your heart. We have seen who You are. Now help us remove what stands between us and You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
