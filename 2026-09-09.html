Attitude

Stewardship and MissionLesson 11Day 5 of 7

In brief

Paul had seen beggars his whole life, hands out at every city gate. In Macedonia he watched the posture flip. Churches in deep poverty and a terrible ordeal came begging, urgently, for the privilege of giving money away, and their joy overflowed into generosity the math cannot explain. They gave gladly, freely, beyond ability, and themselves first of all. That is the attitude God prizes, not because He needs the gift, but because the giver is becoming like Him. God loves a cheerful giver because cheerful giving is what God is like.

Yesterday

Maybe last night you actually did it. Five quiet minutes, a proportion decided, a date on the calendar, the future vote removed. Yesterday made the case that giving should be settled in advance, the way God settled His own Gift before the world fell. But a plan can be kept two ways. There is the clenched way, teeth gritted, invoice paid, and there is the way a grandmother keeps a birthday, counting down to it. Today the letter looks past the schedule to the face. The Macedonians did not just give correctly. They gave like people who had won something, and that difference is the whole subject.

Historical Context

They Begged for the Privilege to Give

Every ancient city had its posture for desperation. At the gates, along the temple steps, beside the harbor road, the destitute held out their hands and pleaded, and everyone walking past knew exactly what the posture meant. Begging was the bottom. It was what a person did when every other option had died, and respectable people organized their lives to avoid ever being seen doing it. Pleading urgently for something was, by definition, the act of someone with nothing.

Now read what Paul says the Macedonian churches did. They earnestly pleaded with us. Poor churches, ordeal-battered churches, took up the beggar's urgent posture, and the thing they begged for was the chance to give their money away to strangers eight hundred miles from home. At the city gates, an outstretched hand asked. Here the posture ran in reverse, hands stretched out with coins already in them, insisting. Even the line he will quote about God loving a cheerful giver was not new coinage. Greek-speaking Jews already knew it from their translation of Proverbs, part of a long tradition that alms should be handed over with a glad face, never a grudging one. What was new in Macedonia was watching an entire province act like the proverb was true. The gift was real money. The begging was real begging. And the joy, which nobody could have staged, was the most disorienting part of all.

Into the Word

Two givers can hand over identical amounts on the same morning and be doing two entirely different things. One is settling an obligation. The other is celebrating something. Today's passage is a portrait of the second kind, painted from life, and Paul hangs it where rich Corinth has to walk past it. Five brushstrokes make up the Macedonian attitude, joy, generosity, freedom, privilege, and consecration, and the question under all of them is simple. What would it take for giving to feel like that?

Where does an attitude like that come from? Paul traces it to a joy God has always looked for in His people, and the trail runs through an older list of gifts.

One more brushstroke completes the portrait. This attitude is contagious, and it is not confined to money. Paul saw the same free-hearted willingness in a person, and generosity's reach goes past the coin purse entirely.

Street Level

I'd Rather Do It Myself

From the next room, the dishwasher is being unloaded by a fourteen-year-old, and you can hear the attitude from here. Cabinet doors land like cymbals. Each plate is handled like it weighs a hundred pounds. And over the top of it all comes the sigh, long, theatrical, pitched to carry, the universal teenage broadcast that a great injustice is underway. Here is the strange part. The job is getting done. Every plate will end up in the right cabinet, exactly as requested, and the parent listening in the next room still feels the thing parents have felt since the invention of chores. I would rather do it myself.

Stop and ask why, because the answer is the whole point. The help was real. The dishes were done. But the parent was never actually after a clean cabinet. She can load dishes faster alone, and both of them know it. What she wanted was a kid who wants to be part of the family's work, and the sigh announces the opposite, that this house has become a landlord and the chore is rent. That is what a grudging gift sounds like from God's side of the room, and it explains a verse we might otherwise misread. God loves a cheerful giver, and it was never because cheer is the admission fee. It is because He is not short on supply. Nothing we hand Him arrives at a house that was lacking it. He asks anyway, because giving is where children grow up to look like a Father who gives with delight. The gift gets a job done. The gladness is what tells Him He has a kid in the house, and not a tenant.

Today’s World

The Cheerful Giver Keeps Showing Up in the Data

Every spring since 2012, the World Happiness Report has ranked the world's countries by how their people rate their own lives. The 2025 edition, released on March 20, 2025 by Oxford's Wellbeing Research Centre with Gallup and the UN's Sustainable Development Solutions Network, aimed its spotlight somewhere new. Its theme was caring and sharing, a global audit of human kindness, and the audit came back stubbornly good. Across more than 140 countries, benevolent acts like helping a stranger, donating, and volunteering were still running about ten percent above their pre-pandemic levels. People also proved far kinder than anyone believed. When researchers deliberately lost wallets in public, the wallets came back at roughly twice the rate people predicted. And threaded through the chapters ran an older finding, that the person a gift most reliably lifts is the giver.

Then comes the detail that lands squarely on today's verse. The lift is not automatic. The research keeps finding that giving raises a giver's well-being most under three conditions, when the gift is freely chosen, when it connects you to the person helped, and when you can see that it made a difference. Giving that feels forced produces almost none of the effect. Read that slowly. Twenty centuries after Paul wrote that giving should flow without regret or compulsion, because God loves a cheerful giver, the World Happiness Report has arrived at the same doorstep. Gladness cannot be extracted, only grown, and it grows exactly where Paul planted it, in free, connected, purposeful giving. The data can now find the glow on a giver's face. It still cannot manufacture it. Paul points deeper, to grace as the source of the gladness he describes.

Christ at the center

Why would God care so much about the attitude inside a gift? Because He has one. Scripture does not describe a Giver who parts with things reluctantly. It describes a Father who gives good gifts with delight, and a Son who, for the joy set before Him, endured the cross (Hebrews 12:2). Read that slowly. Joy, set before Him, on the far side of the most costly gift ever given, and the joy was you, brought home. Jesus also kept the Macedonian order. He gave Himself first, into His Father's hands, and then to us, His body given, His blood poured out, no one taking His life but He laying it down freely. Nothing in the gospel was extracted from Him. That is why a grudging gift can never quite tell the truth about God, no matter how large it is, and why a glad one, however small, tells the family story straight. God loves a cheerful giver for the simplest reason in the universe. He has never been any other kind.

Application

Practice one act of glad-faced giving today. When the moment comes to give anything, your tip, your seat, your parking spot, your full attention, hand it over with warmth the other person can actually feel. No sigh, no glance at the phone, no "I guess." If a sour thought rises on the way, pray one sentence first. Lord, You gave me everything gladly. Then finish the gift with a face that means it, and notice what the gladness does to you, not just to them.

Discussion Question

"When you give (money, time, help at home, patience with a hard person), what does your face usually say (glad to do it, keeping score, counting the cost, hoping somebody noticed)? And what one thing would have to change inside you for your most routine act of giving this week to become something you actually enjoy?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we have kept plenty of commitments with clenched teeth, and today we admit that You were never fooled by any of them. You see the face behind every gift. So we are not asking You to make us give more today. We are asking You to make us givers who look like You. Pour into us the joy Macedonia had, joy that does not wait for the ordeal to end or the account to recover. Take the sigh out of our serving, the scorekeeping out of our helping, the reluctance out of our open hands. We give ourselves to You first, again, the whole self, because we know the smaller gifts follow wherever the self has already gone. And when we give this week, let something of Your own gladness leak through our faces, so that the people who receive from us catch a glimpse of the God who gives with delight. In Jesus' name, Amen.
