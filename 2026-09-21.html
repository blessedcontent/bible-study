The Love of God

Grace, Love, and FellowshipLesson 13Day 3 of 7

In brief

An old apostle writes three words everyone has seen on a poster, God is love, and then refuses to let them stay a slogan. He tells you what the love did. It sent. Today follows that one verb from the Father's decision, through a night conversation Jesus had with a nervous teacher, to a cross that arrived before anyone had cleaned up, and out to a list of everything that cannot pry that love off you. God's love is not a feeling He has about you. It is a Son He sent for you.

Yesterday

Yesterday we followed grace from the first line of Paul's letter to the last and found a trade inside it, the rich One made poor for our sake. Grace had a direction, from Him to us. Today asks where that direction came from. Paul's blessing names it next, the love of God (2 Corinthians 13:14).

Historical Context

The Ancients Had Stories About Dying for a Friend

A Corinthian who wanted to feel something about sacrifice went to the theater. Euripides had written a play about a queen named Alcestis who agreed to die in her husband's place when nobody else would, and audiences had been weeping over it for centuries. Romans told their children about the consul Decius Mus, who rode into the enemy line at the battle of Vesuvius and gave his life so that Rome would win. Stories like those were the kind the ancient world admired, and they tended to share a shape. Someone worthy died for someone worth it. A wife for a husband. A general for his city. A friend for a friend.

Paul does not deny that such devotion existed. In the letter he wrote from Corinth he grants that someone might dare to die for a good man (Romans 5:7). Then he writes a sentence that runs against the grain of those stories, that Christ died for us while we were still sinners, still enemies (Romans 5:10). The admired sacrifices were aimed at a spouse, a city, a friend, someone judged worth it. Paul aims Christ's death at the people who were against Him. Perhaps the most quoted sentence in the Bible was spoken at night to one nervous religious teacher (John 3:16), and what it describes is a love story with an unexpected cast. The ones who deserved nothing got the Son.

Into the Word

John writes the three words everyone knows, God is love (1 John 4:8), and then, before anyone can turn them into a mood, he tells you exactly what he means. Love is something God did. The word he leans on, over and over, is sent. So today we follow that verb. Where did the sending start, what did it cost, when did it happen, and what does it mean for people who keep expecting love to be taken back? The question underneath all of it is simple. What changes when you learn that God's love has a verb?

Where did John learn to talk like that? From a conversation Jesus had at night with a teacher who came with questions he could not ask in daylight.

When did He send Him? Not after we cleaned up. Paul gives the timing in one sentence, and it is the sentence the old stories could never have told.

If that is when it started, when does it stop? Paul answers with a list, and the list is one of the most reassuring things he ever wrote.

Street Level

Every Line Was in Her Handwriting

Picture the visitors' entrance of a state prison on a Saturday morning, and a woman in her sixties at the front desk, signing the visitor sheet. Then she sits in a plastic chair until an officer comes out and tells her what he told her last Saturday. He refused. Her son is inside on a sentence he earned, and he will not come out to the glass. Not for her. He has not come out in eleven months. She drives home. Next Saturday she signs the sheet again.

Nobody would call it dramatic. Her name is on every sheet in the drawer, week after week, with the column for his answer empty. She is not pretending he did nothing. She knows the charge and the length of the sentence better than he does. She is not waiting for him to change, either. She comes while he is still refusing, and the sheets prove it, one line at a time.

John's definition of love is that God loved us first and sent His Son. Paul's proof of it is a date, Christ dying for us while we were still sinners. Every line is in His handwriting. The Son was sent before anyone came out to the glass, and no refusal from inside can erase a Saturday already spent in that chair. The one the love is aimed at can go on saying no. The sheets in the drawer will not change. Or, some Saturday, he can come out and find that she never stopped signing.

Today’s World

A Recruiter for the Child Still Waiting

Wendy's Wonderful Kids assigns an adoption recruiter to children who have waited a long time for a family, often older children. The recruiter takes on each child's search as a particular search, meeting the child and looking for a family among the adults who already know that child. A former teacher. A coach. A relative nobody had called. The child did not have to ask for any of it.

A randomized study of 956 children, published in 2015, found that children with a recruiter were 1.7 times as likely to be adopted, with larger gains for older children. A recruiter cannot make a young person say yes, and not every child found a family. But the search began before any child asked. That is the order John gives for God's love. He loved us and sent, before anyone went looking for Him.

Source Journal of Public Child Welfare

Experimental Evaluation of a Child-Focused Adoption Recruitment Program for Children and Youth in Foster Care. Journal of Public Child Welfare, 2015. Read the source.

Christ at the center

Everything today hangs on one question. Who exactly did God send? If the Son were anything less than God Himself, then John's sentence falls apart, because the love of God would turn out to be God delegating. He would have loved us by making someone else pay. The Word who was with God in the beginning was God (John 1:1), and that is what makes the sending love rather than management. The Father's gift was not the sacrifice of a lesser being. He gave His one-of-a-kind Son, who is fully God and who gave Himself willingly. The One who came did not come to condemn (John 3:17), and He arrived while we were still against Him, which means the love of God is not an idea you have to climb toward. It walked into Galilee, then up a hill outside Jerusalem, and it has not gone anywhere since. Nothing in all creation can separate you from it, because it has a name, and the name is Jesus.

Application

Give love a verb today. Think of one person who has done nothing lately to earn your kindness, then choose one small thing you can do for them before the day ends that does not depend on how they respond. Make the meal, send the note, pray for them by name for five whole minutes. Do it first. Do it while they are still whoever they are. That is the order God used with you.

Discussion Question

When you say God loves you, are you picturing a feeling He has or a thing He did (think of the last time you doubted it, and what would have settled the doubt)? Which item on Paul's list of things that cannot separate you from His love do you secretly believe could?

Closing Prayer

Father, You did not wait for us. You sent Your Son while we were still turned away, and we thank You for a love that came first and came at Your own cost. Forgive us for treating it like a mood You might be in today and out of tomorrow. Settle it in us the way You settled it in Paul, so that nothing on his list, and nothing on ours, can talk us out of it. Then send us. Give us one person to love before they deserve it, and the nerve to do it without keeping score. In Jesus' name, Amen.
