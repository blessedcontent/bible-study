The Gift of Tongues

Spiritual GiftsLesson 6Day 5 of 7

In brief

Across the gulf from Corinth, pilgrims paid to hear a priestess whose answers needed official interpreters, because in that world the stranger the speech, the stronger the god. Corinth's church dragged that scale into worship and cranked its loudest gift. Paul honors the gift itself, real languages given by the Spirit, and then takes the scale away. Words nobody understands build nobody up. So speak to be understood, take turns, let someone interpret, keep the gathering in order. God has never tried to sound impressive. He wants to be understood, because understanding is how love builds.

Yesterday

Yesterday love got its fifteen verbs, and the chapter ended with the only measure that matters. Not how a gift sounds, but what it builds. Today that measure meets its first real test case, the gift Corinth had turned up the loudest. Tongues had become the congregation's showpiece, the proof of spirituality, the sound that filled the living room whether anyone understood it or not. Paul walks in carrying yesterday's chapter like a level against a crooked wall. Love is patient, love is kind, and love asks one question of every word spoken in worship. Did that build the person beside you?

Historical Context

The Priestess Nobody Could Understand

A day's journey from Corinth, on the slopes across the gulf, sat the most famous religious address in the Greek world. Delphi. Kings and farmers alike queued there to put their questions to the god Apollo, and the answers came through a woman seated on a tripod in the temple's inner room. What came out of her was not conversation. Ancient writers describe utterances that had to pass through the shrine's official interpreters before a pilgrim could carry them home, and the arrangement bothered nobody. Just the opposite. In that world, speech you could not follow was the whole point. The ecstatic cults that danced through Corinth's own streets ran on the same instinct, frenzied cries, clashing cymbals, language dissolving as the god took over. Losing intelligibility was the proof of contact.

Now picture Corinth's converts, raised on that scale, walking into Christian worship. When a brother stood and poured out praise in a language nobody present knew, every pagan-trained nerve in the room said the same thing. This must be the peak. The stranger the speech, the stronger the spirit. Paul spends a whole chapter dismantling that scale, because the God of Israel has always been the talking God, the One who speaks in order to be heard. Paul would rather say five understandable words than ten thousand nobody can follow (1 Corinthians 14:19). In Corinth's religion, mystery was the product. In Christ's church, meaning is.

Into the Word

So what about tongues? Of all the gifts on this week's lists, this is the one the Corinthians pushed to the front and the one that still stirs the most questions. Paul refuses both easy exits. He will not despise the gift, and he will not let it run the room. Today's passage watches him do something harder than either, honor a true gift while retraining an entire congregation on what worship is for. His argument starts with what the gift actually is, and it ends with two or three sentences of house rules that still hold.

Begin where the gift began. Jesus promised that new tongues would follow the gospel out into the world (Mark 16:17), and at Pentecost the promise landed with an audience. Visitors from a dozen nations heard Galilean fishermen declaring the magnificent acts of God, each in his own native language (Acts 2:1-13). The same sign fell at a Roman officer's house (Acts 10:44-48) and on a handful of disciples in Ephesus (Acts 19:6). In line with those scenes, the tongues of 1 Corinthians are best understood as the Spirit-granted ability to speak real languages the speaker never learned. The gift was a bridge to human ears, never a fog machine.

Now watch how much ink Paul gives it. Tongues appears in the gift list (1 Corinthians 12:8-10), in the ranked catalog (1 Corinthians 12:28) with its own rhetorical question (1 Corinthians 12:30), and twice in the love chapter (1 Corinthians 13:1, 1 Corinthians 13:8). Then chapter fourteen says the word tongue fifteen times, more than the rest of the letter combined, and even reaches for a rare term for foreign speech drawn from the prophets (1 Corinthians 14:21). Writers linger where the wound is. The gift was real, and its misuse was tipping public worship into disorder and confusion.

Street Level

Every Announcement Was True and You Were Still Lost

Land in an airport where you do not speak the language and you find out what it feels like to drown in accurate information. The ceiling speakers never rest. Gate changes, boarding calls, final warnings, all of it real, all of it urgent, none of it landing. Somewhere in that river of sound is your name, your flight, your one chance at the connection, and you cannot find it. A whole crowd stands up and moves and you do not know whether to follow. Then a gate agent looks at the boarding pass shaking in your hand, points down the corridor, and says four words in rough English. Other gate. B4. Run. You will remember her face for the rest of your life.

That is the scene Paul is worried about, except the airport is a church. The gift in Corinth was real speech carrying real praise, and Paul never doubts it. He just asks the lost traveler's question. Did it reach anyone? Words that never become meaning cannot build a person, no matter how true they are, and there is always somebody in the room holding a boarding pass. The visitor. The teenager. The friend who has never opened a Bible. Church vocabulary can turn into ceiling speakers, constant, correct, and landing on no one. Love does what the gate agent did. It sees one lost face, drops the fluency, and points. Ten thousand words filled that terminal. Four of them saved your trip.

Today’s World

The Words That Only Sound Like Sense

In 2025 researchers Cruz and Lombrozo published a study in Nature Human Behaviour on what technical language does to the people reading it, and they found something unsettling. Jargon makes a weak explanation feel more complete than it is, masking the gaps so readers walk away satisfied while actually understanding less. The impressive words do not hand over knowledge. They counterfeit the feeling of having received it.

Read that next to Paul and the overlap is uncanny. A room can be full of true, technically flawless speech and leave everyone in it a little more lost and a little more sure they are the problem. That is speaking into the air, and it happens in churches every week, not in ancient tongues but in insider vocabulary, in answers pitched over the visitor's head, in words chosen to sound weighty rather than to land. Paul's fix was never to say less that is true. It was to make the truth reach the person in the next chair. Being understood is not a lower calling than sounding profound. In Paul's math it is the higher one, because only the words that actually land can build.

Christ at the center

Translation is not a side project for God. It is His habit. He spoke through prophets in the language of shepherds and kings, and when the time fully came, He did the unthinkable. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:14), God translating Himself into a life we could read, a face we could watch, hands we could touch. Jesus taught in the dialect of farmers and fishermen. His parables ran on seed, bread, sheep, and lamps, the vocabulary of everybody. When the Spirit came at Pentecost, the first miracle of the church age was a translation miracle, every visitor hearing the mighty works of God in the language their mother sang over their cradle. And the cross speaks the one language that needs no interpreter anywhere on earth, love that gives everything. Corinth wanted a God impressive enough to be incomprehensible. In Jesus, God chose the opposite glory. He has never once spoken over our heads on purpose.

Application

Today, translate one truth. Take something Jesus means to you and say it in one sentence a twelve-year-old could repeat, no church words allowed. No blessed, no saved, no fellowship. Write the sentence down, then test it out loud on one actual person, a kid, a coworker, the friend who never darkens a church door. If their eyes don't glaze, you built something. If they do, revise and try again tomorrow. Speaking to be understood is not dumbing it down. It is love doing carpentry.

Discussion Question

"Where does your faith-talk most often turn into speaking into the air, true words that land on nobody (insider vocabulary around guests, answers to questions nobody asked, volume where clarity was needed)? And who in your life needs one truth about God translated into their language this week, whether that means Spanish, teenager, or plain English?"

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for being the God who speaks to be heard. You could have stayed incomprehensible, and instead You sent prophets, then Your Son, plain enough for fishermen and children and us. Forgive us for the times our worship and our witness have been sound aimed at nobody, words that impressed the room and built no one in it. Teach us the difference between reverence and fog. Where our congregations gather, give us order that welcomes the stranger, turns taken in love, and every voice raising the same house. Give the members among us who carry second languages a holy restlessness for the people who need You in exactly those words. And keep us from ever measuring Your Spirit by volume. We would rather say five words that land than ten thousand that echo. In Jesus' name, Amen.
