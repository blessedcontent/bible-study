Distractions and Temptations

Sin, the Gospel, and the LawLesson 9Day 2 of 7

In brief

Samson kept his hair, kept his vow, kept his strength for most of two decades, until the morning he walked into a Philistine town five miles from home and could not stop looking at a girl.

  • ☩The pull that took Samson down was not a single big sin. It was a slow drift of small ones, a series of unguarded glances, an appetite he never bothered to govern.
  • ☩Satan does not need a moral catastrophe to keep us from God. He just needs an inbox, a feed, a calendar full of things that are not exactly wrong but will not leave room for anything else.
  • ☩Jesus knew the same exhaustion we know, full days and hungry crowds and tired legs at a well in Samaria, and built one move into His operating system. Before sunrise. To a deserted place. Alone.
  • ☩The Word of God is not extra credit on a busy day. It is the rope that pulls a Samson out of the riddle before the riddle takes his eyes.

Yesterday

Yesterday we hung two vocabularies on the wall: the Hebrew cabinet of words for sin (chattat, missing the mark; pesha, covenant breach; avon, bent and twisted) and the Hebrew cabinet of words for the law (piqqudim, mitzvot, huqqim, torah). The Memory Text from Psalm 119 held both halves in one breath: "Your precepts have given me life. Save me." Today we meet a man who was given more precepts than most. A Nazirite from his mother's womb, set apart before he could remember being set apart, with a daily reminder of his vow hanging on the side of his own head. And the very first scene of his adult story is Samson seeing a Philistine woman in Timnah and not looking away.

Historical Context

Timnah, Five Miles Down the Hill

The opening verse of Judges 14 says Samson "went down to Timnah," and the verb is doing more than describing geography. Timnah sat on the southern edge of the Sorek Valley, only five or six miles west of Samson's hometown of Zorah, but it was downhill in every sense. Samson's family lived in the hill country of Dan, the part of Israel's allotment that had never been fully cleared of Philistines. Timnah was a Philistine settlement next door, close enough to see from the family roof and just culturally far enough away to feel exotic. The Philistines were the Iron Age sea-people who had locked Israel's economy in a vise for a generation. The previous chapter has just told us that the Spirit of the LORD began to stir Samson while he was still a teenager and that he had been set apart from the womb as a Nazirite, the only person in the Bible whose lifelong vow was declared before his birth.

To "go down to Timnah" was to walk five miles in the wrong direction. Not a foreign mission trip, not a strategic dating decision, but a young man walking out of the world his calling had been pressed into and toward the very nation he was supposed to be delivering Israel from. The narrator slips in a small parenthetical at verse four that the LORD was using even this for His purposes, the Bible's standard reminder that God is not surprised by our distractions. But that note does not soften the diagnosis of what Samson is doing. He is being pulled downhill, on his own legs, by his own eyes, for his own reasons. Judges chapter sixteen will eventually show us where the downhill walk ends, in a stone-floored Philistine temple with the supports of the building leaning against his back. The first move toward that ending was the casual stroll to a town five miles off the map of his calling.

Into the Word

Open Judges 14 and the first verb in the chapter is "went down," and what Samson is going down to is a five-mile change in the trajectory of his entire life. He sees a Philistine woman in Timnah and demands her as a wife. He overrules his parents. He walks through a vineyard a Nazirite was forbidden to touch. He scrapes honey out of a lion's carcass a Nazirite was forbidden to touch. He hosts a wedding mishteh, in Hebrew literally a "drinking-place" a Nazirite was forbidden to host. None of those steps looks like a catastrophe at the time. Each looks like a reasonable accommodation to a normal life. Two chapters later the slow drift becomes the moment everything snaps. Samson is on Delilah's lap and his mouth is moving.

Street Level

The Bermuda Grass at the Fence Line

Most people who have ever owned a backyard with a rose bed against the fence have lost the same fight. The neighbor's bermuda grass is fine. It is a normal lawn grass that millions of people pay good money for. It is not poison, it is not on any official invasive list, and on any given morning, looking out the kitchen window at the property line, nothing is happening that you would think to act on. There is no day when the grass attacks the roses. There is no morning when you wake up to find a battle has been fought. There is only the underground runner, the rhizome, that puts out one new node every two weeks all season long for ten or fifteen years. By the time you can see what is happening above ground, the rose bed has been quietly choked from below. The roses did not lose a war. They lost their lunch. Every week. Almost nothing. For a decade.

That is the shape Samson's fall took. There was never a morning he woke with a clear decision to break his Nazirite vow. There was only the trip to Timnah, and then the lion, and then the wedding feast, and then the prostitute in Gaza, and then the Valley of Sorek, and then the lap. The runners had been moving under the fence for years before the scissors ever came out. We have the same gardening problem in our spiritual yard. The relationships, the feeds, the appetites, the categories of consumption we have decided are individually fine. The hair is still on our head. The vow is still in our file. But the soil under the roses is being colonized one rhizome at a time, and one morning we will pull back the mulch and there will be no roots left.

Today’s World

Ninety-Six Times a Day

In April 2025, Perspectives in Public Health, the peer-reviewed journal of the United Kingdom's Royal Society for Public Health, published a paper by Sharpe and Spooner (Institute of Psychology, Business, and Human Sciences, University of Chichester) that gave the modern phenomenon its first formal name. "Dopamine-scrolling," they argued, was no longer a side effect of other problems like distraction or sleep loss. It was its own diagnosable pattern. A habitual, low-grade pursuit of novel content, with no destination, no goal, no obvious harm in any single moment, sustained for hundreds of brief sessions every day. The authors asked public health authorities to begin treating it as a phenomenon in its own right, the way an earlier generation of researchers had to argue that tobacco was its own object of study and not a personality quirk of the people who used it.

The numbers around the paper land in Samson's lap. StatsPanda, citing DataReportal's Digital 2025 Global Overview and BankMyCell, reports that the average smartphone user now picks the device up about ninety-six times a day, once every ten waking minutes, and spends four hours and thirty-seven minutes on it across all those sessions. No single one of those checks is a moral catastrophe. The unlock for the weather is fine. The unlock to glance at a text is fine. The unlock to see if the package shipped is fine. The drift is the architecture, not any individual lookup. The brain's reward circuitry treats each refresh as a small uncertain reward, and at four-and-a-half hours a day the small rewards add up across a year to more than sixteen hundred hours of screen time, roughly a hundred waking days, spent on something the user did not consciously choose to spend any of it on.

That is the modern shape of "he went down to Timnah." It is not a single decision to walk into a Philistine vineyard. It is ninety-six unlocks a day, every day, for years, none of them quite crossing a line, all of them quietly pulling the eye downhill. The Hebrew verb at the head of Samson's fall was ra'ah, "he saw." A British public health journal has just put a more modern name on the place that verb tends to take a life if it is not governed.

Christ at the center

The bridge from Samson to Jesus runs through the garden across the brook we walked into last week. Both walked into a fight with the powers of darkness, and both faced a moment where the question on the table was whether their own will would bend to the Father's. Samson is the strong man who depended on his strength, and his strength left him in a Philistine lap. Jesus is the strong man who emptied Himself before the contest, walked into Gethsemane, and prayed the sentence Samson never prayed: "Not as I will, but as You will." Where Samson's eyes pulled him into a Philistine field, Jesus's eyes were lifted to His Father all night in lonely places. Where Samson lost his vision and was paraded between two pillars of a pagan temple, Jesus willingly stretched out between two pieces of wood and broke the back of the powers Samson was supposed to be delivering Israel from. The strong Man came on Samson's behalf and on ours, and the way out of distraction begins where He first walked it.

Application

For one day this week, set a calendar alert for thirty minutes before you normally start your day. Use that thirty minutes for one thing only: open the Bible and sit with it. No podcast, no email check, no scrolling "just for a minute," no caffeine routine that turns into thirty minutes of news. Read the next chapter of wherever you are, or read Psalm 119 if you have nowhere. The thirty minutes is the test. If it is taken from you tomorrow morning, you will know whose hand took it.

Discussion Question

"Look at the last week of your phone screen-time report, or the last twenty minutes of your scrolling, and ask honestly: what is the Timnah in your life right now? (A category of content. An app. A relationship that pulls you down a hill you know you should not be walking. A consumption habit you have stopped resisting.) What would it look like this week to build one small fence around that Timnah in a way Jesus would recognize, not as a willpower diet but as a thirty-minute alone-with-the-Father move that comes earlier in your day than the pull?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we come to You as people who have been pulled in five directions before the coffee was even cool, and who have hardly noticed that we were being pulled. We have a hundred small Timnahs that we go down to without thinking. An inbox we open before You. A feed we scroll before the Bible. A calendar packed so full there is no breath left for the conversation we said we wanted with You. Forgive us. Show us today the slow drift Samson never noticed until his eyes were already gone. Teach us the discipline Your Son built into every gospel: deserted place, before sunrise, alone with You. We do not need a more impressive plan, Father. We need the seeking-first move that orders everything else. Pull our eyes off the next shiny thing and back onto the One whose face is the only sight that will outlast every other. In Jesus' name, Amen.
