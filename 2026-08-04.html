A More Excellent Way

Spiritual GiftsLesson 6Day 4 of 7

In brief

Corinth's proudest export was a bronze that rang like nothing else on earth, and its church was getting famous for the same thing. Impressive sound. Right in the middle of his teaching on gifts, Paul stops the catalog and writes a portrait instead. Tongues without love are that bronze, ringing empty. Prophecy without love is nothing. Mountain-moving faith without love is nothing. Then come fifteen verbs, patient, kind, keeping no record, bearing all things, that show what love actually does all day. Love is not one gift among many. It is the way every gift becomes worth having.

Yesterday

Yesterday ended mid-thought on purpose. Desire the greater gifts, Paul said, and I will show you an even better way, and then the chapter break left us standing in the doorway. Today he opens the door. The Corinthians surely expected the ranking conversation to resume, a final verdict on which gift outranks the rest. Instead Paul sets every gift down and describes the current they are all meant to run on. Yesterday's body had many parts and one life. Today we find out what that life feels like from the inside, and it turns out to have fifteen verbs.

Historical Context

The Most Expensive Noise in the World

Collectors in Rome paid fortunes for it. Corinthian bronze, an alloy the ancients swore glowed with traces of gold and silver, was the single most coveted luxury metal in the empire, so famous that writers used the phrase as shorthand for extravagance itself. Legend said the alloy was born the day Corinth burned, precious metals melting together in the ruins. Whatever the truth, the city's foundries had turned sound and shine into its signature product. And bronze had a second career in the ancient world. Theaters seated bronze resonance jars among the audience, tuned vessels that caught an actor's voice and rang it louder, and the ecstatic cults that filled Corinth's streets drove their processions with clashing bronze cymbals. Bronze was the sound of performance, of religion at maximum volume, of wealth announcing itself.

So when Paul writes that a person with every tongue and no love is a resounding gong or a clanging cymbal, Corinth does not hear a random image. It hears its own export. A bronze jar amplifies whatever strikes it and produces nothing of its own. It is brilliant, costly, loud, and hollow, and it cannot love you. Paul's warning lands with the precision of a local joke told at the city's expense. A church can become the very thing its city was famous for. Impressive noise, beautifully made, ringing on every corner, and empty in the middle.

Into the Word

Picture the reader in Corinth reaching the end of the gift lists, pen ready to tally which members hold the greater gifts. Then the page turns and the scoring sheet dissolves. Paul suspends the catalog and writes the most famous chapter he ever produced (1 Corinthians 13:1-7), and Peter, writing to scattered churches years later, will press the same claim from another direction (1 Peter 4:8-11). Between them the question changes shape. Not which gift is greatest, but what makes any gift worth anything at all.

Paul begins by running Corinth's favorite gifts at full volume with the love removed. Tongues first, the congregation's showpiece, a real gift the Spirit really gives (1 Corinthians 12:10), listed among God's own appointments (1 Corinthians 12:28) and never expected of everyone (1 Corinthians 12:30). Crank it to angelic registers with love subtracted and Paul hears only the city's bronze, a resounding gong, a clanging cymbal (1 Corinthians 13:1). Expensive sound. Nothing inside.

Street Level

Nobody Was Ever Loved by Thread Count

A nice hotel does everything right. The sheets are four hundred thread count, the towels are warm, the front desk smiles and uses your name, and a mint waits on the pillow like a tiny reward for existing. Every detail is executed by trained professionals, and none of it is for you. It is for anyone. You could be swapped tonight for any other traveler with a credit card and the room would never notice. Now think of the other bed you know. The guest room at your grandmother's house, with a mattress shaped like a canoe and towels older than you are. On the nightstand sits a fan, already plugged in and aimed at the pillow, because she remembers you sleep hot. In the pantry is the cereal you loved when you were nine. She bought it Tuesday.

Every measurable category goes to the hotel. Skill, budget, equipment, polish. And you sleep better in the canoe, because the fan and the cereal are not amenities. They are evidence. Somebody thought about you before you arrived. That is the difference Paul is naming when he says a person with every gift and no love adds up to nothing. Talent can turn down a bed. Only love notices you sleep hot. A church full of impressive abilities can still feel exactly like a hotel, excellent, efficient, and for anyone. The fifteen verbs are how it becomes a grandmother's house instead. Love is not the mint on the pillow. It is the fan, aimed ahead of time, at you.

Today’s World

The Medicine Was Being Held

For generations the smallest, most fragile newborns were treated the way you would expect. Lifted away from the mother, placed alone in a warm high-tech incubator, watched by machines. It seemed obvious. Then researchers tried the opposite. In a trial across Ghana, India, Malawi, Nigeria, and Tanzania, more than 3,200 babies weighing under four pounds were given immediate kangaroo mother care, laid skin to skin on a parent's bare chest within the first hours after birth and kept there. Deaths dropped by 25 percent, deaths from infection by more than a third. The World Health Organization now recommends immediate kangaroo mother care for preterm or low-birth-weight babies.

Notice what the treatment was. Not a new drug, not a better machine. A parent, holding a child. The most advanced incubator in the world can hold a steady temperature, but it cannot do the one thing a mother's chest does, which is love a specific baby on purpose, warmth and heartbeat and breath aimed at this one. That is the difference Paul refuses to let the Corinthians miss. Every gift in the room can be running at full power, the equipment flawless, the competence real, and it still adds up to nothing if love is not the thing being practiced. The fifteen verbs are not decoration laid over the skill. They are the medicine. Sometimes the more excellent way is simply to hold on.

Christ at the center

Try an old exercise with fresh eyes. Read the fifteen verbs and put a name where love stands. Jesus is patient, and Peter can testify. Jesus is kind, and the lepers He touched can testify. Jesus does not envy, though He owned nothing. Jesus is not rude, though He was interrupted, crowded, and cornered for three years. Jesus keeps no record of wrongs, and from the cross He said Father, forgive them, with the nails still in. The paragraph reads like a biography because it is one, the character of Christ written out in verbs. Now put your own name in the blanks and feel where the sentences break. That gap is why the cross exists. Every verb on the list, He conjugated to the very end, bearing all things up a hill, believing all things in a garden, enduring all things until it was finished. Paul promised a way more excellent than any gift. The way has a name and scarred hands. We do not just admire this love. We are loved by it.

Application

Read the fifteen verbs slowly today and let one of them sting. That is your verb. Long fuse, kindness, no scorekeeping, not seeking your own, whichever one names the place your love goes thin. Then aim it at one person before the day ends. Answer the slow text without the edge. Do the kind thing for the family member who irritates you fastest. Tear up one grudge you have been keeping in the drawer. One verb, one person, on purpose. That is how a feeling becomes a way.

Discussion Question

"Which of love's fifteen verbs breaks down first in your closest relationships (the long fuse in traffic with your family, keeping no record in your marriage, not seeking your own with friends)? And what would practicing that single verb on one specific person look like this week?"

Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, we have wanted the gifts more than the love, and we confess it. We have practiced our abilities and left our patience unrehearsed. We have kept records You died to erase. Today Your word read us, verb by verb, and we felt where our sentences break. Thank You for being the portrait we could not paint, patient with us, kind to us, keeping no record against us. Now write those verbs into our reflexes. Give us the long fuse at home, where it is hardest. Give us kindness that costs something before sundown. Where our gifts have been making noise, wire them to love until they start making disciples. We do not ask to feel more loving. We ask to love, the way You love us, until the feeling catches up. In Jesus' name, Amen.
