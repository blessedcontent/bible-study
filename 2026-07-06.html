Foolishness to Those Who Are Perishing

The Message of the CrossLesson 2Day 3 of 7

In brief

To the smartest people in Corinth, Paul's message was a joke. A Savior who got Himself crucified by Rome? Educated people did not take that seriously, and devout Jews found it close to blasphemy. So Paul grabs the exact word they were using against him, foolishness, and builds his whole argument on it. God chose the one story guaranteed to offend human pride, he says, precisely so that no one could be argued or impressed into the kingdom, only humbled into it. What the world laughs off as foolish is God's wisdom doing the one thing human cleverness never could.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul made his decision. He would preach the cross plainly and refuse to turn it into a performance. Today we hear how that plain message actually sounded to the people in the seats. Not noble. Not profound. Foolish. To the educated crowd in Corinth, a Savior who got Himself executed by the Romans was not a religion worth considering, it was a punchline. Paul does not flinch from that reaction. He picks up the very word they were using against him, foolishness, and turns it into the center of his argument. What looks like foolishness, he is about to say, is exactly how God planned to save the world.

Historical Context

The Messiah Nobody Ordered

To understand why people laughed, you have to know who they were waiting for. For generations the Jewish people had pinned their hope on a coming Messiah, and the picture was not subtle. He would be a king in the line of David, a military deliverer who would drive the Romans into the sea, end the occupation, and put Israel back on top. Mothers told their children about him. Rebels died trying to be him. The one thing absolutely no one was expecting was a Messiah who lost, who was arrested, flogged, and nailed to a Roman cross like the very rebels Rome wanted to crush.

It was worse than disappointing. Their own Scriptures said that anyone hung on a tree was under God's curse, so a crucified Messiah sounded less like good news and more like blasphemy. And to the Greeks across town it was simply stupid, the idea that an executed provincial nobody could be the hinge of human history. Both crowds heard the same message and reached for the same word. Foolishness. Paul is not surprised by this, and he is not ashamed of it. He is about to argue that the offense is the whole point, that God deliberately chose the one story guaranteed to insult human pride.

Into the Word

Paul does something bold across this whole stretch of the letter (1 Corinthians 1:18-31). Instead of dodging the word his critics threw at him, he grabs it and runs. He stands in a city full of professional thinkers, asks where all their wisdom actually got them (1 Corinthians 1:20), and keeps preaching a crucified Christ that lands on sophisticated ears as pure foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:23). Watch him use that word "foolish" over and over through the next few verses, daring you to feel the full scandal of the cross before he turns it inside out. The question driving the section is simple. If the message of the cross really is foolishness, then whose foolishness is it, and what does that say about the people doing the laughing?

Street Level

The Shot Too Embarrassing to Make

There is a way to shoot a free throw that almost no professional will use, even though it plainly works better. You shoot it underhand, scooping the ball up with both hands from down between your knees. It looks ridiculous. People call it the "granny shot." And yet one of the greatest free-throw shooters who ever lived shot that way on purpose, because the physics are simply kinder, a softer arc and a gentler landing on the rim. He begged other players to switch. Almost none would. They would rather keep missing in a way that looks cool than start making them in a way that looks foolish.

Read that last sentence again, because it is the whole human race standing at the free-throw line. God offered us a way home that works, every time, no exceptions. It just happens to look ridiculous, a beaten man dying on a Roman cross for crimes He never committed. The wise and the strong looked at it and said, not like that, I have my pride. And they walked away having missed the one shot that never rims out. The cross will always look like the granny shot to a watching world. It also happens to be the only thing that actually goes in.

Today’s World

The Idea They Demoted Her For

For most of her career, Katalin Karikó was the scientist the system wanted to forget. She was convinced that a fragile molecule called messenger RNA could be turned into medicine, and almost no one believed her. Grant after grant came back rejected. In 1995 the University of Pennsylvania gave her a choice, abandon the research or accept a demotion and a pay cut, and she took the demotion to keep going. In 2005 the journals Nature and Science turned down the paper that would later change the world, telling her it was not novel enough to interest their readers. For decades she was the woman with the foolish idea, working in borrowed lab space, getting nowhere the world could see.

Then the foolish idea became the mRNA vaccines that reached billions of arms, and in 2023 Katalin Karikó walked up to accept the Nobel Prize in Medicine. Sit with that arc, because it is the shape of the whole chapter. The experts were not stupid. They were certain, and their certainty had no slot for the thing that turned out to matter most. Paul watched the same blindness in Corinth. The brightest minds in the city looked at a crucified Savior and filed Him under nonsense, too foolish to fund, too plain to take seriously. The cross will always read as the demoted idea to a confident world. It also happens to be the one that saves everybody who finally stops laughing.

Christ at the center

Stand at the foot of the cross and look at it the way Corinth did. A failed teacher. A dead revolutionary. The very picture of weakness and defeat. Now hear what God says about that same scene. This is My power. This is My wisdom. This is how I outsmart every proud mind and rescue everyone who finally stops trusting their own. The cross is God's great reversal, the place where He took the worst thing human beings could do to His Son and made it the best thing that ever happened to us. Jesus let the world's wisdom do its absolute worst, let it mock Him and kill Him, and then He walked out of the tomb and proved that what looked like foolishness was the deepest wisdom in the universe. The people laughing were the ones who could not see. The people being saved were the ones who finally could.

Application

Think of one part of your faith that you feel a little embarrassed about, the piece that would get a raised eyebrow at work or among certain friends. Maybe it is that you actually believe Jesus rose from the dead, or that you pray about real decisions, or that you trust a 2,000-year-old execution to somehow save you. Today, instead of hiding that part, own it. Say it plainly to yourself, and if the moment comes, to someone else, without apologizing for how it sounds. The thing the world calls foolish is the very thing holding you up.

Discussion Question

"Where do you feel the pull to make your faith more respectable than the cross actually is, and what would it look like this week to stop apologizing for the part of the gospel that sounds foolish to the people around you?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we admit we care more than we would like about looking foolish. We trim our faith to fit the room, go quiet when the cross might embarrass us, and quietly wish the gospel were a little more respectable. Forgive us. Thank You that You were never embarrassed by the cross, that You chose the very thing the world mocks to save the people the world overlooks, people like us. Give us the nerve to stand with a message that gets laughed at, because we have tasted its power and we know it is real. Make us humble enough to be counted among the fools who found You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
