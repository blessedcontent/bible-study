A Place

How to Study the BibleLesson 5Day 3 of 7

In brief

Daniel, an old man surrounded by enemies, still climbed to the same upper room three times a day and opened the same window toward a temple that no longer stood.

  • ☩ A place tells your soul that this matters enough to have an address.
  • ☩ Jesus left the house before sunrise for a specific kind of spot, and Mark chose the word "desolate" instead of the more polite word "outside."
  • ☩ Daniel's open window and Mary's seat at the feet of Jesus are two of the shortest sermons on space in Scripture, and they make the same point.
  • ☩ A corner becomes an altar the same way a path becomes a road: by being walked until the ground remembers.

Yesterday

Yesterday the alarm went off fifteen minutes earlier than before, and the assignment was a single paragraph read out loud, slowly, with the phone face-down. That was the raw material of a Bible-studying life. Today the question shifts from when to where. Not vaguely, but actually: what floor did your bare feet touch, what chair accepted you, what light fell across the page? Time and place are a pair, and abiding (yesterday's Greek word meno, to stay, to dwell) always happens somewhere with an address. Today the week walks into the room.

Historical Context

The Window Toward Jerusalem

For centuries, faithful Jews had a rule so ordinary it barely needed teaching: when you pray, turn your body toward the temple. Daniel was an old man in Babylon, serving at the highest level of Persian government, when he made a habit that almost got him killed. Three times a day he climbed to an upper room in his house, opened a particular window that faced west toward Jerusalem, and prayed. The room was not consecrated. The window was not carved with anything holy. It was just a window, and on the far side of it a temple lay in ruins six hundred miles away. That specificity is what his enemies used to trap him. If his prayer had been a vague inner intention, they could not have caught him; but because it had a location and a posture, he was predictable in the best possible sense. His place was not secret. It was just his.

The same instinct keeps showing up. Peter climbs to the flat rooftop of a tanner's house in Joppa and sees the sheet descending. Paul heads for a riverside outside the gates of Philippi where the Jewish women gathered for prayer. Jesus, Mark tells us in a single sentence, went out to an eremos topos, a deserted place, far enough from the house to be alone and close enough to return before breakfast. Ancient devotion was never floating in the abstract. It had an address. The ground where a faithful person knelt was, over time, indistinguishable from an altar.

Into the Word

Before the fishermen were awake, before the village dogs had stirred, Jesus was already outside. Mark gives us one sentence and three details: very early, while it was still dark, a deserted place. He does not say He slipped into a side room or prayed on the way. He left the building, walked past the town noise, and located the only kind of room whose door was guaranteed not to open until He was finished. Mary learned the same discipline from the other direction, refusing to leave the spot at His feet even when the kitchen was calling. If the Son of God needed a specific place, and His closest student picked hers and would not give it up, no servant of His is going to get by without one.

Street Level

The Chair That Remembers

In every home there is a chair that nobody planned to turn into anything. It was bought for the corner by the window because the corner needed a chair. The upholstery is wrong for the rest of the room. The light is slightly too low for reading without squinting. For the first few years it was just a place to sit. Then one winter, somebody started reading there in the mornings, and the next winter, somebody read there again, and by the third year, when that person walked into the room before dawn, their feet went to the chair without asking. The chair had started remembering before the person had.

A place does not have to be holy to become an altar. It only has to accumulate. The Greek word for the spot Jesus found in the hour before sunrise is eremos topos, a deserted place, and Mark does not say it was scenic or sacred. It was just the same kind of room every morning. Daniel opened the same window three times a day in his upper room in Babylon, and the window was not the point; the opening of it was. A place becomes a teacher when the body learns it, and the body learns a place only by showing up to it over and over. The chair does not make you faithful. It just makes faithfulness easier than the alternative, which is deciding to be faithful from scratch every day.

Today’s World

The Great Good Place

Almost two decades before the first smartphone, a sociologist named Ray Oldenburg wrote a book arguing that the health of a culture could be measured by how many "great good places" it still had left. He did not mean the living room, and he did not mean the office. He meant the third place: the front porch, the barbershop, the corner diner, the church basement, the library reading room. These were spaces where the same people showed up in the same chairs, and where a person could walk in alone and still be known. Oldenburg warned in 1989 that America was losing them fast. He was right, and the decline has accelerated. A 2025 study in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine reported that nearly 40 percent of U.S. adults now live with moderate to severe loneliness. The Institute of Museum and Library Services shows physical library visits collapsing from roughly 1.25 billion a year in 2019 to about 671 million by 2022. The front stoop still exists. Almost nobody is on it.

Urban planners and behavioral researchers have stopped treating this as a soft finding. A line of 2025 studies on environmental cues and habit formation has confirmed what Oldenburg intuited with a notebook in the 1980s: a place is not a backdrop for behavior, it is a trigger for it. A specific chair, a particular corner, a recurring slant of window light can eventually shift behavioral control from willpower to the environment itself. A 2025 review of habit research followed executives through major life transitions and found that those who deliberately rebuilt the same cues in a new city kept the habits their peers lost in the move. The space is the scaffolding. Remove it, and the practice collapses, no matter how sincere the intention behind it was.

Daniel's upper room was a great good place before the sociologist named it. Jesus' olive grove was a great good place before Oldenburg's first draft. The Hebrew the psalmist uses in Psalm 27:8 is paneh, the full face of God turned toward a named spot the worshiper returns to on purpose. The research is catching up a few thousand years late to say the same thing: a single, repeatable location is the one human technology that can make devotion outlast a mood. If the cafes, the porches, and the reading rooms have thinned out around you, the one inside your own house matters more, not less. Somebody still has to show up to it on purpose.

Christ at the center

Jesus did not only go to a place; He went to the same place often enough that His enemies knew its address. Luke 22:39 says He withdrew to the Mount of Olives "as was his custom." John 18:2 tells us why that detail matters: Judas knew the garden because Jesus had met His Father there many times. The betrayer did not need to track Him. He only had to walk to the olive press Jesus had chosen as His spot. A place is that vulnerable. It exposes you; it becomes predictable; it can be used against you. The Son of God accepted that exposure as the price of being reachable by His Father. The kiss in the garden landed in the same corner of the hillside where hours of midnight prayer had been offered. The cross itself was reached through a place of consistent retreat, not in spite of it. Whatever spot we finally choose this week will cost us a kind of visibility too. A schedule others can interrupt. A chair family members will know means something. A vulnerability to being found. That is not the flaw of the practice. That is the practice.

Application

Pick your spot today. Not tomorrow, today, before you close this page. The chair by the window, the kitchen table corner before anyone is up, the back porch, the step on the landing. Physically walk over to it, sit down in it for thirty seconds, and leave the Bible you picked on Sabbath resting on that exact surface. Tomorrow morning your feet will already know where to go.

Discussion Question

"Where in your home (or your routine) have you actually met God most reliably in the past year (if nowhere, name the first spot you would try), and what is the one visible thing that would have to be in that spot by tomorrow morning for it to start being that kind of place from here on?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we have prayed in cars and in hallways and halfway through a loaded dishwasher, and You have heard every word. But we feel the thin quality of those prayers, and we want more than scraps. Give us a place. A chair, a corner, a window that faces something other than a screen. Teach us, like Daniel, to let that place become predictable enough to get us in trouble with the part of us that would rather stay mobile. Teach us, like Mary, to refuse the kitchen when Your feet are in the living room. And when the noise of the house begins again, let the spot we leave behind keep working in us, the way Jesus' eremos kept working through the long day that followed. Make the corner we choose today an altar by Friday, not by anything we add to it, but by Your presence quietly gathering where we keep showing up. In Jesus' name, Amen.
