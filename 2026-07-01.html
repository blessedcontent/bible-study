Many in This City

Paul's Ministry in CorinthLesson 1Day 5 of 7

In brief

Paul keeps preaching, and the synagogue turns ugly. They resist, they curse, and he finally shakes out his clothes and walks next door. Then comes the shock. The synagogue's own leader believes, and so do many others. But Paul is rattled, worn down by the fight and bracing for worse. So one night Jesus stands beside him in a vision with a command and a promise. Stop being afraid. Keep talking. I have many people in this city. The people Paul could not see were already God's. When the work feels like it is failing, God may be counting a harvest you have not met yet.

Yesterday

Yesterday we walked Corinth's idol-soaked streets and felt the sheer weight of the place, a city where immorality and false gods were simply the air everyone breathed. The obvious question hangs in the air: could the gospel actually take root in soil like that? Today we get the answer, and it comes in two parts. First, real opposition, the kind that wears a person down and makes them want to quit. But then, woven right through it, real fruit, and a word from God that reframes the whole struggle. Paul is about to learn that the resistance he can see is not the whole story of what God is doing in Corinth.

Historical Context

When the Synagogue Ruler Crossed the Line

Every synagogue had a ruler, the man responsible for the building, the services, and the honor of the community. He was a pillar, a person of standing, and in a tight-knit minority like the Jews of Corinth, his loyalty held the group together. So when Luke reports that Crispus, the synagogue ruler himself, came to believe in Jesus, this was not a quiet personal decision. It was a public earthquake. The most respected man in the room had crossed over to the message that was tearing the community apart, and he brought his whole household with him.

Make it sharper still. After being driven out of the synagogue, Paul did not leave the neighborhood. He simply moved his meetings to the house of a God-fearing Gentile named Titius Justus, whose home stood right next door. Picture that. The new church now met one wall away from the synagogue that had just rejected its message, with the synagogue's former leader sitting in the congregation. It was bold, almost provocative, a daily visible reminder that the gospel had not retreated. That was the charged, high-tension setting in which Paul was quietly running out of steam, and in which God chose to speak to him in the night.

Into the Word

The story moves fast here, from rejection to breakthrough to a private moment of fear. Paul preaches in the synagogue until the hostility boils over, then turns to the Gentiles, and against all odds the church grows, even pulling in the synagogue's own leader (Acts 18:4-8). But success and opposition together can be their own kind of exhausting, and that is exactly when God shows up. Watch the human cost of ministry and the divine encouragement that meets it.

Street Level

The Voice in the Empty Studio

There is something lonely about a late-night radio host. Picture one alone in a small booth at two in the morning, headphones on, talking into a microphone in an empty room. No audience to see, no applause, no laughter, just a red light and the sound of their own voice and the very real feeling that they might be talking to nobody at all. Some nights it must seem pointless, like shouting down a well. Then a letter arrives weeks later from a long-haul trucker who says that voice was the only thing that kept him awake and alive on a thousand miles of dark highway, and he had been out there listening the whole time.

Paul was preaching into what felt like an empty studio. The synagogue had turned on him, the opposition was loud, and the feeling in his body at the end of a long day was that he was failing. So Jesus stood beside him in the night with the one fact that changes how you count everything, that He already had many people in this city. The audience Paul could not see was real, already there, already God's. When the work in front of you looks like talking to nobody, that feeling is not the truth of the room. The red light is on, and Someone has people out in the dark you simply have not met yet.

Today’s World

The Impact You Cannot Feel

There is a particular discouragement that comes with quiet, frontline work, the gnawing sense that your text will not matter, that your check-in is a bother, that nobody is really out there. Researchers put that feeling to the test. Across a set of 13 careful experiments involving nearly 6,000 people, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, they had some folks reach out to others, sometimes just a short message, sometimes a small gift like a coffee or a plant, then asked the people on the receiving end how much it actually meant. The results leaned the same way every single time. The ones reaching out badly underestimated how much their gesture landed. And the gap was widest exactly where it felt riskiest, when the contact was a surprise or the relationship felt distant.

That is the precise lie Paul was up against in Corinth. At the end of a long, hostile day the feeling in his chest was that he was failing, shouting down an empty well. Then Jesus stood beside him in the night with the one fact his feelings could not reach, that He already had many people in this city. The discouragement was real. It simply was not accurate. What feels like talking to nobody is so often a room far fuller than you can sense, a harvest already out there in the dark that you just have not met yet.

Christ at the center

Look closely at who shows up in that night vision. It is Jesus, standing beside a frightened man and saying, "I am with you." This is the same Lord who, on His own worst night, knelt in a garden so afraid that He sweat blood, and an angel came to strengthen Him. He knows from the inside what it is to keep going when fear says stop. So He does not lecture Paul about courage. He simply comes near. And the reason He can promise a harvest is that He has already paid for it. When Jesus says, "I have many people in this city," He is not guessing about Corinth's odds. He is speaking as the One whose cross has already purchased every person who will ever say yes to Him there. The people Paul feared were people Christ had already bought. That is why the mission is never as fragile as it feels. The outcome was secured at Calvary before Paul ever opened his mouth.

Application

Where are you tempted to quit because it feels like nothing is working, a prayer for someone, a ministry, a hard conversation you keep having? Today, do not measure it by what you can see. Take the exact fear you would use to justify giving up and write it down, then write God's reply over it, "I am with you, and I have people here you cannot see yet." Then do the next small faithful thing anyway, once more, this week.

Discussion Question

"When you feel like giving up on something God has called you to, what are you usually measuring it by, and how might it change things to believe that some of the most important results are people and outcomes you simply cannot see yet?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we know what it is to keep working at something and feel only the resistance, the closed doors and the cold shoulders, while the fruit stays hidden from us. Thank You that You do not shame us for being afraid. You come near, the way You came near to Paul, and You promise to strengthen us with Your own hand. Help us to believe that the people and the outcomes we cannot see are already in Your keeping. When we are ready to walk away, steady us, brace what is sagging in us, and give us the courage to keep speaking and not be silent. We trust that the harvest is Yours, bought and certain. In Jesus' name, Amen.
