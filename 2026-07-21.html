Protecting the Church's Identity

Sin in the ChurchLesson 4Day 4 of 7

In brief

Picture the scene the city could not stop watching. Two members of the same small church stand in Corinth's forum, suing each other before a pagan judge while the neighbors enjoy the show. Paul is nearly speechless. Is there really no one wise among you? People destined to judge angels are hiring the world to referee a business squabble. Underneath the lawsuits he finds the real problem, a church forgetting who it is, and he answers with a list of everything they used to be. You were washed. Do not let a dispute talk you out of your name.

Yesterday

Yesterday the church learned the difference between God's jurisdiction and its own. God judges outsiders, Paul said. The family tends what happens under its own roof. Today we watch the Corinthians run that principle exactly backwards. Matters that belonged inside the family, disputes between brothers, are being marched downtown and handed to outsiders to decide. The same church that had no stomach for judging a scandal inside its walls found plenty of stomach for suing its own members in front of pagans. Tolerance where there should have been grief, and litigation where there should have been family. Paul reads both as symptoms of the same amnesia. They have forgotten who they are.

Historical Context

The Synagogue Next Door Settled Its Own Quarrels

Two Jewish merchants in a Roman city had a problem, a shipment paid for and never delivered, and everyone in their community knew exactly where that problem would go. Not to the governor's tribunal. To a quiet room near the synagogue, where a panel of respected elders would hear both men out, weigh the Torah, and settle the matter. For centuries, scattered Jewish communities had carried their own justice with them, a court of their own wherever enough families gathered. Rome, pragmatic as ever, mostly let them. Moses had commanded Israel to appoint judges within the community back in the wilderness years, and the habit survived exile, empire, and every hostile neighborhood in between. Family matters stayed in the family. Dragging a fellow Jew before a pagan magistrate was close to unthinkable, a public announcement that your community could not govern itself.

The church in Corinth was born inside that inheritance. Its first members had watched internal justice work their whole lives, patient, private, anchored in God's law. And they walked away from it. Believers with disputes went downtown instead, to the public tribunal where orators performed and rank quietly tipped the scales, and where the city could watch Jesus' people tear at each other for sport. Paul's astonishment in this chapter is not that Christians had conflicts. Every family does. It is that the family with the oldest tradition of settling its own matters, and the greatest Judge to imitate, had chosen the world's stage for its fights. The church did not just have a legal problem. It had an identity problem.

Into the Word

There is a question hiding in the middle of this passage that drips with irony. Is there really not one wise person among you? This is Corinth, the church that boasted about wisdom, sorted itself by clever teachers, and rated preachers like entertainment. Paul has spent three chapters deflating that pride, and now it lands with a thud. All that wisdom, and nobody can arbitrate a dispute between brothers. Today's reading (1 Corinthians 6:1-13) follows the lawsuits down to the appetites underneath them, and it keeps asking the church one question. Do you remember whose you are?

We never learn what the case was about. The passage sits bracketed by sexual scandal on both sides (1 Corinthians 5, 1 Corinthians 6:12-20), so the matter may have been tangled in the same mess, and some readers have wondered whether the litigants were the father and son of the affair itself (1 Corinthians 5:1). Paul never says, and he does not need to. Whatever the case was (1 Corinthians 6:1-11), his grief was the spectacle, brother against brother, in front of unbelievers (1 Corinthians 6:6), in courts where rank and rhetoric tipped the scales while the city kept score. Even Paul's outline here is borrowed. Moses, too, set legal process right beside sexual sin (Deuteronomy 22:22-24), and Paul walks the same path, handling the church's problems on the Torah's rails rather than Rome's.

Street Level

The Name on the Front of the Jersey

Late in a blowout loss, the cameras find two teammates on the same sideline, helmets off, chests together, screaming at each other over a blown assignment. Every phone in the stadium is already up and recording. By morning the clip has ten million views and a one-word caption. Meltdown. Nobody in the comments knows what the assignment was. Nobody cares. The score is forgotten, the opponent is forgotten, and all anyone remembers is that this team turned on itself in public. Which is why every veteran locker room lives by the same unwritten rule. Have it out in here if you have to. Grab a facemask, clear the air, say the hard thing behind that door. Never out there.

Paul is the old captain in this chapter. He never pretends believers will not collide. Families do, teams do, churches do. His grief is the venue. Two brothers marched their quarrel into Corinth's forum and let a city that despised their Lord hand down the family's verdict, and whatever that verdict was, the loss was already total, because the city did not learn who was right. It learned that Jesus' people fight like everyone else. Remember what a team jersey teaches. The name on the back is yours. The name on the front belongs to everybody, and it is the only one the crowd can read from the stands. You can win the argument and stain that name, or take the loss and keep it clean. The captain only ever cares about the front of the jersey.

Today’s World

The Fight Two of Every Five Websites Watched

WordPress is the free software quietly running roughly two of every five websites on earth, built over twenty years by a worldwide community of volunteers who mostly liked each other. Then, in September 2024, at the community's own flagship conference, WordPress's co-founder stood on stage and publicly declared war on one of the ecosystem's biggest hosting companies, WP Engine, calling it "a cancer to WordPress" in a dispute over trademarks and contributions. Within weeks the fight was everywhere the public could see it. Blocked access that stranded thousands of sites, a federal lawsuit, a court injunction, banned contributors, resignations, and a running stream of blog posts and interviews. An independent timeline that tracks the conflict had logged 74 separate events by early 2026, with the case still grinding through court. Meanwhile the trackers that measure such things recorded a turn of their own. WordPress's long-growing share of the web has been slipping across the same stretch, a fact the usage charts report without explaining.

Here is the detail worth sitting with. Almost nobody watching ever learned the merits. Whether the original grievance was righteous is a question the internet never answered, because it never asked. What the watching world saw was simpler and worse, the family that built the open web tearing at itself in public. No tracker can prove what the fight has cost the platform, and a slipping market share can have many causes. But a witness does not wait on proof. That is Paul's grief in Corinth, updated. When brothers take the family quarrel to a venue the world can watch, the world does not learn who was right. It learns that the name on the front cannot hold its own house together. You can win the case and lose the witness. Twenty years of trust can shrink in one keynote.

Christ at the center

The most Jesus-shaped sentence in this chapter is a question nobody wants to ask. Why not rather be wronged? (1 Corinthians 6:7). Paul dares to suggest that losing on purpose can be the most Christian move on the table, and he learned that from his Lord. Jesus once refused to arbitrate an inheritance fight between brothers, asking who had appointed Him their judge (Luke 12:14), and then went on to do something far stranger than judging. He absorbed the loss Himself. Falsely accused, He offered no defense. Entitled to armies of angels, He took the nails. The only man who ever deserved to win every case chose to be wronged, cheated, and condemned, and that unjust verdict is the reason we can be called justified at all. Paul tips the same irony toward Corinth, reminding them that the saints will one day judge the world and even angels (1 Corinthians 6:2, 1 Corinthians 6:3). People with that future can afford to lose a property dispute. The cross does not just forgive our fights. It embarrasses them.

Application

Name the dispute you are closest to right now, the simmering conflict with another believer, the money someone owes you, the public callout you have half-drafted. Before you take the next step, do the math Paul forces on Corinth. What would it cost you to absorb this wrong, and what will winning it cost the family's witness? Then take one concrete de-escalating step today. Propose a mediator you both trust, make the phone call instead of the post, or decide, in writing, what you are willing to lose to keep a brother.

Discussion Question

"Where are you in a standoff with another believer right now, and who is the audience watching you fight it (a comment section, a family text thread, the coworkers who hear your side first)? And in that one specific conflict, what would it actually cost you to take Paul's question seriously, why not rather be wronged?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we confess how much we like to win. We keep score in our churches, our marriages, our group chats, and we can turn even Your family into a courtroom when we feel shortchanged. Forgive us for the fights we have staged in front of a watching world, and for the times we made unbelievers the referees of Your household. Remind us who we are, washed, sanctified, justified, not because we won our case but because Jesus lost His on purpose, for us. Give us one wise friend when conflict comes, the humility to sit down with them, and the strange courage to be wronged rather than to wound. Guard Your church's name in our neighborhoods this week, and let the way we handle our worst disagreements make somebody curious about our Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.
