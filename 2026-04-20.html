Scripture, the Authority

The Role of the BibleLesson 4Day 3 of 7

In brief

An old apostle in a Roman dungeon, writing what he knows is his last letter, hands a young pastor the only sentence that settles the question of who gets the final word.

  • ☩ Paul coined a word that did not exist in Greek before he used it. Scripture, he says, is "God-breathed," the same breath that shaped Adam out of dust still moving through the pages.
  • ☩ The temptation is never to throw the Bible away. The temptation is to keep it while quietly editing it, skipping the parts that press.
  • ☩ God is not against the mind. He wrote to them, argued with them, invited them, and taught them. He simply refuses to be bypassed by them.
  • ☩ When Scripture and our opinion collide, one of them has to move. Today settles which.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul, chained in a Roman cell, put the sword of the Spirit into our hands and told us where the real fight was. Today he is still in chains, only worse. The house arrest has become a dungeon. The letter to Ephesus has become a final letter to a young pastor he is trying to prepare for a long road without him. Sunday gave us the weapon. Monday tells us where the weapon gets its edge. Before we ask how powerful the Bible is, we have to settle the older question. Who is behind it? Whose authority do its words carry? If you have ever picked up the Bible wanting it to agree with you, today is where that wanting gets named.

Historical Context

The Last Letter

Second Timothy was almost certainly the last letter Paul ever wrote. By the time he dictates it, probably around A.D. 66 or 67, he is in a second Roman imprisonment under Nero, and the conditions have gone from bad to grim. This is not the rented house of Acts 28, where guests could visit freely. This is an underground holding cell, cold and dark enough that he asks Timothy specifically to bring a cloak and his scrolls before winter (2 Timothy 4:13). Paul knows what is coming. He tells Timothy so in plain language: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race" (2 Timothy 4:7).

Into that chill, one last instruction. Timothy is a young pastor in Ephesus, a wealthy cosmopolitan city full of teachers who believed they had outgrown the old Jewish Scriptures. Some were adding rules, some were subtracting miracles, some were suggesting that the resurrection had already happened spiritually and no one had quite noticed. Paul's final counsel is not sophisticated. Stay with the Book. Hold the sacred writings you learned from your mother and grandmother. And then, in the center of that counsel, Paul uses a word nobody had ever used before. Theopneustos. Literally, "God-breathed." There is no earlier use of this compound in any surviving Greek text. Paul coins it on the spot, as if no existing word quite said what he needed to say about these pages. The Scripture is not merely inspired. It is exhaled. The same breath that made a living being out of dust in Genesis 2 still moves through every line a weary pastor is about to read by candlelight.

Into the Word

The anchor today is Paul's sentence about Scripture's source and job, 2 Timothy 3:15-17. The supporting verses sharpen what the anchor implies. Isaiah reminds us that God's ways run on an entirely different altitude than ours, which is why our first instinct with the Bible is usually to lower it to our level. Jesus' great commandment tells us God is not rejecting the mind; He is asking for it, all of it, submitted rather than replaced. Read together, these verses answer a practical question most readers feel without naming: when the Bible and my opinion collide, who bends?

Street Level

The Rulebook in the Ref's Pocket

Watch a close call in the fourth quarter of a football game. The receiver leaps, the defender contests, the ball hits the turf, and the stadium becomes a roar of seventy thousand people all certain they saw what happened. The coach is on the sideline yelling. The quarterback is waving his arms. The defensive coordinator is on the headset. Every sportscaster in the booth is offering a theory. And then the referee jogs to the sideline, pulls out a small black-and-white pamphlet that never leaves his pocket, and checks the exact language of the rule. Everybody's opinion has to sit down. The rulebook is going to decide the play. Not the coach. Not the quarterback. Not the camera angle the crowd liked best. The rulebook.

That is what theopneustos does. Scripture is the rulebook in God's back pocket, pulled out when every other voice in the stadium has had its say. You are allowed to have opinions. You are allowed to feel strongly. You are allowed to stand up and wave your arms. But when the passage is read, the opinions sit down. "God-breathed" is not a sentimental compliment the apostle paid his favorite book. It is a jurisdictional claim. When the text speaks, it is the final call on the field. The moment we start editing Scripture to agree with our preferences, we have stopped being players in God's game and started trying to be the referee ourselves. And the referee who appoints himself never lasts through the fourth quarter.

Today’s World

The Religion of the Curated Self

Sociologists call the dominant spiritual posture of our moment expressive individualism. In its most honest form it goes like this: my deepest self is discovered from the inside, not imposed from the outside, and any tradition, teacher, or text that contradicts that inner voice must either adapt to me or be set aside. In the religious version of this, Scripture stops being an authority and becomes a resource. A playlist. You skip the verses that grate and loop the verses that comfort. A 2025 global synthesis of religion and culture, published in the Open Journal of Stigmatized Knowledge and Suppressed Discourses, described the result exactly: a rapid liberalization of ethics accompanied by sincere religious identification, together producing congregations full of people who are genuinely "spiritual" and genuinely sovereign at the same time.

The numbers have been trending in one direction for years. A 2019 Pew report on American religion found that the share of adults who say the Bible is the literal word of God has been cut roughly in half across two generations, while the share who say it is "a book of fables and history" has steadily risen. More recent research in Politics and Religion (Cambridge University Press) has mapped a clean statistical line between a person's source of moral authority (internal preference vs. external text) and that person's positions on the most contested ethical questions of the day. The source of authority is the quiet variable behind almost every loud debate. A 9Marks essay on the topic summed it up in a sentence: expressive individualism fills the pews with people for whom church attendance is an expression of their own identities rather than a submission to any text's authority.

Paul's word theopneustos is a direct challenge to this whole posture. The Bible is not a resource you curate. It is a voice that curates you. The question is not whether this book fits my preferences. The question is whether I am willing to let this book decide what my preferences should be.

Christ at the center

Jesus treated Scripture as the final word in every single dispute He walked into. When Satan quoted a psalm at Him, He did not argue theology; He quoted Deuteronomy back. When the Sadducees denied the resurrection, He did not appeal to philosophy; He said, "You are mistaken because you do not know the Scriptures" (Matthew 22:29). When He stood up in the Nazareth synagogue, He opened the scroll of Isaiah and said, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). The Son of God never once appealed over the head of Scripture. He submitted to it, taught from it, quoted it, embodied it, and declared Himself its fulfillment. He is, John says, the Word who was with God and was God, now in the flesh. If the Bible is God-breathed, the Son is the Breath walking around in sandals. You cannot have the Christ of the New Testament on any terms other than His.

Application

Copy 2 Timothy 3:15-17 by hand today. Not typed. Not photographed. Written, on paper, in your own handwriting, slowly enough to feel each word. Then underline whichever of the four functions (teaching, rebuking, correcting, training) you most often dodge. Leave the paper where you will see it tomorrow morning.

Discussion Question

"What is one specific passage or command in Scripture that you have quietly reinterpreted, softened, or set aside because it pressed against something you wanted to keep (a relationship, a habit, a grudge, a financial pattern, a political identity)? And if theopneustos means what Paul says it means, what would you need to stop defending this week in order to let that passage speak on its own terms?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we come as people who have edited You more often than we have obeyed You. We have read the parts that warmed us and skimmed the parts that pressed. We have treated Your Word as a resource for the days we wanted counsel and a nuisance on the days we wanted our own way. Forgive us. Thank You that Your thoughts run higher than ours, because if they did not, we would be led by something no bigger than our own preferences. Give us the humility to climb rather than the pride to edit. Take our minds, too. We confess that we have sometimes thought the mind was Yours to avoid and ours to keep. We offer it back. Let Your Word teach us, rebuke us, correct us, and train us this week, in that order, and let us stay in the room for all four. In Jesus' name, Amen.
