Time

How to Study the BibleLesson 5Day 2 of 7

In brief

The alarm goes off early, the Bible opens, a chapter gets read, the book closes, and the rest of the day rolls on as if the whole exchange never happened.

  • ☩ Making time for God is the first surrender, and the one that makes every other surrender possible.
  • ☩ Hurry is not a scheduling problem; it is a heart problem that a faster reading speed will never fix.
  • ☩ Jesus inherited the ancient practice of rising in the dark to pray, and then He became the reason anyone still does it.
  • ☩ Psalm 46 ends with the only way a human being has ever actually known God: by stopping.

Yesterday

Yesterday asked you to pick one chapter, write the reference on a sticky note, and place it where the morning could not miss it. That sticky note was the first commitment of the week. Today the week pays the bill. The chapter you chose is going to require fifteen or twenty minutes that currently belong to something else: the phone, the commute audio, the second cup of coffee, the news you already knew was bad. Isaiah's promise from Sabbath was that the Word does not return empty. But the Word can only come back with anything if it is first sent out into an actual block of your day. Time is the soil this whole week depends on.

Historical Context

The Hour Before Dawn

In first-century Judea, a practicing Jew's day was built around three fixed prayer times. Shacharit at dawn. Mincha in the afternoon. Maariv at nightfall. The morning prayer was the anchor, and it required rising in the dark so the opening words of the Shema could be said as the first light broke over the Mount of Olives. The rabbis argued about how to define "morning light" for this purpose; one answer went like this: early enough to tell the blue thread from the white thread on the edge of a prayer shawl. The discipline was not about optimizing a schedule. It was a daily confession that the day belonged to God before the day began.

That rhythm produced Jesus. Mark tells us He "got up very early in the morning, while it was still dark, and went out and made his way to a deserted place" to pray. He did not invent that hour. He inherited it. But He did something with it that no one else had done: He poured the most important communication of His life into that quiet pre-sunrise block of solitude. By the time John 15 was spoken in an upper room on the night of His arrest, the disciples had watched Him do this for three years. He was not giving them an abstract discourse on abiding. He was describing the life they had watched Him live every single morning before they were awake.

Into the Word

A grapevine in a vineyard never strains. The branch does not wake up on Tuesday and decide it will produce grapes that week; it simply stays on the trunk, and the sap does the rest. That picture is why Jesus reached for it on the night before His arrest. When He told His disciples to remain in Him, He was not handing them a new assignment. He was describing a connection they already had and asking whether they would live consciously inside it. The psalmist called this rhythm delight. The Bereans lived it by examining the Scriptures daily. The first cost of conscious abiding is time, which is why today starts there.

Street Level

The Seventh Minute

The phone buzzes, and it is the friend who only calls when something is actually happening. You pick up, and the first four minutes are logistics: yes, everybody is fine, yes, the kids are back in school, yes, the house finally sold. By minute five you are both half-distracted, trading closing phrases, thinking about the dinner still on the stove. Then there is a pause neither of you rushes to fill. And in the silence of minute seven, after the updates have been checked off and the call was almost already over, the real reason they called finally surfaces, quietly, with the tone that changes everything.

Abiding has a seventh minute. Most of what passes for time with God in modern life is the first three minutes: a drive-time podcast, a verse from an app, a quick prayer while the coffee brews. None of it is wasted. But none of it reaches the silence where the real thing tends to surface. The vine and the branch do their most important work in the unhurried hour, not the notification. Jesus used the word meno, abide, as if it were a place you sit down in. Hurry is not a scheduling problem; it is a heart problem that a faster devotional will never fix. A seventh minute only happens inside a block, and blocks have to be defended.

Today’s World

Interrupted Even When We Are Resting

Researchers now measure something called time poverty, and the finding that has surprised them most is how little it depends on the actual number of hours in your day. A 2025 diary study in the Journal of Happiness Studies tracked people across ordinary weeks and found that those whose leisure was repeatedly interrupted reported feeling significantly more time poor than those whose leisure ran continuously, even when the total hours were identical. The length of a block, not the size of it, is what makes it feel like anything. Fifteen minutes in a chair with no phone mattered more to well-being than two hours spent half-checking one.

Harvard Business School economists studying the same phenomenon across very large American samples have reported what they call a startling result: the subjective feeling of being time poor is associated with lower well-being than even the shock of losing a job. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ American Time Use Survey, released in 2025, showed the average adult logs about five hours of leisure a day, but more than half of that is television, typically consumed while a phone handles the rest of the attention. A 2025 paper in Knowledge Management Research and Practice added a further cost: employees living chronically time poor showed measurably lower knowledge sharing with coworkers. Nobody builds anything worth handing over in three minute margins.

The word Jesus used for abiding was meno. It is a stationary verb. A branch does not sip from the Vine in fifteen second intervals, and abiding was never going to fit inside a format the attention economy is willing to spare. What the economists are proving in spreadsheets, Jesus said in a garden: fruit requires continuity, not access. The seventh minute of the phone call is where the reason for the call finally arrives. The seventh minute of the morning is where the Vine finally speaks.

Christ at the center

Look at the arithmetic of His life. He had three years of public ministry, crowds following Him everywhere, twelve men who could barely get five minutes alone with Him, a constant queue of the sick and the desperate, and the certain knowledge that the cross was waiting at the end. If anyone in human history had a legitimate reason to treat prayer as an efficiency loss, it was the Son of God on His way to redeem the world. Instead, the gospel writers make a point of noting how often He rose in the dark and went somewhere nobody could reach Him. He did not meno in crowds. He meno-ed in the Father. The fruit that fed five thousand, healed ten lepers, and forgave a thief on the neighboring cross grew on a Vine whose roots were fed every morning before anyone knew He was awake. No servant has ever had a better reason than He did to skip the quiet hour. None of them did. Neither can we.

Application

Tomorrow morning, set your alarm fifteen minutes earlier than you currently do. Not an hour. Fifteen. Open your Bible to the chapter you chose on Sabbath. Do not try to finish it. Read the first paragraph out loud, slowly. Close the book. Sit quietly for sixty seconds without reaching for the phone. That is the whole assignment. The morning is not about volume; it is about presence. Rebuild the habit one paragraph at a time.

Discussion Question

"What are you currently doing in the first thirty minutes after you wake up (the honest answer, not the aspirational one: phone, news, coffee, scrolling, nothing at all), and what would have to change in the last thirty minutes before bed for tomorrow morning to actually belong to God instead of to the rush?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we confess that our schedules have been shaped by everything except You. We have traded the quiet of the dawn for the glow of a phone, and we have told ourselves that we do not have the time we used to have. We are not sure that is true. Teach us to raphah, to drop our hands long enough for the knowledge of You to catch up to us. Remind us that the branch never strained to produce the grape. Give us back the pre-sunrise hour our grandparents knew without thinking about it, and fill it with Your presence. Where our search has been quarter-hearted, pull it toward the middle of the page. Where we have read in a hurry, slow us down. And if there is a single paragraph this week that You want to carve into our lives, lead us to it tomorrow morning, and keep us there until it has had time to do what You sent it to do. In Jesus' name, Amen.
