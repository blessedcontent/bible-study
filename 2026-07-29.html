Warning Against Idolatry

All to the Glory of GodLesson 5Day 5 of 7

In brief

An invitation sits on a Corinthian kitchen table. Dinner in the temple dining room, the god listed as host. Some church members see no problem, since idols are nothing. Today Paul agrees the statue is hollow and then says the party is not unhosted. What pagans sacrifice goes to demons, and the man who takes communion on Sabbath cannot spend Friday at their table. Meat from the market is just meat. Worship is never just dinner. You can eat at either table, but you cannot eat at both.

Yesterday

Yesterday left us standing among the graves, holding a mold pressed from Israel's story. Remember the first failure on the list. The people sat down to eat and drink in front of a golden calf, and got up to party. A meal in honor of a false god, that was the crack where a rescued nation broke. Now watch Paul turn from their desert to Corinth's dinner scene, because the same crack runs straight through it. The city's temples are still setting tables, the invitations are still arriving, and some of the church is still convinced attendance is harmless. Today Paul stops describing the danger and names the host.

Historical Context

The Unseen World Was Standing Room Only

Nobody in the first century thought the air was empty. A Corinthian managed unseen powers the way we manage passwords, constantly and mostly without thinking. Little household gods watched the hearth. Spirits loitered at crossroads and doorways. Sailors tattooed vows to Poseidon, mothers hung charms on cradles, and businessmen scratched curses onto lead tablets and dropped them into wells, several of which archaeologists have pulled out of the ground at Corinth. Even the philosophers who mocked the myths kept a respectful nod for the daimones, the crowd of middling spirits thought to fill the space between gods and men. The question in that world was never whether invisible powers existed. It was which ones you were feeding.

That is the world Paul writes into, and it explains the edge in his voice today. He has already agreed, twice, that an idol is nothing, a carved nothing with a famous name. But nothing-statues can still have occupied tables. Behind the harmless-looking marble, Paul says, stands something ancient and hungry that Scripture calls demonic, gladly collecting worship addressed to anyone but God. The Corinthian sophisticate smirking at the myths while reclining in the temple's dining room had made a category error. He thought he was outsmarting an empty statue. He was keeping company with what stood behind it. The statue was hollow. The party never was.

Into the Word

Paul has been circling the idol-meat question for two chapters. Today he lands, inside the same sweep of warning we entered yesterday (1 Corinthians 10:5-22), and his first word on arrival is flee. Then he does something remarkable for a man drawing a hard line. He reasons with them, sensible people that you are, judge for yourselves, as he takes the meat question back up (1 Corinthians 10:14-22). The argument he builds runs through the communion table, because you cannot understand what is wrong with the demons' table until you understand what actually happens at the Lord's.

Be clear about what Paul is not saying, because he is careful here. Meat that started at an altar and ended up at the market stall is just meat, and believers may buy it with a clean conscience, exactly as he argued when the issue first came up (1 Corinthians 8:1-13). The idol has no reality to contaminate anything (1 Corinthians 8:4). But participating in the pagan ceremony itself is a different act entirely (1 Corinthians 10:20-21). What pagans sacrifice, Paul says, they sacrifice to demons, and I do not want you to be participants with demons (1 Corinthians 10:20). He is standing on the song of Moses, which said Israel sacrificed to demons when it chased other gods (Deuteronomy 32:17). The statue is hollow. What collects the worship behind it is not.

Street Level

The One Thing You Cannot Split

Two of your closest friends stop speaking to each other, for good this time, and you make the honorable decision not to choose. At first the arrangement holds. You get lunch with her on Tuesdays, you meet him for breakfast on Saturdays, and nobody minds, because nobody owns your calendar. The trouble starts in the inner room. She processes the whole falling-out with you, all of it, and one evening while she is mid-sentence, his name lights up your phone on the table between you, and you feel the two loyalties grind somewhere in your stomach. You never repeat a word. It does not matter. Within months she is trimming what she tells you. He has gone vague. Neither of them caught you betraying anything. They just both did the quiet math. A secret kept in the middle lives next door to the person it is about. A year later you know less about both of them than you did the week they split.

Notice what died and what survived. The lunches survived. Presence was never the problem, and Paul says the same about Corinth's ordinary tables. The market meat stayed meat. The friendships with unbelieving neighbors stayed open. What cannot survive is a seat in two inner rooms, because confidence is the kind of gift that only exists whole. Splitting it does not divide it. It dissolves it. That is the cannot in you cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons. Communion is not attendance. It is participation, the inner room where a covenant actually lives, and every idol runs its feast on the same currency. Hold a seat at both and each one empties the other, no matter how carefully you manage the calendar. God's jealousy is simply what any wholehearted love does when it is handed half. Some gifts multiply when you share them. Loyalty is not one of them.

Today’s World

The Table That Feeds on Its Guests

In 2018 the Supreme Court let states legalize sports betting, and the table filled fast. One state became thirty-eight. Annual wagers grew from under five billion dollars to more than 121 billion by 2023, with 94 percent of it placed through a phone. The industry's word for itself is entertainment, a game, nothing. The ledgers tell it differently. Researchers at UC San Diego, writing in JAMA Internal Medicine in early 2025, tracked internet searches for help with gambling addiction and found them up 23 percent nationwide since legalization began, millions of quiet cries for help typed into a search bar. A UCLA study of household finances found that in states allowing online sports gambling, the likelihood of a personal bankruptcy filing rose 25 to 30 percent in the years after legalization, while average debt-collection amounts rose 8 percent.

Read those numbers again beside today's chapter. The industry sells a seat at a game, and the receipts describe a table that feeds on its guests. That is not a scandal unique to betting apps. It is the economics of every occupied table, and it is why Paul refused to call the temple banquets harmless even while agreeing the statues were hollow. Corinth's sophisticates said the idol is nothing. The app says it is just a game. Paul's answer covers both. The nothing has a table, the table has a host, and the host is hungry. One table in the world feeds its guests, at the Host's own expense. Every other table eventually reverses the direction of the meal. So before you sit down anywhere, ask the only question that matters. Who eats here, and who gets eaten.

Christ at the center

Look closely at what Paul calls the communion cup, the cup of blessing. It did not start out that way. In Gethsemane, Jesus knelt over a different cup and asked three times whether it could pass from Him (Matthew 26:39). That cup held the judgment our idolatries earned, every table we set for other gods, every loyalty we split. He drank it to the bottom. That is why the cup He hands us holds only blessing. The exchange happened in the dark, His portion for ours. And it tells you everything about the difference between the two tables in today's reading. Every idol's table runs on the same economics, it feeds on its worshipers, taking their money, their devotion, their years, and returning hunger. At the Lord's table the economics run backward. The Host is the meal. His body, His blood, His life handed across the table to people who brought nothing but appetite. Demons consume their guests. Jesus feeds His. That is the whole difference, and it is everything.

Application

Name your other table. Somewhere in your week there is a place your loyalty keeps eating, a feed that gets your first attention, a money goal that quietly steers your decisions, an approval you keep working for, a habit that takes and takes and calls itself harmless. Write down its name today, the honest one. Then push back from that table with one physical act before bedtime. Delete the app for a week. Decline the invitation. Move the money. Say no out loud. One table has a Host who feeds you. Stop paying for a seat at the one that feeds on you.

Discussion Question

"If you audited your last month like an accountant, where did your loyalty actually eat most often (the feed you check before you pray, the money goal that steers your choices, the person whose approval sets your mood)? And what would it cost you, honestly, to push back from that table this week, and is the cost the reason you have not done it?"

Closing Prayer

Lord, thank You for being jealous. We have lived long enough to know what love looks like when it stops caring who else sits at the table, and we do not want that kind of politeness from You. We confess that we have kept seats at other tables, the ones that take our attention, our money, our hunger for approval, and hand back emptiness with a smile. Forgive our split calendars and our divided hearts. Thank You, Jesus, for the cup You drank in the dark so that the cup in our hands could hold nothing but blessing. Make Your table the one we come home to. Fill our hearts so full of love for You and for the people around us that the old vacancies stay closed. We are not stronger than You, and we are done pretending we want to be. In Jesus' name, Amen.
