Unity

Stewardship and MissionLesson 11Day 6 of 7

In brief

When the relief fund finally travels, Paul refuses to carry it alone. Titus goes, and with him two brothers the churches themselves chose, every coin handled in the open, because sacred money moves with witnesses. But watch what Paul is really building. Greek money on Jewish tables. Surplus flowing toward need until it evens out. Former strangers proving with their wallets that they are one family now, in full view of the churches. Money can split a congregation, and money can stitch congregations together. An offering aimed by love does what sermons alone cannot. It makes the family visible.

Yesterday

Yesterday left us watching the Macedonian believers beg for the privilege of giving, and the point landed close to the skin. The attitude is most of the gift. But a glad gift, once it leaves a glad hand, still has somewhere to go. It gets counted, carried, guarded, and finally spent on real bread for real people, and every step of that journey preaches something. Today the letter follows the money out the door. Who carries it, how they were chosen, and what it means when Greek coins finally land on Jewish tables. It turns out the offering was never just relief. It was architecture.

Historical Context

Sacred Money Traveled With Witnesses

Jewish communities scattered across the empire had solved this problem generations earlier. Every year, from Rome to Babylon, Jewish families paid the half-shekel temple tax, and every year those collections had to cross bandit country to reach Jerusalem. So the communities built a system everyone could trust. The money was gathered publicly, sealed, and sent with appointed envoys, respected men chosen by their own communities, traveling together so that no single pair of hands ever held the sacred funds alone. The convoys were famous. Even Roman officials wrote about the Jewish gold that streamed toward Jerusalem, and the system worked because the couriers were witnesses for each other from the first coin to the last.

Paul, a diaspora Jew who grew up inside that system, builds the church's collection on the same wisdom. Titus does not travel alone. Two brothers go with him, not friends Paul picked, but men the churches themselves selected, praised by all the churches, so that every hand on the money answers to somebody. Paul says the reason out loud, taking great care to do what is right, not only before the Lord but before men, because he knew his critics were watching for one loose coin to build an accusation on. And there is a second layer under the logistics. When that little delegation finally walked into Jerusalem, Greek believers carrying Greek gifts to Jewish saints, the convoy itself was the sermon. The money arrived with faces attached, and the faces said family.

Into the Word

Money is the last thing most of us would trust to hold a family together, because we have all watched it do the opposite. Estates that split siblings. Church budgets that split boards. Corinth knew money's dividing power. Yet across these two chapters (2 Corinthians 8:1-9:15), Paul keeps handling the offering like a load-bearing beam, something God intends to build unity on. Today's passages show the design, and it starts with what the money is supposed to do between churches that have never met.

A vision that beautiful still has to survive the road, and Paul knows exactly how offerings die. Not usually from theft. From suspicion. So watch how carefully he builds the carrying.

So the money is aimed at equality and carried by witnesses. One question remains, the deepest one. Why does Paul care this much? Years later, writing to Rome while this very gift traveled with him, he showed his heart.

Street Level

The Cords Ran Across the Fence Line

The night the ice storm took the power out, the neighborhood found out what it was. One house on the block had a generator, and by morning the map had redrawn itself. Orange extension cords ran out of that garage, across the driveway, over the fence line, into the kitchen of the retired couple two doors down. Coolers migrated. The family with the gas stove boiled water for the family with the newborn. People who had waved at each other for nine years stood in one garage learning each other's names, and nobody wrote anything down, because nobody was keeping accounts. For four cold days the property lines stayed right where the county drew them, and you could not see them from the street. What you could see was the cords.

That is what Paul is building with a relief offering, and why he handles it like architecture. Money aimed by love does what those cords did. Surplus runs across the line to need, in the open, where everyone can watch the current move, and the current is expected to reverse. Next storm it is your freezer and their chainsaw. Equality, in Paul's design, was never everyone owning identical amounts. It is a family in which abundance is a rotating assignment, need is a rotating assignment, and the connection outlasts them both. So picture Corinth's coins landing in Jerusalem's kitchens, crossing a fence line centuries in the making, and picture what the watching world could suddenly see. It had been one household all along. Generosity is the cord that proves it.

Today’s World

The Mission Field Started Sending Missionaries Back

On June 9, 2025, the Pew Research Center published a decade's worth of religious census work, and one line of it quietly rearranged the map of the Christian world. By 2020, sub-Saharan Africa had become home to roughly three in ten of the world's Christians, more of them than any other region on earth, a distinction Europe long held. The faith's center of gravity has moved south, and the traffic has turned around with it. Nigeria's Redeemed Christian Church of God has planted hundreds of congregations across North America. Mission researchers now describe a polycentric faith, with major missionary movements based in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, Brazilian and Korean senders prominent among them. Scholars even have a name for the pattern, reverse mission, churches in regions that Western agencies once treated chiefly as mission fields now sending missionaries and planting churches of their own, including in the countries that first sent them.

A century and a half ago, that flow ran almost entirely one direction. Missionaries, money, and printed Scriptures sailed out from Europe and North America toward continents the senders called the mission field, and nobody drawing those maps imagined the current ever coming back. But Paul would have. The collection he carried ran on the principle that in God's family, surplus is a temporary assignment, your abundance for their need now, theirs for yours later, and he grounded it in an exchange already in motion, spiritual blessing flowing out of Jerusalem and material blessing flowing back. Twenty centuries later the wheel has turned again, and churches in regions the old maps marked as the mission field are sending preachers, prayer, and whole congregations back to the places that first mailed it. The family never was a charity. It is a circulation, and the current is still moving.

Christ at the center

The night before He died, Jesus prayed for people He could see and people He could not, for everyone who would ever believe, that they may all be one, so that the world would know the Father sent Him. Then He went out and paid for that oneness, tearing down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile in His own flesh, making one new family where two hostile peoples had stood. Everything here lives downstream from that purchase. The equality between churches exists because both churches were bought with the same blood. The delegation walking to Jerusalem exists because the wall it once could not have crossed is rubble. Giving, Paul says, signals loyalty to Christ the Head, and a body that shares its supply is simply a body that is actually connected to Him. So do not read today as church finance. Somewhere between a Greek harbor and a Jerusalem doorway, coins carried by witnesses became evidence that His prayer was being answered. The offering plate is one of the places where it still is.

Application

Aim one gift across a line today. Choose believers you will never meet, a congregation in another country, a mission serving a different language, a church plant nothing like your own, and give something real toward their need. Before you send it, say one sentence out loud. These are my family. Then pray for them by name, or by place if you have no names, and notice how differently the money feels when it travels as family business instead of charity to strangers.

Discussion Question

"When your giving goes somewhere far away (a mission field, disaster relief, believers in another country), does it honestly feel like family business or like charity to strangers? And what would change about how you give, and how much you care where it lands, if you pictured the people receiving it seated at your own table?"

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for making us part of a family bigger than our address, our language, and our lifetime. Thank You for the believers we have never met who prayed the gospel down the road to us, and for the ones right now, somewhere far away, whose need is the assignment You are handing to our surplus. Teach us the circulation of Your household. Keep us from gripping abundance like a rank or hiding need like a shame. Make our church's handling of money so honest, so open, so witnessed, that no shadow ever falls across Your name because of us. And when our gifts travel where we cannot, let them arrive the way that first delegation did, with love attached, saying family in a language every saint can read. Knit Your church into one, Lord, and let it begin with our open hands. In Jesus' name, Amen.
