The Law

Sin, the Gospel, and the LawLesson 9Day 4 of 7

In brief

The Hebrew word for "law" comes from a root that means to point or to shoot an arrow, and the law's first job has always been to aim the human heart in the direction it was made to fly.

  • ☩Without the law, sin is a fog. With the law, sin is a face in the mirror. The law's first work is not to condemn but to name.
  • ☩Paul says the knowledge of sin comes through the law. John says sin is lawlessness itself. They are the same diagnosis from two angles.
  • ☩Jesus folded the Ten Commandments into one Hebrew sentence (love God) and one Hebrew sentence (love neighbor), then said the entire Bible hangs on those two hooks.
  • ☩The law was never the fence keeping us small. It was the floor under our feet, and the apostle John says the commandments of God are not heavy.

Yesterday

Yesterday Jesus prescribed the surgery: cut off the hand, the foot, the eye that keeps tripping the trap. We named the strongholds the enemy has built in the actual rooms of our lives. Today we step back to the prior question. How do we know what counts as a stronghold in the first place? What is the standard the eye is measured by? Without the law, the eye does not know what it has been looking at. Without a standard outside ourselves, the only court we have is the one Samson trusted on Sunday morning, yashar hi b'einay, "right in my own eyes." The law is the older mirror. It does not flatter and it does not lie, and the man who keeps looking into it is the only man who can see what needs to be cut.

Historical Context

A Code Unlike Any Other in the Ancient World

By the time Israel stood at the foot of Sinai, the ancient Near East already had a long shelf of legal codes. The Code of Hammurabi from Babylon (around 1750 BC) carved hundreds of clauses onto a stone stele showing the king receiving justice from the sun-god Shamash. The Hittite Laws regulated marriage, theft, and ritual purity for a kingdom stretching across modern Turkey. The Middle Assyrian Laws set out brutal penalties for women caught in adultery and detailed property rules. All of them were royal codes. All of them were promulgated through a king as the human guarantor of order. All of them began with the king's name and his honor as the reason the code existed.

The Sinai Ten were structured differently in ways the audience would have noticed instantly. The code was given to an entire people, not handed down through a king. It was spoken aloud by God Himself, the only time in human history a national assembly heard the divine voice giving its founding law (Exod. 19-20). It opened not with a royal name but with a relational claim: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery." Identity first, then instruction. The Hebrew word for "law" is torah, and its root verb yarah means to throw an arrow, to point in a direction, to teach. The same root gives Hebrew the word moreh, a teacher. Torah in Hebrew was never primarily punitive. It was directional, the older word for what a Father gives a child the morning they go off into a world full of cliffs.

Into the Word

Two short verses together do the entire diagnostic work the rest of the week will treat. Paul names what the law does. John names what sin is. Then Jesus walks in and tells us the whole Old Testament library hangs on two hooks, and both of them are about love.

Street Level

The Tuning Fork at the Piano

Walk into any concert hall in the world an hour before a performance and you will hear the same sound. A tuning fork being struck against a stand. The fork is a small steel U on a wooden handle. You tap it, and it sings a single note: middle A, four hundred and forty vibrations per second, the same pitch in 1885 as it is today. The tuning fork does not tune anything. It cannot reach inside the piano. It cannot adjust the tension on a single string. All it can do is sound the true note, hold it in the air, and let the human ear hear how far the piano has drifted since the last time it was tuned.

That is the entire job of the law. Torah, the Hebrew word with the arrow root, sounds the true note. It does not tune the human heart. It cannot reach inside us and adjust what is out of pitch. What it can do, and what nothing else in the universe can do quite the same way, is hold a perfect A in the air long enough for us to hear how flat we have gone. Romans 3:20 has been saying this for two thousand years: the knowledge of sin comes through the law. The tuner is somebody else. The fork is doing exactly what it was made to do when it tells you the truth about the piano. The discomfort you feel when the law lands on a specific corner of your week is not the law's failure. It is the sound of a true note next to an honest ear.

Today’s World

Forty Years of Code Made Visible in Sixty Seconds

On February 6, 2023, two earthquakes struck southern Turkey within hours of each other along the East Anatolian Fault. The combined death toll across Turkey and Syria passed fifty-five thousand. Two and a half years later, a 2025 paper in the engineering journal Applied Sciences, part of the steady stream of follow-up research published since the disaster, quantified what the rubble had already told the world. The buildings that came down were not random. The pattern was a generation of code compliance, or the lack of it.

Buildings constructed under Turkey's 1998 seismic code and its later revisions, properly inspected, properly engineered, properly built to the modern standard, generally held. Buildings constructed under the older 1975 code, or more commonly constructed with the modern code on the paperwork but not on the rebar (the steel skipped, the columns thinned, the inspections paid off), were where most of the bodies came from. Cambridge University's post-quake field report named the same finding in starker terms: deficiencies in structure and construction shortcuts were the main cause of casualties. A natural disaster did the killing through the gaps in the code, and the code itself had been on the books long enough to have saved everyone underneath it.

That is what Paul means in Romans 3:20 when he says the knowledge of sin comes through the law. The law does not cause the earthquake. Sin causes the earthquake. The law is what lets us name which buildings were not put up the way they were supposed to be. Without the code the rubble is just rubble and we shake our heads at fate. With the code the rubble is a diagnosis, a specific failure with a specific name at a specific address. The law was always doing this exact work in the human heart. It names the gaps that fate alone could never explain.

Christ at the center

Jesus is the only Person who ever fulfilled the two hooks the rest of the canon hangs on. He loved His Father with all His heart and soul and mind and strength, every breath of His thirty-three years, and He loved His neighbor as Himself all the way to a cross where the neighbor was beating Him. Tomorrow we will see what that perfect fulfillment cost Him. Today the news is simpler. The law that names our sin is the same law Jesus walked through without one misstep, and the obedience He earned has been written under our names. He did not abolish the standard. He did not lower the bar. He stood under the bar Himself and held it up so we could walk underneath it inside Him. The law tells us what love looks like; the cross is where love stood up to its own definition. Every time we read Romans 3:20 and feel the X-ray naming the tumor, we are reading words written by a man who knew the Surgeon by name.

Application

Read Exodus 20:3-17 out loud today. Not silently. Out loud. The whole Ten, slowly, with your name in the second-person verbs. Take six or seven minutes. Then sit with two questions for five more. Which commandment does the Hebrew arrow seem to be pointing at this week of my life? And what would change tomorrow if I treated that commandment less as a fence keeping me small and more as the floor my Father has put under me so I do not fall through?

Discussion Question

"When you hear the phrase 'God's law,' what is the first picture that rises in your chest, and how honest does that picture feel under the Bible's own language for it? (A courtroom and a verdict. A fence around a garden. A list of demands you keep failing. A mirror you do not look at if you do not have to. A floor under your feet you stopped noticing.) What would it look like this week to read one stretch of Scripture (Psalm 119, Exodus 20, the Sermon on the Mount) the way a child reads a love letter from a parent who is trying to keep him alive?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we have spent too long treating Your law as a list of restrictions instead of as the love letter it actually is. We have read it grudgingly when we read it at all, and we have been quick to call it a burden every time it told us something about ourselves we did not want to hear. Forgive us. Today open our eyes to torah the way Your psalmist opened his: as the arrow You shoot to point us toward a life we would never aim at on our own. Show us today the specific commandment our week needs. Help us not to flinch when it names the wound; let it do its diagnostic work all the way through. And keep us from despair when the naming lands, because the same Voice that spoke from Sinai walked among us in Galilee and stood under the bar for us at Calvary. Your commands are not heavy. Make our hearts light enough to lift them. In Jesus' name, Amen.
