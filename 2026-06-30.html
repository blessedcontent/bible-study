The City of Corinth

Paul's Ministry in CorinthLesson 1Day 4 of 7

In brief

Walk the streets of Corinth and you smell it before you see it, meat from the temple butchers, incense drifting off a hundred shrines, the noise of a port that never sleeps. Two seas pour their cargo and their sailors through this one town, and money, sex, and gods are all for sale. Into that, Paul moves in with a Jewish couple making tents and starts a church. The believers there did not leave the world to follow Jesus. They had to learn to follow Him while still standing in its mess. God does not wait for a clean city to plant a holy church. He grows one right in the middle of the chaos.

Yesterday

Yesterday we watched Paul make his stubborn decision: know nothing but Christ crucified, and dig in for the long haul. He stayed in Corinth a year and a half, far longer than anywhere else on that journey. Today we find out why that took so much resolve. It is one thing to admire Paul's commitment in the abstract. It is another to walk the streets he walked, to feel the exact gravity of the place, and to realize what it cost him and the young believers to live for Jesus there. You cannot understand the letters to the Corinthians without first understanding Corinth. So today we put our feet on its pavement and look around.

Historical Context

The Edict That Sent Two Tentmakers East

Around AD 49, the emperor Claudius lost patience with the Jewish community in Rome and ordered them all out of the city. Among the families packing up their lives and looking for somewhere new were a couple named Aquila and Priscilla, leatherworkers and tentmakers by trade. They landed in Corinth, a natural choice, because Corinth was where the money and the traffic were. That imperial expulsion, a small cruelty of Roman politics, is the unseen reason Paul walked into Corinth and immediately found friends, a workbench, and a home. God was wiring the city for the gospel before Paul ever arrived.

And what a city it was. Corinth sat on a narrow neck of land with a harbor on each side, so the wealth of the eastern and western Mediterranean flowed straight through it. Towering over the marketplace was the Acrocorinth, a steep hill crowned with a temple to Aphrodite, the goddess of love, and the city had a reputation so wild that "to act like a Corinthian" had become slang for living loose. Shrines to dozens of gods dotted the streets, and most of the meat sold in the market had first been offered to one idol or another. For a new believer, there was no neighborhood to escape to. Idolatry and immorality were not the red-light district. They were the air everyone breathed.

Into the Word

To feel what the gospel was up against in Corinth, we look at two snapshots. First, how Paul actually got his start in the city, quietly, at a workbench with two new friends. Then, two glimpses from the letter he would later send back, where the moral and religious pressure of the place rises right off the page. The city was not a neutral backdrop. It was a constant undertow, and these verses show both the foothold the gospel found and the current it had to fight.

Street Level

The One Sober Person in the Room

Picture the designated driver at a loud party. They are not waiting out in the parking lot for it to be over, and they are not hiding in the car with the doors locked. They are inside, in the middle of it, laughing at the jokes, holding a cup that happens to be soda, fully part of the night. But something is different about them, and everyone can feel it. They stay clear while the room gets blurry, because they have somewhere to be later and people who are counting on them to get everyone home in one piece.

That is the posture Paul expected from the believers in Corinth, and it is the one Jesus still expects from us. Holiness was never about leaving the party. Paul flatly told them that steering clear of every immoral person would mean leaving the planet. The Corinthian Christians had to stay right in the middle of their wild city, at the dinners and the markets and the festivals, present and warm and genuinely there. They just could not get drunk on what the city was pouring. Being different does not mean being absent. It means keeping a clear head in a room that is losing its own, because you remember Who you belong to and where the night is actually taking you.

Today’s World

Designed So You Cannot Look Away

Corinth's pressure came carved in marble on every corner. Ours fits in a pocket. The endless feed you thumb through at a red light was not some accident of technology. A designer named Aza Raskin invented the infinite scroll, the trick that makes a screen pour out content forever so your thumb never hits a natural place to stop, and he has since said publicly that he regrets it. Add the slot-machine pull of notifications and small unpredictable rewards, and you are holding a device built by teams of engineers whose actual job is to capture your attention and not give it back. Insiders like Tristan Harris, who founded the Center for Humane Technology, have spent years warning that this is deliberate. The pull most people quietly blame on their own weak willpower was engineered on purpose.

That is Corinth with a charging cable. The idols there were not tucked away in one bad part of town the believers could just avoid. They were the air everyone breathed, woven into the meat, the markets, the festivals. The Corinthian Christians could not move somewhere cleaner, and neither can we. And the answer then is the answer now. God never told them to flee the world, only to keep a clear head while standing in the middle of it. You do not beat an engineered current by pretending it is not pulling on you. You beat it by remembering Whose you are while your feet are still in the water.

Christ at the center

Corinth was a city full of images of gods who were not there. Every shrine was a face carved for a being who could not see, a name for a power that could not save. And into that gallery of empty statues, God sent the opposite of an idol. Jesus is not an image we made of a god we imagined. He is the real God who took on a real face and walked into a real city like this one. Where the idols were nothing pretending to be something, Christ is everything who became approachable. He did not hover safely above Corinth's filth; He moved into towns just like it, ate with the kind of people Paul lists in that hard verse, the swindlers and the immoral, and offered them a way out that no marble god ever could. The city sold a thousand counterfeit saviors. Jesus is the one who is actually real, and He came looking for the people the counterfeits had used up.

Application

Name your Corinth honestly. What is the one value of the place you live and work in, the pressure in the air, that quietly pulls on you the hardest? It might be the worship of money, the assumption that sex has no rules, the demand to always be impressive online. Write it down. Then write one specific way you will stay in that world this week, present and kind, without absorbing that value. Holiness is not running away. It is being clearly different right where you already are.

Discussion Question

"Which pressure in your everyday environment do you most often underestimate (money, status, the way everyone around you treats relationships or success), and what is one concrete habit that could help you stay genuinely different without cutting yourself off from the people who need you there?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we live in our own Corinth, surrounded by things that promise what they can never deliver. Thank You that You do not ask us to flee the world but to live differently inside it, the way You grew a church right in the heart of that loud, idol-soaked city. Open our eyes to the counterfeits we have quietly started serving, the hollow gods we fear and feed. Remind us that they are nothing, and that You alone are real. Give us the courage to stay present among the people around us without being shaped into their image, so that our lives point them to the one Savior who is not made of stone. In Jesus' name, Amen.
