Planning

Stewardship and MissionLesson 11Day 4 of 7

In brief

A prophet stands up in Antioch and announces a famine that has not arrived yet, and the church's answer is not panic. It is a plan, each believer according to ability, relief on the road before the hunger peaks. Paul learned giving in that school, and today he brings that pattern to Corinth. God loves the giver who decided in the heart beforehand, in proportion to what is actually in the barn, and He practices what He asks. His own Gift was planned before the world fell. Grace is spontaneous in its love and deliberate in its delivery.

Yesterday

Yesterday ended at the bottom of the indescribable Gift, with four clean fuels in the tank, gratitude, imitation, shared blessing, and visible love. But Corinth is standing proof that good fuel alone moves nothing. A year ago this church had the purest motives in Greece. They wanted to give, they pledged first, and then the wanting quietly died of no decisions. Today Paul adds the unglamorous ingredient that carries intention across the finish line. Planning. It sounds like the least spiritual word in the Christian vocabulary, right up until you discover who did it first, and how long before the world needed Him to.

Historical Context

The Church Planned for a Famine That Had Not Hit Yet

Years before this letter, in the church at Antioch, a prophet named Agabus stood up in a meeting and said something nobody wanted to hear. A severe famine was coming, not someday but soon, across the whole Roman world. Now watch what that young church did with advance warning. No hoarding. No panic buying. Luke records their response in a single businesslike sentence, the disciples, each according to his ability, decided to send relief to the believers living in Judea. They set amounts. They organized delivery. And when the gift finally traveled, it went in the hands of two trusted men, Barnabas and a young convert named Saul of Tarsus.

Which means the man writing to Corinth about planned generosity is not theorizing. Paul carried the church's first famine-relief fund down to Jerusalem with his own hands, and every mark of that operation shows up again in the collection he is organizing now. Warning turned into decision. Ability measured honestly. Relief moving before need became catastrophe. The early church simply assumed that love does logistics, that compassion worth anything gets a delivery date. So when Paul tells Corinth that giving should be decided beforehand and scaled to what a person actually has, he is handing them a tested pattern. He learned it as the courier. Now he teaches it as the architect.

Into the Word

We like to think the holiest giving is the most spontaneous kind, the tears, the impulse, the moment. Paul disagrees, gently and completely. Woven through his whole appeal (2 Corinthians 8:1-9:15) runs a conviction that generosity is decided, not felt into existence. And he grounds it in the strangest place imaginable, not in budgeting wisdom but in the character of God, whose greatest Gift was scheduled before the first sunrise. Start there, at the oldest decision on record.

If God's giving was decided in advance, His people's giving gets the same shape. Paul names it in the day's anchor, one word at the center of the verse.

Decided beforehand answers when. Paul's second principle answers how much, and he builds it from the example already marching ahead of Corinth. The Macedonians gave according to their ability and even beyond it (2 Corinthians 8:3), and Corinth is urged to finish its stalled project out of what it actually possesses (2 Corinthians 8:11). Then he compresses the whole principle into one freeing sentence.

Street Level

The List You Wrote While You Were Full

Everyone has run the experiment, usually by accident. Go to the grocery store hungry, with no list, and watch what happens. The store was built for exactly this version of you. The bakery vents near the front door, the endcap displays, the candy staged at the checkout where the line holds you still. You wander the aisles making forty small decisions in the worst possible condition to make them, and the cart fills up with a stranger's choices. At home, unpacking the bags, you discover you bought everything except the thing you came for. The list people never have that night. Somebody at a calm kitchen table, full and unhurried, already decided what this trip was for. In the store, they are not deciding anything. They are keeping a promise.

Paul is teaching Corinth to become list people. Their generosity had not died of stinginess. It died of no decision, a warm intention sent into the moment to fend for itself, and the moment always has its own endcaps, the bill that just arrived, the mood, the excuse, the sudden importance of a newer want. So he moves the deciding out of the moment altogether. Settled in the quiet. Measured against what is actually in the account. Finished before anything is asked. That is not generosity with the joy managed out of it. It is the opposite, because nobody feels cheerful making forty pressured choices in a rigged aisle, while a person keeping a promise made in the calm walks through the same aisle light. Write the list while you are full. You already know what the trip is for.

Today’s World

The Money Beat the Flood to the Village

For most of modern history, disaster relief has been a reaction. The water rises, the photos spread, the appeals go out, and help arrives weeks after the worst moment has passed. Along Bangladesh's Jamuna River, the United Nations now runs the sequence in reverse. Under an approach OCHA calls anticipatory action, the decisions are all made in the calm season. Forecast thresholds are agreed on, relief money is set aside in advance, and lists of the most vulnerable families are drawn up before the sky changes. When the river's forecast crosses the agreed line, the plan simply executes. In July 2024, as the Jamuna climbed toward a dangerous crest, 6.2 million dollars in cash and assistance reached vulnerable families ahead of the peak flooding, and the framework was renewed again for the 2025 monsoon season.

The evidence for deciding early keeps stacking up. When researchers evaluated the July 2020 activation on the same river, households paid before the water arrived were 36 percent less likely to go a whole day without eating during the flood, and they were more likely to have moved their livestock to safety and less likely to fall back on costly borrowing to survive the weeks that followed. Set that beside Antioch's famine fund and the echo is hard to miss. A warning taken seriously. A decision made in the calm. Relief traveling before the hunger peaks. It is the oldest church playbook in the book, now running on satellite forecasts, and it proves Paul's quiet claim at modern scale. The crisis is the worst possible time to start deciding, and generosity settled beforehand is not colder than the spontaneous kind. It is the kind that arrives in time.

Christ at the center

Nothing about the cross was improvised. The Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world in the purposes of God, which means Calvary was kept, not conceived, on that Friday. Trace the deliberateness through Jesus' own life. He spoke of His hour before it came. He set His face toward Jerusalem when every road out of Galilee was safer. In Gethsemane the decision was ratified in blood-sweat before the soldiers ever arrived, not this cup passing, but Your will be done. By the time the nails came, the choice was ancient. That is what today's word means at full scale. He decided in His heart beforehand, and no compulsion put Him there. No one takes My life from Me, He said. I lay it down of My own accord (John 10:18). So when Paul asks us to give as people who decided in advance, he is not teaching finance. He is teaching family resemblance. The cross was an appointment God kept. Planned grace is still grace. It is grace that shows up.

Application

Tonight, in the quiet, make the decision the moment always steals. Take five unhurried minutes with God and settle two things on paper. The proportion of your income you will give, decided against what you actually have, and the day it happens each month. Then remove the future vote, set the reminder, schedule the transfer, put the envelope where your keys live. You are not being rigid. You are being like the God who planned His Gift before the foundation of the world.

Discussion Question

"Is your giving decided or improvised (a standing plan you keep, whatever happens to be in your wallet when the plate passes, or a year-old good intention like Corinth's)? What one decision, made this week in the quiet and scaled honestly to what you have, would let your generosity survive your moods and your busiest months?"

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for planning us into Your heart before the world existed, and for a rescue that was never a rescue improvised. We confess that our good intentions have a short shelf life. We feel generous in the moment and forgetful by the weekend, and a year later our pledges look like Corinth's, sincere, dusty, and unfinished. Teach us Your kind of deliberateness. Help each of us decide in the quiet what we will give, in honest proportion to what You have actually placed in our hands, and then keep the decision the way You kept Yours. Guard us from regret giving and cornered giving, and give us instead the cheerfulness of people who already chose. Make our generosity so settled that it survives our moods, and so joyful that no one could mistake it for duty. In Jesus' name, Amen.
