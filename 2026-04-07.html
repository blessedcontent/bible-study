God Is Love

To Know GodLesson 2Day 4 of 7

In brief

John doesn't say God is loving. He says God is love, which means love isn't something God does when He feels like it; it is who He is at every level of His being.

  • ☩ The Bible's definition of love starts with God, not with us; our human experience of love is the copy, His is the original
  • ☩ God's love is not reactive; He loved us first, while we were still sinners, before we did anything to earn it or even wanted it
  • ☩ Yesterday's holiness and today's love are not in tension; God's love is holy love, pure and trustworthy precisely because it flows from a character with no corruption
  • ☩ The greatest proof of God's love is not a feeling or a concept but a Person: Jesus, sent into a world that didn't ask for Him, dying for people who didn't deserve it

Yesterday

Yesterday we explored how God's holiness is the attribute that makes every other attribute safe. Today we see why that matters so much: because God is love. And love, when it comes from a Being who is perfectly holy, is the most powerful force in the universe. A love backed by flawed character can be possessive, manipulative, or conditional. But a love backed by complete holiness? That love can be trusted absolutely. Today's study doesn't replace yesterday's; it builds on it. God's holiness is the foundation. His love is what He builds on it.

Historical Context

A Word the Greeks Didn't Have a Category For

The Greek language had an unusually rich vocabulary for love. Eros described romantic, passionate desire. Philia described the deep bond of friendship and loyalty. Storge described the natural affection between family members. Each word carved out a specific territory in the landscape of human relationships, and each was well understood in the Greco-Roman world. But when the early Christians needed a word for the love of God, none of these fit.

They reached for agape, a word that existed in Greek but was rarely used and had no strong cultural associations. It was, in a sense, an empty vessel waiting to be filled. And the early church filled it with a definition that had no parallel in any culture: love that is initiated by the lover regardless of the worthiness of the beloved, love that gives at cost to itself without expectation of return, love that persists even when rejected. The Hebrew Scriptures had already been building this concept through the word hesed, covenant love, which described God's loyal, steadfast, protective commitment to His people. Hesed wasn't a feeling; it was a binding decision backed by God's own character.

When John wrote "God is love," he was making a claim that no Greek philosopher would have understood and no pagan religion would have recognized. The gods of the ancient world could desire you, use you, or reward you. They did not agape you. That kind of love was a Jewish and Christian invention, because it was a description of a God no other tradition had ever encountered.

Into the Word

Today's anchor is 1 John 4:7-19, one of the most concentrated explorations of God's love in all of Scripture. John builds a complete theology of love in thirteen verses: where it comes from, how God proved it, what it drives out, and what it produces in those who receive it. We'll walk through the passage in two movements, then let Romans 5:8 and 1 Corinthians 13 add depth to the picture.

Street Level

The Hot Spring in January

You are hiking in January. The ground is frozen. Your breath hangs in the air. Every stream you've crossed has been locked under a sheet of ice. Then the trail drops into a valley and you see steam rising from a pool of water. You step closer and feel it: warmth, pouring off the surface in waves. A natural hot spring. The water is nearly scalding. You dip your hand in and the cold that has been pressing into your bones for hours just dissolves.

Here is what makes the spring remarkable. It is not warm because the sun is shining. It is not warm because the air around it is pleasant. The January air is brutal, and the spring does not care. Its heat comes from deep underground, from the earth's core, from a source so far below the surface that nothing happening at the top can cool it. The air can freeze. Snow can pile on the rocks around it. The spring just keeps pouring out heat, because heat is what it is, all the way down.

That is 1 John 4:8. Human love is more like a campfire: it needs fuel, it needs tending, it gutters in the wind, and sometimes it just goes out. You can love someone deeply on a Tuesday and feel nothing on a Thursday. Your love rises and falls with your energy, your mood, your sense of being loved in return. God's love is the hot spring. It does not fluctuate with conditions. It does not depend on your performance or your lovability. It flows from a source so deep in His nature that nothing you do, or fail to do, can turn it off. That is what John means when he says God is love. Not that He feels it. Not that He chooses it when it's convenient. He radiates it the way the earth radiates heat: constantly, inexhaustibly, from the core of who He is.

Today’s World

Your First Relationship Is Still Shaping Your Picture of God

A 2025 study published in Frontiers in Neurology confirmed what psychologists of religion have long suspected: a person's attachment style, the relational pattern formed in early childhood, directly shapes how they experience a connection to God. Individuals with secure attachment, those who grew up with caregivers who were consistently available and responsive, tend to perceive God as loving, present, and safe. Those with insecure attachment, whether anxious or avoidant, tend to perceive God as distant, unpredictable, or punishing, even when they intellectually believe He is loving.

The implications for today's study are significant. When John says people's picture of God "emerges from their human definition of love, which is always distorted and imperfect," modern psychology backs him up at the neurological level. Our brains literally wire themselves around our earliest relational experiences, and those wires don't automatically rewire when we hear a sermon about God's love. This is why John doesn't just tell us God is love and leave it there. He gives us proof: the cross, the sending of the Son, the initiative of a God who loved first. John is building a case strong enough to override the default settings our childhood installed. And the research suggests that is exactly what's needed.

Christ at the center

If you want to know what God's love actually looks like when it walks into a room, look at Jesus. He is the proof text of 1 John 4:9: "God sent his one and only Son into the world so that we might live through him." Jesus is agape incarnate. He healed people who couldn't pay Him back. He sought out the ones everyone else had given up on. He washed the feet of a man He knew would betray Him within hours. And when the full weight of human sin pressed down on Him in a garden and then on a cross, He didn't withdraw. He absorbed it.

The cross is not just a theological concept. It is the moment when God proved, with blood and breath and broken body, that His love is not a word. It is the most costly action ever taken in the history of the universe, taken by Someone who didn't owe us anything, for people who had done nothing to deserve it. "We love because he first loved us." Jesus is the "first." Everything else is response.

Application

Read 1 Corinthians 13:4-8 twice today. The first time, replace "love" with "God" and let the truth of His character wash over you. The second time, replace "love" with your own name and sit honestly with the result. Don't rush to fix the gap. Just notice it. Then ask God to do in you what only His love can do: slowly reshape your definition of love, and your capacity for it, from the inside out.

Discussion Question

"John says 'perfect love drives out fear.' Is there a specific fear in your relationship with God that you suspect might be rooted in a distorted picture of His love rather than in who He actually is?"

Closing Prayer

Father, I am beginning to see that my definition of love has been too small and too shaped by my own experience. Today Your Word tells me that love doesn't start with me; it starts with You. You loved first. You loved at cost. You loved while I was still turned away from You. I confess that fear still lives in places where Your perfect love has not yet fully reached, and I ask You to drive it out, not all at once if that's not how You work, but steadily, persistently, the way You do everything. Reshape my understanding of love from the ground up. Let it start with who You are, not with what I've experienced. And let it overflow into every relationship I touch today. In Jesus' name, Amen.
