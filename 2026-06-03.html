Sufficient Grace

Repentance and ForgivenessLesson 10Day 5 of 7

In brief

A man kneels in the cleft of a rock on the mountain his nation just betrayed God on, and the God they betrayed walks past him and says His own name in seven Hebrew words.

  • ☩Exodus 34:6 will be quoted by Psalm 86, Psalm 103, Psalm 145, Joel, Jonah, Nahum, and Nehemiah. The Bible's working definition of who God is comes from a chapter set inside the worst betrayal Israel ever committed.
  • ☩The three Hebrew words for sin we met two weeks ago (the missing-the-mark, the covenant-breach, the bent-and-twisted) all appear together in one verse here. God names every kind, then names the verb He uses on them.
  • ☩Paul's two nouns in Romans 6:23 do not balance on the scale. Opsonion, the soldier's daily wage paid in coins. Charisma, the gift no one earned and no one is paying back.
  • ☩The cross is the place these two chapters meet. The forgiveness Moses heard at Sinai and the love Paul names at Calvary are not two different mercies. They are one God in two pulpits.

Yesterday

Yesterday the inaugural verb turned our feet toward a door. Today we walk through it and find the room behind it has been furnished long before we arrived. The kindness Paul called the engine of repentance in Romans 2 was not a new idea Paul invented. It was the sentence God Himself had spoken to Moses on the mountain, fifteen hundred years earlier, after the worst betrayal Israel ever committed. The seven words the LORD used to name Himself in Exodus 34:6-7 became the Bible's most-quoted line about who God is. Tuesday's turn brings us to the cleft of a rock where the One we just turned toward has been waiting to walk past us saying His own name out loud.

Historical Context

The Sentence the Rest of the Old Testament Could Not Stop Quoting

Exodus 34 is the second time Moses goes up the mountain. The first time he came down with two tablets in his hands and a calf still smoking on an altar at the base. He smashed the tablets. The covenant document was now a pile of fragments in the dust at the foot of the mountain. The chapter we read today opens with God telling Moses to cut two new tablets out of the same stone and climb the mountain again, and the LORD will write on them the words that were on the first set. The covenant is being given a second time, in the same handwriting, to the same nation, after the worst betrayal that nation will ever commit. That is the setting in which the seven-word self-introduction is spoken.

The line in Exodus 34:6-7 became the Bible's quoting-line for who God is. Psalm 103:8 repeats it almost verbatim. Psalm 86:15 repeats it. Psalm 145 repeats it. Joel 2:13. Jonah 4:2 (where Jonah complains that God is too forgiving). Nahum 1:3. Nehemiah 9:17. The Bible's own canonical answer to the question "Who is the God of Israel?" is the sentence God spoke after the calf. And what makes the chapter even more astonishing is that the three Hebrew sin words Lesson 9's Sabbath taught us, the words for the wound that English has only one short word for (chattat missing the mark, pesha covenant breach, avon bent and twisted), all appear together in verse 7 as the three things God says He forgives. The whole cabinet of sin words and the verb of forgiveness in one breath. The same Voice that named the sins names the lifting of them.

Into the Word

Today's anchor is the most-quoted sentence in the Old Testament about who God is, spoken on the morning after the worst betrayal. We will sit with the seven Hebrew words inside the divine self-introduction, then walk into Paul's two-noun sentence at Romans 6:23 that gives the same news in market vocabulary, then land at Paul's most personally costly sentence at Romans 5:8 about when Christ died for us. The order matters. Wednesday is the day grace stops being an abstract noun and becomes the actual sound of God's own voice.

Street Level

The Fine That Wasn't

You find the books in a box in the garage during a move. Two of them. The local library's barcode stickers still on the spines. You had them out for what was supposed to be three weeks of summer reading. They have been in the box for six years.

On the drive to the library you do the math in your head. A quarter a day. Two books. Six years. You round generously and arrive at somewhere over a thousand dollars. You rehearse the conversation. You think about whether to apologize or just hand the books over and ask for the total. You picture the librarian's eyebrows climbing as the screen loads. By the time you pull into the parking lot your shoulders are up around your ears. You walk to the desk holding the books like you are carrying a bill.

The librarian scans them, looks at the screen for two seconds, then slides the books to the return cart. "We eliminated late fees in 2020," she says, the way a person says it for the fortieth time that month. "Welcome back. We're glad you found them." That is it. The wage you owed was very specific. You had done the math. The screen said zero. You stand there for a second too long, waiting for the catch, because every fiber of you was prepared for the bill to come due. There is no bill. There is only a librarian smiling at the next person in line.

Romans 6:23 is that empty screen, and the librarian was somebody Else. Opsonion is the math you did in the car. Charisma is the screen with no balance. The Cross is the line item that absorbed the thousand dollars on the other side of the system, so that when you walked up to the desk with the actual amount you owed, the only thing the desk had to say to you was your name and "welcome back."

Today’s World

The Order the Door Comes In

In February 2026 J-PAL North America, the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab affiliated with MIT, published an evidence brief synthesizing more than two decades of randomized-trial work on a homelessness program called Housing First. The model, developed in the early 1990s by Sam Tsemberis at Pathways to Housing in New York, asked a question the United States homeless services system had not asked. What happens if you give a person a key to an apartment before requiring sobriety, before requiring program completion, before requiring proof that they have earned it? The traditional "linear" approach treated housing as the prize at the end of a ladder. Housing First flipped the order. Key first, ladder optional, and everything else from inside the apartment instead of out on the street.

The data has now run across multiple randomized evaluations. In the Canadian At Home/Chez Soi multisite trial, the largest Housing First RCT to date, participants spent seventy-three percent of a two-year follow-up stably housed, compared with thirty-two percent for the treatment-as-usual controls. The J-PAL brief is careful to note that effects on downstream outcomes (emergency department use, hospitalizations, employment, substance use) have come back more mixed across studies, but the housing-stability finding itself is consistent and the one researchers feel confident standing on. The gift produced what the wage could not. People who were told "earn the housing" mostly never made it onto the ladder. People who were handed the key climbed the ladder anyway, just from inside the apartment.

Paul made the same announcement in Romans 6:23. The wage of sin is death. The gift of God is eternal life. The screen at the library was already at zero by the time you walked up to the desk; the apartment door was already unlocked by the time the resident climbed the stairs. Grace is not the prize at the end of the ladder. It is the floor that was put under us while we were still on the street.

Christ at the center

The verb under Exodus 34:7's "forgiving" is nose, "to lift" or "to carry." Isaiah picked it up seven centuries later in Isaiah 53:11: "By His knowledge My righteous Servant shall justify many, for He shall bear their iniquities." John the Baptist picked it up at the Jordan: "Behold the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world." The Greek verb John used (airon) is the New Testament's translation of nose. Same action, two languages, fifteen hundred years apart. The God who walked past Moses saying He was the One who lifts iniquity and transgression and sin was already on His way to do it by walking the lifting up a Roman hill in a body. Charisma, the gift Paul names in Romans 6, was not free in the sense that it cost nothing. It was free to us because someone else paid the opsonion. The wage was paid; the gift was offered. The seven words at Sinai and the four words at Calvary ("It is finished," tetelestai, the Greek receipt-stamp for "paid in full") are the same sentence in two pulpits.

Application

Write out the seven Hebrew descriptors from Exodus 34:6-7 on the back of yesterday's card: compassionate, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love, abounding in truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity / transgression / sin. Read them aloud once, slowly, in the order God said them. Then take one specific failure from this past week (the one Tuesday's Parakletos named, the one your "I am sorry" sentence said out loud) and put it under verse 7. Say one sentence over it: "This is the kind of sin God has named Himself the lifter of." Then close your eyes for thirty seconds and let the seven words be what God says about you today, not what you say about yourself.

Discussion Question

"Which of God's seven Hebrew self-descriptors are you having the hardest time believing about Him right now? (Compassionate, when life this year has felt like He has not been paying attention. Slow to anger, when you keep waiting for the other shoe to drop. Abounding in steadfast love, when a particular failure keeps replaying. Keeping mercy for thousands, when you feel like the special-case exception.) What would change about your Wednesday if you let the verse where God says His own name be louder than the verse you have been writing about Him this week?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we come to the cleft of the rock this morning as Moses did, with a nation full of broken tablets in our pockets and a calf-smoke smell still on our clothes. We have not deserved the seven words You spoke to him. We do not deserve them today. And the staggering news of this chapter is that You spoke them anyway, and You meant every one. We confess that we have been carrying the opsonion ledger in one hand and refusing the charisma in the other. Forgive us. Lift our every chattat, every pesha, every avon with the verb You named Yourself the lifter by. Let the cross be the place we finally believe that "while we were yet" means what it says. We did not climb up to You. You came down. We are not paying back the gift. We are receiving it. Make us a people whose lives quote Exodus 34:6 the way the rest of Your Book has been quoting it for three thousand years. We need You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
