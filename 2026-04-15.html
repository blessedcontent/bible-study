The Greatest Offense

Pride Versus HumilityLesson 3Day 5 of 7

In brief

On the night Jesus was preparing to die for them, His closest followers argued about which one of them was the greatest.

  • ☩ The disciples' argument about greatness happened at the Last Supper, hours before the cross, making the timing almost incomprehensible
  • ☩ Jesus redefined greatness entirely: in His kingdom, the greatest is the one who serves, not the one who is served
  • ☩ Pride is not just a personal weakness; Scripture calls it the sin most offensive to God and the most dangerous to the human soul
  • ☩ Proximity to Jesus does not automatically remove pride; we must actively ask God to diagnose and uproot it

Yesterday

Yesterday we saw humility embodied in a single life. Moses walked away from the most powerful position in the ancient world, was broken in the wilderness, and emerged as the most humble man on earth. His story shows what humility can produce when a person surrenders their self-sufficiency to God. Today we see the opposite: what happens when people who are close to God still refuse to let go of their pride. The disciples had spent years walking with Jesus, eating with Him, watching Him transform lives. And yet, on the very night He would give His life for them, they argued about which one of them deserved the highest rank.

Historical Context

The Argument That Happened at the Wrong Table

Luke places this dispute at the Last Supper. Think about the timing. Jesus has just instituted the communion meal. He has broken bread and said, "This is my body, which is given for you" (Luke 22:19). He has passed the cup and said, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). He has just announced that one of them will betray Him. The air in that upper room was thick with the weight of what was coming. And in the middle of all of it, the twelve men closest to Jesus started arguing about which one of them was the most important.

The Greek word Luke uses for their dispute is philoneikia, which literally means "love of strife" or "rivalry." This was not a philosophical debate about leadership theory. It was a status competition, driven by the same honor-shame impulse that governed Roman dinner seating and that Jesus had already addressed in Luke 14:7-11. The cultural context makes the scene even more damning: in the Roman patron-client system, the title euergetes ("benefactor") was awarded to wealthy men who funded public works, and it came with enormous social power. Jesus uses that exact word in His response: "Those in authority over them call themselves benefactors" (Luke 22:25). He is saying: the world's system of greatness runs on being served, being honored, being titled. My kingdom runs on the opposite.

Into the Word

Luke 22:24-27 captures one of the most sobering moments in the Gospels: the collision between human pride and divine humility at the very table where salvation was being inaugurated. Jesus' response does not merely correct the disciples' behavior. He redefines the concept of greatness itself, and then offers Himself as the living definition. Alongside this passage, Proverbs gives us the Old Testament verdict on what pride actually does in the sight of God.

Street Level

The Root-Bound Plant

There is a houseplant on your windowsill that looks perfectly healthy. The leaves are green. It gets plenty of light. You water it on schedule. By every visible measure, this plant is thriving. But it has stopped growing. It has not put out a new leaf in months. You cannot figure out why. Then one day you tip the pot sideways, slide it off, and see the problem. The roots have wrapped so tightly around themselves that they have formed a solid mass at the bottom of the pot. There is no room left. Every root that tries to reach outward hits another root circling back on itself. The plant is alive, but it is strangling itself from the inside, and you could not see any of it from the top.

That is what pride does to people who are close to Jesus. From the outside, everything looks right. They attend the right gatherings, say the right words, participate in the right activities. The leaves are green. But underneath, where nobody can see, the roots of self-interest have circled back on themselves so many times that there is no room for actual growth. The plant is not reaching outward; it is feeding on its own loops. And the most dangerous part is that the person cannot see it either. You have to pull the pot off to find root binding. It does not announce itself. It just quietly prevents everything above the soil from reaching its potential.

The disciples at the Last Supper were root-bound. Three years of proximity to Jesus, the best light any human being has ever received, and their root system was still circling back to the same question: which one of us is the greatest? They were green on top. They had healed the sick and cast out demons. But the hidden root mass was choking their ability to grow into what Jesus was calling them to become. Proximity to good soil does not fix root binding. The only solution is to take the plant out of the pot, break apart the root ball with your hands, and repot it with room to grow. It is uncomfortable. It looks violent. But without it, the plant stays locked in a container it has outgrown, circling back on itself forever. That is what God is offering to do with your pride this week, if you will let Him pull the pot off.

Today’s World

When Ego Sinks the Team

A 2026 study published in Frontiers in Psychology investigated how narcissistic leadership affects team performance, and the results illuminate exactly why the disciples' argument was so destructive. The researchers found that when team members score high in narcissistic rivalry, they interpret a leader's behavior as competition for status rather than an invitation to collaborate. The result: instead of working together, team members engage in "derogating others, competing for dominance, and other dysfunctional conflict behaviors." The team does not fail because the mission is unclear or the resources are insufficient. It fails because each member is privately asking the same question the disciples asked: "which of them should be considered the greatest?"

What makes this research so relevant is the finding that narcissistic conflict persists even when the leader models the opposite behavior. The team's ego dynamics can override the leader's example. That is precisely what happened in the upper room. Jesus, the most humble leader who has ever existed, was literally sitting at the table with them, and it was not enough to prevent the argument. Proximity to a humble leader does not automatically produce humility in followers. Something deeper has to change. The research confirms what Scripture has always said: pride is not an information problem. Knowing better does not fix it. It requires an internal transformation that only honesty before God, and the grace He gives to the humble, can accomplish.

Christ at the center

The most powerful sentence in this passage is the one Jesus says about Himself: "I am among you as one who serves." At the very moment the disciples were competing for the highest position, the King of the universe was occupying the lowest one. And within hours, He would descend even further: from servant to sacrifice, from the table to the cross, from "the one who serves" to the One who gives His life. Jesus did not just correct the disciples' understanding of greatness. He demonstrated it at the cost of everything. The greatest offense is not that we argue about rank. The greatest offense is that we argue about rank while the Son of God is kneeling to wash our feet (John 13:4-5).

Application

Pause and ask God a dangerous question: "Where is pride hiding in my heart that I cannot see?" Do not rush past the silence. If God brings something to mind, write it down. Then pray honestly: "Lord, take my heart, for I cannot give it. It is Yours. Keep it pure, for I cannot keep it for You. Save me in spite of myself. Mold me, fashion me, raise me into a place where Your love can flow through me instead of my pride."

Discussion Question

"Is there a situation in your life right now where you are technically 'close to Jesus' (attending church, reading your Bible, praying regularly) but still quietly competing for rank or recognition? What would genuine servanthood look like in that specific situation?"

Closing Prayer

Lord, we see ourselves in those disciples. We have walked with You, received from You, watched You work in our lives, and still our hearts compete for rank. We confess that proximity to You has not automatically removed our pride. We have sat at Your table and argued about who matters most. Forgive us. Pull the pot off our roots today and show us where self-interest has circled back on itself so tightly that there is no room left for growth. Break apart what needs breaking, even if it hurts, so that we can finally grow in the direction You intended. Teach us to be servants, not because servanthood is a strategy for advancement, but because the One we follow identified Himself as the One who serves. In Jesus' name, Amen.
