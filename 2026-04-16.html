Look at Him

Pride Versus HumilityLesson 3Day 6 of 7

In brief

The cure for pride is not trying harder to be humble; it is looking at Jesus long enough that everything else fades.

  • ☩ Philippians 2:3-8 traces the most radical descent in all of history: from equality with God to death on a cross, each step deliberately lower than the last
  • ☩ Jesus did not consider His divine status something to exploit; He emptied Himself, becoming a servant, a human, a sacrifice
  • ☩ When we truly see who He is and what He did, our own perceived greatness pales into complete insignificance
  • ☩ The only way to grow in our relationship with Jesus is to come to Him with humility, and humility grows when we look at Him

Yesterday

Yesterday we saw pride at its most alarming: the disciples arguing about rank at the very table where Jesus was inaugurating the new covenant. Jesus responded by declaring, "I am among you as one who serves." Today the week arrives at its destination. We have studied what pride grips (Sunday), how blind it makes us (Monday), what humility looks like in a human life (Tuesday), and how deeply pride offends God (Wednesday). Now we look at the Person who is the final answer to all of it. Philippians 2 is not just another passage in the study. It is the climax: the complete, breathtaking picture of what God Himself did with pride's opposite.

Historical Context

The Lowest Rung

When Paul wrote to the Philippians that Jesus humbled Himself "to death—even death on a cross," he was not adding a poetic flourish. He was naming the single most degrading form of execution the Roman Empire had devised. Crucifixion was reserved for slaves, pirates, and enemies of the state. Roman citizens were legally exempt from it. The victim was stripped naked, nailed or tied to a wooden beam, and displayed in a public place where passersby could mock them as they slowly suffocated over hours or even days. Cicero called it "the cruelest and most disgusting penalty." Seneca said it was better to commit suicide than to endure it. Jewish law added its own verdict: anyone hung on a tree was cursed by God (Deuteronomy 21:23).

For Paul's audience, the phrase "even death on a cross" functioned as a shock: it marked the absolute floor of the descent. There was nowhere lower to go. No form of death carried more shame, more public humiliation, more social erasure. And that is where the eternal God chose to stop descending. Not because He was forced, not because He ran out of options, but because there was no lower place left. The cross was not an accident that happened to Jesus. It was the destination He chose when He decided that saving humanity required going all the way to the bottom.

Into the Word

This is the passage the entire week has been building toward. Paul quotes what many scholars believe is an early Christian hymn, a piece of theology set to music that the first-century church already knew by heart. The hymn traces two movements: a deliberate descent from the highest place in the universe to the lowest, followed by an exaltation that vindicates every step of that descent. Alongside this, Jesus' own words in Luke 22:27 provide the personal statement that puts the hymn in His own voice. Together, these passages answer the week's central question: what does humility actually look like when God practices it?

The psalmist David understood this posture. In Psalm 138:6, he wrote: "Though the LORD is exalted, he takes note of the humble; but he knows the haughty from a distance." The God who descended in Philippians 2 is the same God who notices the humble and keeps the proud at arm's length. If you have time today, read all of Psalm 138 slowly. Write it out word for word. Let David's words of grateful humility wash over the pride this week has been exposing.

Street Level

The Rim of the Canyon

You drive for hours through flat, unremarkable desert to reach the Grand Canyon. The whole way there, the car is full of normal conversation: work stress, weekend plans, who said what at the meeting, the usual inventory of small concerns that fills our days. You park. You walk the paved path. You round the last bend. And then you see it. The ground drops away into a mile-deep chasm of layered rock, carved over millions of years by a river you can barely see at the bottom. The scale is so far beyond anything you expected that your brain takes a full five seconds to process what your eyes are reporting.

And in those five seconds, something interesting happens to the conversation. It stops. Nobody is talking about work anymore. Nobody is thinking about the meeting. The person who spent the entire drive rehearsing a grievance has completely forgotten it. Not because they decided to let it go, and not because someone told them to be more grateful. They forgot it because they are standing in front of something so immense that their personal inventory of complaints simply lost its gravitational pull. The canyon did not argue with their problems. It did not minimize them. It just made them irrelevant by being bigger.

That is how Philippians 2 cures pride. Paul does not say "stop being proud" or "try harder to be humble." He says: look at Him. Look at the One who was in the form of God and did not consider equality with God something to be exploited. Look at the One who emptied Himself. Look at the descent from deity to servant to human to death to cross. Stand at the rim of that and try to keep talking about your promotion, your reputation, your ranking among peers. You cannot. Not because you have mustered the willpower to be modest, but because you are standing in front of something so vast that self simply fades. The cure for pride has never been a mirror. It has always been a window, and on the other side of that window is Jesus, descending all the way down for you. Stand at that rim long enough, and your pride will not need to be argued away. It will just quietly lose its voice.

Today’s World

The Emotion That Changes What You Do Next

Psychologists have documented an emotion they call "moral elevation," and the research, published across multiple studies in journals like the Journal of Positive Psychology and PLOS One, confirms something remarkable: simply witnessing someone else's act of moral goodness changes your own behavior. When people observe genuine virtue, sacrifice, or selflessness, they experience a measurable physical response: warmth in the chest, a lump in the throat, tears in the eyes. More importantly, they become more generous, more prosocial, and more willing to help others. The researchers found that moral elevation is contagious: seeing goodness makes you want to be good.

The parallels to Philippians 2 are striking. Paul does not begin by commanding humility in the abstract. He says, "Look at Jesus." He narrates the descent: from God to servant to human to cross. And the expected response is not intellectual agreement but transformation. When you truly see what Christ did, something changes inside you. The research calls it "moral elevation." Scripture calls it being changed "from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18). The principle is the same: you become like what you gaze at. If you spend your life looking at status, achievement, and comparison, you will become proud. If you spend your life looking at the One who emptied Himself, you will become humble. The cure for pride has never been willpower. It has always been wonder.

Christ at the center

This is the week's destination. Every day has been pointing here. Sunday showed us what pride grips; Christ let go of everything. Monday showed us pride's blindness; Christ is the light that reveals our true condition. Tuesday showed us Moses' humility forged in the wilderness; Christ chose a wilderness that led to a cross. Wednesday showed us the disciples arguing about rank; Christ was among them as the One who serves. And today we see the complete picture: the eternal God, existing in the form of deity, choosing to descend step by step, from glory to servanthood to humanity to obedience to death to the cross, the lowest rung of the lowest ladder. He bore it all. He did it all. Not because He had to, but because He loved us enough to go all the way to the bottom so that we would never have to stay there. The response to Philippians 2 is not a list of things to do. It is a Person to look at, long enough and honestly enough that pride loses its grip and humility takes its place.

Application

Find somewhere quiet today. Open your Bible to Psalm 138 and write it out by hand, word for word. Do not rush. As you write, pay attention to which words stop you. Then read Philippians 2:5-11 one more time, slowly. Ask yourself: what would change in my life if I truly adopted this attitude? Write down one honest answer.

Discussion Question

"After reading Philippians 2:3-8, what is one area of your life where you have been 'ascending' (seeking recognition, control, or status) that you sense God is calling you to reverse direction on?"

Closing Prayer

Jesus, we have spent this week studying pride, and today we look at You. We see You in the form of God, choosing not to hold onto what was rightfully Yours. We see You emptying Yourself, taking the form of a servant, becoming one of us, and descending to the cross. We stand at the rim of that descent and our pride loses its voice. We do not want to climb anymore. We want to follow You downward, into the kind of humility that serves without keeping score and gives without needing recognition. Change the direction of our hearts today. Where we have been ascending, teach us to kneel. Where we have been grasping, open our hands. Where we have been performing, make us real. We do not ask for the strength to be humble on our own; we ask for eyes to see You so clearly that self simply fades. In Your name, Amen.
