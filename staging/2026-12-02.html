Organizational Authority

Prophetic Authority and RelevanceLesson 10Day 5 of 7

In brief

People have sold land so their neighbors can eat. The money has been laid at the apostles' feet. Yet as the church in Jerusalem grows, a complaint comes from Greek-speaking believers whose widows are being missed when food is handed out. The apostles call everybody together. By the time the meeting is over, everyone knows which seven people will take responsibility. Care becomes dependable when someone answers for the person who was overlooked.

Yesterday

A jailer set a meal before two men he had kept in stocks. Hearing about Jesus changed whom he made room for at home. Feeding one household is one thing. How does a growing church make room for people a few of its members may never meet?

Historical Context

A Family Remembered Alexandria

In a tomb on Mount Scopus near Jerusalem stood a stone burial box. Someone cut a few words into its side in Greek. They remember a man from Alexandria in Egypt who made gates. Here was a family with ties across the sea. Greek was a language they used even to remember their dead. In Acts, the believers who brought the widows' complaint and those who heard it belonged to the same Jewish community. They spoke more than one language.

The believers had already brought gifts to the apostles to share with anyone in need (Acts 4:32-35). As the group grew, some Greek-speaking widows were being passed over. Luke tells us who felt the loss and leaves its cause open. Imagine being there as someone who had sold a field to help. Had your gift reached her?

Into the Word

Someone who sold a field may wonder where the gift went. A widow missing her meal needs her share today. What would a faithful use of authority look like from her place in the room?

Hosea remembers Moses as the prophet through whom God brought Israel out of Egypt and cared for His people (Hosea 12:13). When each household needed to know which day to choose a Passover lamb, God gave Moses and Aaron the words to tell them (Exodus 12:1-3). Now picture all those households bringing their disputes to one man. Moses himself could see what that would mean for the person at the back of the line.

Deborah heard disputes under a palm tree. When Barak was needed to lead Israel into battle, she sent for him (Judges 4:4-8). Centuries later another army drew near, but this time a king already stood among the people. It was Jahaziel who spoke the word God gave them about the battle (2 Chronicles 20:14-17). Even a king could need to stand and listen while somebody else spoke.

When Paul told Titus to appoint elders in Crete, each town would have people charged with caring for a church (Titus 1:5). What sort of judgment would that take? In Corinth the church was proud while a man lived openly with his father's wife. Paul called for grief and action (1 Corinthians 5:1-5). To a believer whose spouse didn't share the faith, he gave different counsel. If the spouse wanted to stay, they could go on with their marriage. If the spouse chose to leave, they could part in peace (1 Corinthians 7:10-16). Knowing one stern answer still leaves us the work of listening to the next person.

Street Level

Her Turn Survived the Bell

The school bell rings just as a girl steps up to kick. All recess she's stood in line behind the same five kids. Now the ball goes under somebody's arm and they all head inside. The next day the same two teams meet at the backstop. The boy who usually starts rolls up his sleeves and takes his place at the front. By the time recess ends, the girl has gone two days without ever touching the ball.

On the third day one of the other kids sees her walk straight past the field. They ask where she's going and hear what happened. One boy tears a page from his pocket notebook. The two teams put their kicking orders on it. When the bell rings, somebody circles the next name on each side, and they pick one kid to keep the page until tomorrow. There's still only one ball and the bell still rings too soon. But a turn can survive overnight now. When the girl comes back, someone is ready to call her name.

In Jerusalem the widows had missed meals. The people who heard their complaint chose seven neighbors they trusted to see that the food got through. At recess the other kids had always been glad to see the girl. Now when the bell rings, one of them has the job of remembering whose turn comes next.

Christ at the center

John tells us what Jesus knew when He got up from supper. The Father had placed everything in His hands. Then John tells us what those hands did. Jesus poured water and washed His disciples' feet (John 13:3-5). At the table again He accepted the names Teacher and Lord. They were right to call Him that, He said, and His example gave them a reason to care for one another (John 13:12-15). The same Lord went on to give His life to set others free (Mark 10:42-45). A church can afford to listen when somebody says she has been overlooked. The One at its head has already knelt at His friends' feet.

Application

Think of one shared task you have a say in. Follow it in your mind all the way from the person who plans it to the person it should reach. Where could someone be missed, and who would notice? Start by naming that person or that gap.

Discussion Question

What did the Twelve do that gave the widows' complaint weight, and how could someone with even a little responsibility make it easier for an overlooked person to be heard?

Closing Prayer

Father, give us ears for the person who has already tried to tell us something is wrong. If we have helped in one way, keep us open to hearing what still needs doing. Give wisdom to those who teach and to those who see that a meal reaches the right hands. Thank You for the Son who got up from the table to serve His friends. Teach us to receive that kindness and to make it dependable for somebody else. In Jesus' name, Amen.
