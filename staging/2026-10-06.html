Isaiah, the Gospel Prophet

The Call of a ProphetLesson 2Day 4 of 7

In brief

A scroll lies open to a page written centuries before the cross. It describes a Man despised, pierced, and silent as a lamb, carrying wrongs that were not His. The prophet who wrote it once saw God's throne room in a vision and was sure he was ruined, until a coal from the altar touched his lips and his guilt was gone. Isaiah preached a Substitute as a man who had already received a mercy he did not earn.

Yesterday

Yesterday fire fell on a soaked altar, and a limping nation fell on its face. Elijah had prayed for turned hearts. More than a century later another prophet stood near an altar, and this time the fire touched him. Today Isaiah tells what he saw.

Historical Context

The Ink Was Dry Before Bethlehem

In early 1947, Bedouin shepherds climbed into a cave near the Dead Sea and found clay jars holding leather scrolls. One of them was the book of Isaiah, all sixty-six chapters, sewn from seventeen sheets into a roll about twenty-four feet long. It is kept at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. The museum dates this copy to about 125 BC, more than a century before Jesus was born.

Unroll it far enough and you reach the lines we call Isaiah 53. The scroll has variant spellings and copyist slips, but its text generally agrees with the traditional Hebrew behind modern Bibles. That matters today. People may debate exactly when the prophecy was first spoken. No one can say it was written after the cross. The portrait of a pierced and silent Servant was already on leather, ink long dry, before anyone in Bethlehem had heard the name Jesus.

Into the Word

How does a person become able to write Isaiah 53? Not in a library. Isaiah tells about the worst and best minute of his life, in the year King Uzziah died. In a vision he saw the Lord enthroned in the temple, and it was as if the ceiling came off. His book sets this scene long before we reach the Servant, so start where he started.

The assignment was hard. Isaiah was sent to people who called evil good and good evil (Isaiah 5:20), to warn them where that road ends. Paul would later say it in one line. The wages of sin is death (Romans 6:23). Yet Isaiah also saw a far horizon, with nations hammering swords into plowshares (Isaiah 2:4). How do you get from a guilty people to a healed world? The answer is a Person, and Isaiah draws His portrait in detail (Isaiah 53).

Street Level

The Account Was in His Name

Imagine four roommates renting an old house near campus. When they moved in, the power company wanted one name on the account, and one of them said, put it in mine. He knew what that meant. If the bill went unpaid, the company would come after him and nobody else.

Nobody set out to cheat him. One roommate had a short month. One bought concert tickets and meant to catch up. One simply forgot, twice. Each skipped share was small, and each had a reason. But unpaid shares do not stay scattered. They all went to the same place, and by winter a shutoff notice came with one name on it. He never called a house meeting. The lights stayed on. About that time his guitar disappeared from the corner of the living room, and the others figured he had lost interest in playing.

In the spring one of them digs through the kitchen drawer for batteries and finds the notice. Four months past due. Under it is a receipt, paid in full, dated the week the guitar went missing. They had read his loss as his own business. It was theirs. Every dollar was real, none of it was his, and he paid without a word because his name was where it all landed. Isaiah saw that on a scale no one can measure. Each of us turned to our own way, and the LORD laid all of it on One who had offered Himself for exactly that, and who did not open His mouth. The roommates had been warm all winter on his guitar.

Today’s World

He Walked In So She Could Walk Out

On March 23, 2018, an armed attacker holed up inside a supermarket in Trèbes, a town in southern France. Arnaud Beltrame was 44, a lieutenant colonel in the gendarmerie, France's military police. He was sent to coordinate the response. One hostage was still inside, a young woman. Beltrame offered to take her place, and the exchange was made.

He left his phone on so the officers outside could follow what was happening. After new gunshots, a tactical unit stormed the building. Beltrame had been gravely wounded, and he died during the night. In March 2026 the gendarmerie honored him again, in a courtyard that now carries his name. Its notice says he saved a young woman's life at the cost of his own.

The likeness has limits. The woman in that store was guilty of nothing, and Beltrame, brave as he was, was a man like the rest of us. Isaiah describes One with no sin of His own who took the place of people who had plenty. Still, one fact from Trèbes helps the old words land. Someone was going to be held in that store. He decided it would be him.

Christ at the center

When the risen Jesus walked with two discouraged disciples, He began with Moses and all the Prophets and explained what was written about Himself (Luke 24:27). Luke does not list the passages. Acts does name one. An Ethiopian official sat in his chariot puzzling over lines from Isaiah 53, and Philip began with that Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus (Acts 8:32-35). The Gospels show the details arriving one at a time. Silent before His judges. Pierced. Counted with criminals. Laid in a rich man's tomb. This is what the prophetic gift is for. It does not exist to satisfy curiosity about the future. It was given so that when the Lamb came, anyone willing could recognize Him. Seven centuries early, God let a forgiven man describe the price of his own forgiveness.

Application

Take two minutes with Isaiah 53:6. Read it slowly, putting your own name in both places where it says all. Then stay for a moment with the last line. You do not need to feel anything in particular. If something specific weighs on you, tell Him, and remember where this verse says it landed.

Discussion Question

Isaiah was forgiven before he was sent, and his book goes on to describe the One who paid for it. Which is harder to believe, that your guilt is removed or that God could use your voice afterward (think of a recent regret)? Why?

Closing Prayer

Holy God, we could not have stood in that temple either. Our lips are no cleaner than Isaiah's. Thank You for the coal, and thank You for the altar it came from. Thank You for Your Servant, who was despised while we looked away. He carried what was ours, and He did not open His mouth. We have each turned to our own way. Thank You that every one of those roads met in Him. Let that mercy loosen our tongues, and make us glad to say here am I because of what He has already done. In Jesus' name, Amen.
