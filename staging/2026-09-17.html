Appeal to the Unrepentant

Dealing With False TeachersLesson 12Day 6 of 7

In brief

A father is packing for a third trip home, and he is afraid of what he will find. Not the strangers this time. His own children, still quarreling, still gossiping, still refusing to turn from sexual sin, two letters after he begged them to stop. Paul gives notice that this visit comes with authority, and then he prays he will never have to use it. Then he hands them one exam question. Is Christ in you? The test was never meant to condemn them. It was meant to prove them real, and a real coin rings true.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul played the boasting game for one paragraph and won it with a list of scars, five floggings, three shipwrecks, a night in the sea. Then he threw the trophy away, because a thorn had taught him that Christ's power lands in weakness. Today the same weak man announces that he is coming back, and this time he will not spare anyone.

Historical Context

Athens Paid a Man to Test Every Coin in the Market

Four centuries before Paul, the city of Athens passed a law that tells you how seriously the Greek world took counterfeits. It hired a public coin tester and sat him in the marketplace with a set of scales and a cutting tool. Anyone could bring him a silver coin. If it rang true, he handed it back approved, and by law the seller had to accept it. If it was a plated fake, he cut a gash across its face on the spot and confiscated it. The tester's whole job was to make honest trade possible, because a market where nobody trusted the money was a market where nothing moved. Corinth, a trading city with its own mint, lived by the same rule. Every coin was a question until somebody tested it.

Paul reaches for that world when he tells the church to examine themselves. The verb he chooses is the coin tester's verb, the word for testing something in order to approve it, and it changes the mood of the whole chapter. He is not setting a trap. He expects the coin to ring true. He also brings a second piece of ancient procedure with him, the rule from the law of Moses that no charge stands without two or three witnesses. His two earlier visits have already testified. The third one will settle the matter. A city that cut fakes in half in the marketplace understood exactly what Paul meant, and it understood the mercy in it too. The tester wanted to hand the coin back.

Into the Word

Read the whole stretch from 2 Corinthians 12:14-13:10 and you can hear a suitcase being packed. Paul is coming a third time, and he says the thing every parent says, I am not after your money, I am after you (2 Corinthians 12:14). Everything he has written, all the defending and boasting, has been for one purpose, to build them up (2 Corinthians 12:19). But building sometimes requires demolition, and today's question is what love does when it comes home and finds the same sins still living in the house.

So he gives notice, the way a parent does before the car pulls in. The man who opened this section with the meekness of Christ (2 Corinthians 10:1) said then that he would be ready to deal with every disobedience once the church's own obedience was complete (2 Corinthians 10:6). The time has come.

You would expect a man with that kind of authority to pray for a clean win. Paul prays the opposite. He asks God that they will do what is right even if it makes him look like he failed, because their restoration is the whole point (2 Corinthians 13:7-9). Then he hands them the exam.

Street Level

The Inspector Came Back to Hang an A

Anyone who has worked in a kitchen knows the feeling when the health inspector walks in with the clipboard. She has real authority. If she finds the wrong thing, she can close the doors that afternoon, and she would rather do almost anything else. Closing restaurants is not why she took the job. So the first visit ends with a list, not a padlock. The thermometer nobody uses. The towel draped over the sink. The raw chicken stored on the shelf above the lettuce. Small things, the kind a busy crew stops noticing, and every one of them on the same sheet as the big ones. Then she gives you a date. "I will be back in thirty days."

The thirty days are the whole point. The return visit is not a trap. It is a test built to be passed, and everybody knows what it is checking, because she wrote it down. She is not coming back to admire the new sign or the fresh paint. She is coming to see what the kitchen has stopped doing. The towel gone. The thermometer in use. The chicken on the bottom shelf. If the changes are real, she signs the form and hangs the letter in the window, and the whole neighborhood can read that this place was examined and approved. The closure order stayed in her folder the entire time. It never had to come out.

Paul is packing for the return visit. He has the authority, he has given the date, and his prayer is that he never has to use it. The exam he hands Corinth is built the same way. It is meant to approve, it comes with the answer already on it, Christ in you, and it is graded by what has stopped. The gossip, the rivalry, the sin nobody flagged. When those go, the grade goes in the window.

Today’s World

The Rudeness Nobody Wrote Up Still Ran the Bill

The Society for Human Resource Management is a professional association for people who run hiring and workplace policy. It tracks rudeness in everyday life and on the job with a quarterly survey called the Civility Index. Its Q3 2025 report, drawn from 1,478 U.S. workers surveyed in July 2025, found that 74 percent had experienced or witnessed uncivil behavior in everyday life during the month before. Of those, 51 percent said at least one incident they saw happened at work. The count includes acts a worker witnessed, not only ones aimed at them.

By the association's own estimate, workplace incivility costs American organizations about 2.1 billion dollars every day in lower productivity and missed work. Workers who ran into it said they deliberately stayed home an average of 1.7 days that month. The report's most telling numbers are about tolerance. Thirty-five percent of workers said their manager ignored acts of incivility, and 44 percent said their manager could have done more to prevent it. The association concludes that the scores show a continued need "to curb incivility before it escalates to more harmful levels." Small rudeness that no one corrects does not stay small. It escalates, it costs, and it keeps people home.

A workplace survey is not a verdict on a church, and its authors were not reading 2 Corinthians. But the pattern they measure is the one Paul feared. His first list was not the scandalous sins. It was gossip, rivalry, and quarreling, the respectable rudeness a community learns to live with. He treated those as seriously as the sins nobody excuses, because he knew what small disorder grows into when a church looks away. The exam he handed Corinth was built to catch it early, while turning was still the easy part.

Christ at the center

The unrepentant in Corinth did not get an execution notice. They got an appeal, and the appeal came from a man copying his Lord. Jesus is the One who was crucified in weakness and lives by the power of God, and He is also the One who is coming back, with authority. Yet the Judge who is coming is the same Jesus who took the verdict first. He was cut like a counterfeit so that counterfeits could be made genuine. That is why Paul can hand the church an exam with the answer already written on it. Christ is in you. Not because they had been good, but because He had been given. The power that leads to turning from sin is not willpower. It is the risen life of Jesus inside a person, the same power that walked out of the tomb. So the test is not a threat hanging over believers. It is a promise being checked. If He is in you, it will show.

Application

Take the eight words from 2 Corinthians 12:20 (quarreling, jealousy, rage, rivalry, slander, gossip, arrogance, disorder) and read them as a checklist of your own last week, not somebody else's. Circle one. Before the day ends, do what repentance does with it. Apologize to the person, delete the thread, give back the credit you took, or simply confess it to God by name. Then say out loud the answer Paul already gave you, "Christ is in me," and go act as if it is true, because it is.

Discussion Question

Which sins on Paul's first list would people in your church treat as respectable (gossip, rivalry, quarreling), and which would get someone disciplined? If Paul puts them all in one paragraph and expects Christ's power to turn us from every item, what would an honest self-examination look like for you, and who could you trust to help you do it?

Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are coming back, and we want to be found as You wish to find us. We confess that some of the sins on Paul's list have become so ordinary in us that we stopped calling them sin, the quarreling we call conviction, the gossip we call concern, the rivalry we call ambition. Examine us. Test us, and let the test prove You are in us, because we have no other hope of passing it. Where we have not turned from what we know is wrong, turn us today by Your power, the same power that raised You from the dead. Give our churches the courage to correct one another the way Paul did, with authority held in reserve and restoration as the whole aim, and make us people who pray for each other's wholeness more than for our own vindication. Ring true in us, Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.
