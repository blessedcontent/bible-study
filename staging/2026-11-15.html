The Bible, Its Own Interpreter

Interpreting Prophetic WritingsLesson 8Day 2 of 7

In brief

On a hillside in Galilee, Jesus quotes sayings from the Law His listeners already know and then says, But I tell you. Do not murder now reaches anger. Do not commit adultery now reaches the lustful look. It could sound like a new teacher clearing away the old Scriptures. Jesus says the opposite. Jesus came to fulfill the Law and the Prophets, not to tear them down, and the Bible keeps explaining itself, one part shedding light on another.

Yesterday

Yesterday, on the road to Emmaus, the risen Jesus walked with two sad followers and explained the Scriptures. They learned that their own Bible had been about Him all along. Today goes back to a hillside in Galilee. There Jesus shows how one part of Scripture explains another.

Historical Context

The King's Experts Needed the Dream First

When Nebuchadnezzar woke from a troubling dream, he called in his magicians and astrologers (Daniel 2). Mesopotamian scholars had handbooks for this kind of work. One long dream handbook survives in copies from the Assyrian royal library at Nineveh, gathered a few generations before Daniel. Its entries follow a simple pattern. If a man dreams this, then that will happen.

A handbook like that is useless until you know the dream. So the king's experts asked him to tell it to them, and he refused. Tell me the dream, he said, and then I will know you can explain it. They answered that no one on earth could do such a thing. Daniel's answer started somewhere else. What the king's wise men could not do, the God of heaven had done, because He reveals what is hidden (Daniel 2:27-28). The dream and its meaning came from the same God.

Into the Word

Picture the crowd on the hillside as Jesus teaches. He is about to set six familiar sayings beside His own words, each time adding, But I tell you (Matthew 5:21-48). A listener could easily take that as a new teacher clearing away the old Scriptures. So before the first one, Jesus tells them how to hear Him.

A command that reaches anger and desire has to be kept somewhere deeper than a stone tablet. Centuries earlier, Jeremiah heard God promise that very thing.

Paul says all Scripture is God-breathed (2 Timothy 3:16). With one God speaking through many writers, one passage can help explain another. Sometimes the key sits in the same chapter. Daniel describes the king's dream and then gives its meaning (Daniel 2:31-35; Daniel 2:36-45). In Matthew 13, Jesus tells a story about a farmer sowing seed and later explains it (Matthew 13:1-9; Matthew 13:18-23). Sometimes the key comes later, from another writer looking back.

Street Level

Not One Wall Came Down

For eleven years the little house at the end of the street had three odd features. A pipe stuck out of the kitchen wall, capped off and going nowhere. The hallway ended at a blank wall, right where you would expect a door. And out back, the concrete foundation ran about twelve feet past the house. The family used it as a patio and kept the grill there. They loved that house, and they teased the builder about its odd spots whenever he stopped by.

Then they saved enough for two more bedrooms and a bathroom, and they called the same builder. The neighbors expected a wrecking crew. Instead he cut away one sheet of drywall at the end of the hall, and there was a doorway, framed eleven years before. The new rooms went up on the patio, which turned out to be exactly their size. The capped pipe carried water to the new bathroom. Not one wall came down. Every odd thing about that house had been waiting for this.

That is how Jesus asks to be heard when He says, But I tell you. It can sound like the wrecking crew has arrived. He says He did not come to tear down the Law or the Prophets but to finish what they were built for. The command against murder had always reached past the act to the anger underneath, the way that slab reached past the back wall. Promises in the Prophets waited like that capped pipe. The same God gave the first words and the later ones, so the later can explain the earlier. The doorway at the end of the hall was there the day they moved in.

Christ at the center

Matthew 5:20 sets a bar that should stop us. Our righteousness has to go deeper than that of the scribes and Pharisees, all the way down to anger and desire. No one clears that bar by trying harder. But the Law and the Prophets never pointed only to a higher bar. Paul says they also bear witness to a righteousness that comes from God as a gift, through faith in Jesus Christ, to all who believe (Romans 3:21-22). Jeremiah's promise is the other half. The God who asks for a clean heart promised to write His law on it. Jesus fulfilled the Law and the Prophets in His own life, and He gives what He fulfilled. The bar was never lowered. It was met.

Application

Take two minutes with Matthew 5:21-22. Ask where that command reaches deeper in you than the outward rule. Then thank God that He promised to write His law on the heart, and ask Him to begin there today.

Discussion Question

Jesus took do not murder down to anger. Why is it easier to keep God's commands only where others can see? Which command do you tend to keep on the surface (about anger, honesty, or desire)?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that Your word holds together, one part explaining another, because You stand behind all of it. Forgive us for keeping Your commands on the surface, where others can see. Write Your law on our hearts, as You promised through Jeremiah. Where anger or desire has taken root, reach that deep. And when Your standard is higher than we can climb, remind us of the righteousness You give through faith in Jesus. In Jesus' name, Amen.
