Biblical Exposition

Interpreting Prophetic WritingsLesson 8Day 6 of 7

In brief

In His hometown synagogue, Jesus stands up and reads from Isaiah. He rolls up the scroll and sits down, and every eye is on Him. Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing, He says. After He rises, He opens His followers' minds to understand the Scriptures. Later Philip starts from Isaiah and tells a traveler about Jesus. Faithful Bible teaching opens the text so that people meet Jesus there, and the risen Lord still opens minds to understand.

Yesterday

Yesterday Jesus asked an expert in the law, How do you read it? Setting Matthew beside Luke showed that perfect means a mercy that reaches enemies. Today Jesus Himself reads and explains the Scriptures. Then He sends others to do the same.

Historical Context

The Synagogue Was Built for Reading

In 1913, archaeologists working in the oldest part of Jerusalem found a stone plaque inscribed in Greek. Most scholars date it before the Romans destroyed the city in AD 70. It says that a priest named Theodotus, a synagogue leader like his father and grandfather, built the synagogue for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments. He added rooms and water for visitors from abroad.

Reading and teaching were the center of a synagogue gathering. In one service Luke describes, the leaders invited visitors to speak after the reading from the Law and the Prophets (Acts 13:14-15). In Luke 4, Jesus is in the synagogue at Nazareth, where He grew up. He stands to read and then sits down to teach, as teachers did. That Sabbath the room followed its usual pattern, until the Reader began to speak.

Into the Word

Jesus did not need a scroll to speak with authority. The crowds noticed that He taught like no one else (Matthew 7:28-29). Yet in His hometown synagogue, He was handed the scroll of Isaiah, and He read from it. What happens when the One the Scriptures speak about reads them aloud?

Nazareth was not unusual. From the start, Jesus' preaching leaned on Scripture. He announced that the time was fulfilled and the kingdom of God was near (Mark 1:14-15). Mark does not say which prophecy He had in mind, though many readers hear Daniel's seventy weeks behind those words, a set span of time leading to an Anointed One (Daniel 9:24-27). On the mountain He quoted the commandments against murder and adultery (Exodus 20:13-14; Matthew 5:21; Matthew 5:27), and the whole sermon kept returning to Scripture (Matthew 5-7, chapters 5, 6, and 7). After His resurrection, He did it again.

Street Level

She Kept One Finger on the Page

A man on his first bird walk has had his field guide open to the same page for ten minutes. Small and brown, it says, with a white stripe over the eye, singing loudly from a low perch. He has read it four times, and every bird in the hedge still looks brown. The woman leading the walk comes and stands beside him. She leaves the book in his hands, keeps one finger on the picture, and points with her other hand. Third fence post. Find it with your eyes first, then bring the binoculars up.

Then he has it. A small brown bird on the post, a white stripe over its eye, its whole body shaking with the song. He looks back down at the page, and every line on it now describes something he has seen. The page had been right the whole time. He needed someone to bring his eyes from the description to the bird. She could point. She could not see it for him. By the time he lowers the binoculars, she is a little way down the trail.

Faithful Bible teaching works like that. It never asks us to put the page down, and it never stops at the page. It keeps a finger on the text and points to the One the text describes, until people see Him. In Nazareth the distance from page to Person was nothing at all. The One Isaiah described was holding the scroll. Since then, faithful teachers have done what she did on the trail. They point, step back, and go find the next person still staring at the page.

Christ at the center

An official from Ethiopia had gone to Jerusalem to worship (Acts 8:27). On the road home he was reading Isaiah aloud, a passage about someone led like a sheep to the slaughter, and he could not tell whom it was about. Philip began with that very Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus (Acts 8:35-39). The man was baptized and went on his way rejoicing, with the joy David once asked God to restore (Psalm 51:12). Read the whole of Acts 8 and you can see what exposition is for.

Jesus does more than explain the truth. He is the truth (John 14:6), and the Father's word is truth (John 17:17). The risen Lord sent His followers to teach everything He had commanded and promised to be with them to the end of the age (Matthew 28:20). Every faithful exposition ends where Philip's did, with Jesus.

Application

Jesus said we live on every word that comes from the mouth of God (Matthew 4:4). Take two minutes with one short passage, perhaps Isaiah 61:1-2. Ask the Spirit to guide you into its truth (John 16:13), then ask one question. What does this show me about Jesus?

Discussion Question

The traveler had Isaiah in his hands and still asked how he could understand without someone to guide him. Why do we need one another to read the Bible well? Who has helped you understand Scripture (a teacher, a friend, or a stranger)?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that Your Son opened the Scriptures and opened minds to understand them. Open ours. When we read, guide us by Your Spirit into the truth, and keep us from reading only to win arguments. Give us humility to learn from others and courage to share what we have understood. Let every passage we explain point people to Jesus, the way Philip did on that desert road. In Jesus' name, Amen.
