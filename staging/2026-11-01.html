The Call to Write

The Prophetic WritingsLesson 6Day 2 of 7

In brief

In the wilderness, raiders strike Israel's slowest walkers at the back of the line, and God tells Moses to put His answer on a scroll. Centuries later He tells Jeremiah to put years of warnings on one scroll, with a reason attached. Perhaps they will turn. God called His messengers to write because He was after rescue. The perhaps belonged to the hearers. On God's side stood forgiveness.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul told believers in Rome that the old Scriptures were written to give them hope. He even quoted the end of the song Moses wrote down. Today goes back to God's first command to write. Why did He want His words on a page?

Historical Context

Babylon Won the Year the Scroll Was Ordered

The order to write everything down came in the fourth year of Jehoiakim, king of Judah (Jeremiah 36:1-2). Jeremiah dates another event to that same year. At Carchemish on the Euphrates River, Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon defeated the army of Pharaoh Neco of Egypt (Jeremiah 46:2). A Babylonian chronicle of those years records the same battle, in 605 BC. Jehoiakim owed his throne to that pharaoh (2 Kings 23:34). Now the pharaoh's army had lost.

In that same year Jeremiah reminded the people that he had been warning them for twenty-three years (Jeremiah 25:1-3). Now he named the danger from the north, Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon (Jeremiah 25:9). The warnings had a name and an army. That was the year God told Jeremiah to put every word on one scroll.

Into the Word

Picture a long line of freed slaves crossing the wilderness. At the back walk the stragglers, the ones who cannot keep up. Moses later reminded Israel that this is where the Amalekites struck, at the stragglers in the rear, when the people were faint and weary (Deuteronomy 25:17-18). After the battle at Rephidim, God tells Moses to write something down. What He wanted on the page says a great deal about why He wanted anything written at all.

God's writers did not always write from safe rooms. Several psalms carry headings that tie them to the years when King Saul was hunting David. One recalls the day a man named Doeg informed on him (Psalm 52). Another recalls the people of Ziph telling Saul where David was hiding (Psalm 54). One comes from a cave (Psalm 57), and one from the night Saul's men watched his house to kill him (Psalm 59). God's people received some of their songs from a man on the run.

Street Level

The Apron Was Still on the Hook

A young line cook does not show up for the Saturday of graduation weekend, when the line at the diner runs out the door. He does not call. Four cooks cover his station through the worst rush of the year. He has been late too many mornings already, and now he is too ashamed to face anyone. For three weeks, whenever the chef's name lights up his phone, he lets it ring.

One night after closing, the chef sits on a milk crate by the back door and types one long message. He leaves nothing out. The late mornings. The Saturday. The four cooks who covered for him. He writes that he can hold the job until Friday and no longer, and he means it. Then come the last lines. Your apron is still on the hook, he writes. Walk in before Friday and your station is yours. I decided that before I started typing.

The cook reads it in his car with the engine off, then reads it again. Every hard line in it is true. But the whole message was written to bring him back through the kitchen door. Whether he walks in is his to decide. What he will find if he does was decided before he read a word. God's scroll to Judah worked the same way. It named real calamity, and God meant it. But He had it written so they might turn, and before a single line was read aloud, He had said what He would do if they did. The apron was still on the hook.

Today’s World

The People Most at Risk Went to the Top

In January 2022, the Highway Code for England, Scotland, and Wales gained three new rules in its opening section. They set out what the code calls a hierarchy of road users. At the top are the people most likely to be injured in a collision. That means pedestrians, cyclists, horse riders, and motorcyclists. Children, older adults, and disabled people, the code adds, are more at risk.

Under the first new rule, the biggest danger brings the biggest duty. Those in charge of vehicles that can cause the greatest harm bear the greatest responsibility to take care, it says, and to reduce the danger they pose to others. The second rule brings this down to a street corner. At a junction, a driver should give way to people crossing, or waiting to cross, the road the car is turning into or out of. That part says should, not must, so ignoring it is not a crime in itself.

Everyone still has to behave responsibly, the code says, and a rule book cannot steer a single car. What it can do is say, near the front, who is most likely to be hurt and who carries the most responsibility. The scroll God ordered after Rephidim took the side of the stragglers Amalek had struck. The code puts the heaviest duty on drivers. The promise on that scroll came from God Himself.

Christ at the center

Judah did not turn. Yet later in the same book, God promised a new covenant, and it ends with nothing left hanging on His side. He would forgive their wrongdoing and remember their sins no more (Jeremiah 31:31-34). On the night before He died, Jesus took a cup and called it the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20). Hebrews quotes Jeremiah's promise and says Jesus' one offering brought it into force (Hebrews 10:14-18). The forgiveness Jeremiah's scroll held out was paid for at the cross. Our part is still to hear and turn, and that call is real. But nothing on God's side is waiting to be settled. The scroll offered forgiveness. The cross paid for it.

Application

Take two minutes. Read Jeremiah 36:3 slowly. If something comes to mind that you need to turn from, name it to God plainly. Then read the last sentence again, and thank Him for the forgiveness He has already promised.

Discussion Question

God put His warnings in writing because He wanted to forgive. How might a warning given in love sound different from one given in anger? When has a hard word turned out to be a way back for you?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that You see the people at the back of the line, and that You put Your promises in writing. Thank You for the songs You gave a hunted man, and for the scroll You gave a nation that would not listen. We confess that we have heard Your warnings and shrugged them off. Turn us. Where we need to change, give us the courage to do it today. And thank You that Your forgiveness was ready before we turned, paid for by Your Son. In Jesus' name, Amen.
