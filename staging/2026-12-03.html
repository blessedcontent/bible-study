Canonical and Noncanonical Prophets

Prophetic Authority and RelevanceLesson 10Day 6 of 7

In brief

On the road out of Jerusalem, a prophet takes hold of his new cloak and tears it into twelve pieces. He tells the man beside him to take ten. Jeroboam has been managing a work crew. He's about to hear that he will be king. There's no book called Ahijah in the Bible, yet Scripture asks us to listen closely to what he said in that field. God can give a messenger something worth obeying and entrust its telling to someone else.

Yesterday

In Antioch, people who prayed together heard the Holy Spirit call two of their own to a particular work. Much of what we know about people like them comes through other hands. How do we recognize the work of a prophet who left us no biblical book bearing his name?

Historical Context

The King Chose His Own Words

Mesha, king of Moab, had an account of his victories cut into stone. A reader can still see which towns he wanted remembered as places he won back from Israel. At the end of David's life, Chronicles names Nathan, the prophet who confronted the king over the death of Uriah (2 Samuel 12:7-14). A king choosing what to carve in stone might have left out a deed like that.

A canonical book is one we receive as part of Scripture. A noncanonical prophet, as we use the word here, is someone whose own work doesn't form a separate biblical book. Nathan could confront David, and Ahijah could speak God's promise to a hard-working foreman named Jeroboam (1 Kings 11:26-28). We can read both encounters because someone else kept the account. The name on the page and the voice we hear on it can belong to different people.

Into the Word

At the end of David's life, Chronicles names three people who kept records. Where would a later reader look to find out what sort of king David had been?

Ahijah, one of the men named in Solomon's story, meets Jeroboam in a field. Notice what Jeroboam can take home that day and what he must wait for.

When the people later made Jeroboam king, the writer connected it to the word God had given through Ahijah (1 Kings 12:15-20). But once he had a throne, Jeroboam feared losing his people if they worshiped in Jerusalem. He set up golden calves, and Israel was drawn into sin (1 Kings 12:26-30). The promise that came true was never permission to do whatever it took to hang on to the gift.

Joel pictured sons and daughters speaking when God poured out His Spirit (Joel 2:28-29). His words reach forward to signs before the day of the LORD (Joel 2:31). That gives us reason to hear a person we might have passed over. It still leaves us with the work Paul gave the church. Test what you hear and keep what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19-22). We can lay a person's words beside Scripture and ask whether they agree. Our affection for the speaker can't do that work for us.

Paul gives us a further question to bring to a claim. Who is this gift meant to help? He includes prophets among Christ's gifts (Ephesians 4:11), then turns to the people whose faith is meant to grow, so that they themselves can serve (Ephesians 4:12-13). John the Baptist let his own disciples see what that looked like when he pointed them toward Jesus.

Street Level

Four Beats for the Cello

At his first rehearsal with the community orchestra, a boy with a borrowed violin keeps losing the cello tune. He can hear its first few notes. Then his own part comes in and the tune disappears under all those bows. During a break the woman who wrote the piece stops beside the first violinist. For the next rehearsal she wants the violins to wait four more beats before coming in. She asks the violinist to tell the others. On his way out, the boy hears the first violinist pass the change along. His friend at the next stand writes it in the margin of his music.

A week later the boy lifts his bow and can't remember. Four beats sooner or four beats later? His friend tips the penciled page toward him. They wait, and this time he hears the cello finish its tune. The woman who wrote the piece chose the change. The first violinist could speak for her. His friend had kept the words for the day he needed them.

Ahijah could tell Jeroboam what God had decided because God had given him that word. We hear the message because someone kept the account in Kings. Jeroboam needed to heed what Ahijah said there in the field, before he could ever read it in a book. Years afterward another reader can still hear what he heard, down to the if that went with the promise.

Christ at the center

One day John the Baptist saw Jesus coming and called Him the Lamb of God who takes away the world's sin. John explained why he had come baptizing. It was so that Jesus might be revealed to Israel (John 1:29-34). The next day two of John's own disciples heard him speak about Jesus and went after Him. Jesus turned, spoke to them, and invited them to come and see where He was staying (John 1:35-39). Think of the generosity in a ministry that can end an afternoon with two fewer people standing beside the preacher. John's voice had carried far enough for them to hear another. And the One they followed could do for them what John could only point toward. He could take their sin away.

Application

Think of someone whose spiritual judgment you value. Call to mind one thing that person helped you see in Scripture. Say it in your own words. What did it show you about God that you can now carry into a decision of your own?

Discussion Question

Which part of Ahijah's message would have been easiest for Jeroboam to keep and which part easiest to leave in the field, and where do you feel that temptation in your own listening?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for people whose names we know and for those who helped Your word reach us without leaving their names on a page. Give us patience to listen to the whole of what You say, especially the part that comes after a promise we love. Give us the honesty to test what people say in Your name. And when someone has helped us see Jesus more clearly, help us follow where He leads. In Jesus' name, Amen.
