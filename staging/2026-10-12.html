Identifying True Prophets

Old Testament ProphetsLesson 3Day 3 of 7

In brief

Two kings sit on thrones at a city gate while four hundred prophets shout the same happy message. One prophet is missing, and the king of Israel explains why. I hate him. He never says anything good about me. When that man is fetched, the messenger coaches him to go along, and he answers with an oath. Generations earlier, a servant hunting lost donkeys already knew what such a man looks like. A true prophet says what God says, pays what it costs, and is proved right by time.

Yesterday

Yesterday Elijah sat under a broom tree, sure he was the last faithful man alive. God fed him, heard him out, and told him about seven thousand others. Today we meet another lonely voice in the same kingdom. He is standing in front of the same king.

Historical Context

Kings Kept Prophets Who Said Yes

Dig through the royal archives of the ancient Near East and you will find prophets on file. Clay tablets from the city of Mari on the Euphrates, written in the 1700s BC, report messages that prophets sent to their king. A thousand years later, scribes in Nineveh copied out oracles given to the Assyrian king Esarhaddon. One line keeps coming back. Fear not, Esarhaddon. Your enemies will fall, and your throne is safe. Much of what survives is good news for the throne, though Mari's prophets also sent their king warnings.

So when Ahab gathers four hundred prophets and every one of them tells him to march, the pull toward a king's approval is plain to see. The surprise in this story is not the four hundred. It is the one. Israel's law had put a standard in writing. A prophet answers to God for every word, whatever the king hopes to hear.

Into the Word

Picture the threshing floor outside the gate of Samaria. Two kings sit in their royal robes. Four hundred prophets are prophesying in front of them, and one has made himself a set of iron horns to act out the coming victory. They all agree. When everyone who speaks for God says the same thing, and one man does not, how do you know whom to believe? Long before that day, God had fenced the prophet's office with a warning.

Now back to Samaria (1 Kings 22:1-14). Ahab wants the town of Ramoth-gilead back from Aram. He asks Jehoshaphat, the king from Sabbath's memory text, to ride with him. Jehoshaphat agrees, and then asks to hear from the LORD first. The four hundred say go. Jehoshaphat is still uneasy, and he asks whether there is not one more prophet of the LORD. Ahab's answer tells us more than he meant to.

That is how a prophet looked under pressure at court. How did ordinary people know one? Go back several generations, before Israel had any king. A young man named Saul and a servant have spent days hunting his father's lost donkeys (1 Samuel 9:3-14). Saul is ready to quit.

Street Level

He Put the Pin Where the Deed Said

A man is about to pour the slab for a new garage along the edge of his property. Everybody knows where the edge is. It runs along the row of old pines. The seller said so. The neighbor across the road says so. The county's online map shows it there. His brother-in-law asks one question. Has anyone actually surveyed it? There's one guy, the man says, but I can't stand him. He never tells me anything I want to hear.

They call him anyway. The contractor meets the surveyor in the driveway and keeps it friendly. Concrete comes Friday. Everybody already agrees it's the pines. Just confirm it. I don't put the pin where anybody wants it, he says. I put it where the deed says. He spends the day with the deed and the old county records, and then he drives his iron pins into the ground eleven feet short of the pines. The man refuses to pay him and pours the slab anyway. Three years later he tries to sell, the buyer's bank orders a survey, and the garage turns out to sit eleven feet onto someone else's land.

A surveyor does not decide where a line ought to be. He finds where the deed already put it. That was Micaiah's whole oath, and the contractor in the driveway was the messenger who asked him to go along. Notice that this surveyor is no pessimist. Last spring his pins gave a widow nine more feet of garden than she thought she owned. The line was never his to move in either direction. And being disliked is not what made him right. Plenty of people are disliked for being wrong. What that county says about him is what a servant once said about Samuel. His pins hold.

Today’s World

They Fired the Man Who Inspected the Hull

In January 2018 a company called OceanGate was readying a deep-sea craft named Titan. It was built to take people to the Titanic wreck. The company asked David Lochridge, who ran its marine operations, to inspect it. His written report raised safety concerns, the carbon fiber hull among them. It said Titan should not carry people during its test dives.

On January 19 he met with company leaders and kept asking for more testing. On January 23 he was fired. A company email cited an impasse with the chief executive over the hull. That June the company sued him. Its filing faulted him for refusing to accept that its own hull monitors and step-by-step test dives were the better safeguard. His whistleblower complaint to a federal safety agency was closed after the two sides settled that November.

That hull was retired after a crack appeared in 2019, and a second carbon fiber hull replaced it. On June 18, 2023, the second hull imploded during a dive, and all five people aboard died. In August 2025 a Coast Guard board called the loss preventable. It named a toxic workplace culture and a failed whistleblower process among the contributing factors. A firing does not show who was right, and an inspector is not a prophet. But notice the company's filing. He would not accept its assurance. Micaiah's messenger asked for the same thing, one more agreeable voice.

Christ at the center

Set Micaiah beside Jesus and the outline matches. Micaiah swore to speak only what the LORD told him. Jesus said He spoke just what the Father had taught Him (John 8:28). Ahab hated the prophet who would not flatter him. Jesus said the world hated Him because He testified that its works were evil (John 7:7). Micaiah was struck in the face for telling the truth, and so was Jesus at His trial (John 18:22-23). But here the outline breaks. Micaiah saw Israel scattered like sheep without a shepherd, and all he could do was report it. Jesus saw the crowds that same way, had compassion on them (Matthew 9:36), and became the Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11). And He passed Deuteronomy's test with a prediction no one could fake. He said He would be killed and rise again after three days (Mark 8:31). He did.

Application

Think of one person who once told you something true that you did not want to hear. Take two minutes with God and ask an honest question. Have I treated that person the way Ahab treated Micaiah? Tell Him what you find. If you would like to do more, thank that person sometime this week.

Discussion Question

Ahab judged a prophet by whether the message felt good. Where are you tempted to choose your voices the same way (preachers, podcasts, friends)? What would change if you asked did God say it before you asked do I like it?

Closing Prayer

Father, we confess that we like the four hundred. We like voices that agree with us and messages that cost us nothing. Forgive us for the times we have made our comfort the test of what is true. Thank You for servants who said what You said and paid for it. Give us ears for Your word when it is unwelcome, and grace toward the people who bring it. Where we are the ones who must speak, keep us from flattery and from harshness alike. Let our words and our lives point the same way, toward You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
