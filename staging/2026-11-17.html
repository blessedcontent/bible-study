The Literary Context

Interpreting Prophetic WritingsLesson 8Day 4 of 7

In brief

A woman in a garden hears a voice ask whether God really said not to eat from any tree. Much later, on a high edge of the temple, the devil quotes a psalm to Jesus and dares Him to jump. Both times the words sound like God's. Both times they have been cut loose from their setting. Scripture lifted out of its context can be turned against God Himself, and Jesus answered by setting Scripture beside Scripture.

Yesterday

Yesterday we read Isaiah's promise and Paul's greeting in the world they were first written to. The setting kept us from misreading them. Today looks at a closer setting, the words around a verse. Twice in Scripture an enemy pulls God's words out of theirs.

Historical Context

The Temple's Edge Made Visitors Dizzy

Herod the Great began rebuilding the temple around 20 BC. He enlarged the platform around it with massive walls, and along the south side he built a long hall of columns. Josephus, a Jewish historian writing near the end of the first century, describes its height. Anyone looking down from its roof, he says, grew dizzy, because the eye could not reach the bottom of the valley.

Matthew does not say which edge of the temple the devil chose, and many readers picture a corner high above that valley (Matthew 4:5). Wherever it was, it was a height at the center of Israel's worship. A fall from there would have needed a rescue in the holiest place in the land. And the devil's dare came with a Bible verse attached.

Into the Word

A sentence can be quoted perfectly and still be used wrongly. Scripture shows it happening at the very beginning, in a garden, and again when the tempter comes to Jesus. In both scenes the real question is the same. What did God actually say, and what was He saying it about?

Context also tells us what kind of sentence we are reading. Matthew reports that Judas went away and hanged himself (Matthew 27:5). That is a record of despair, not an instruction. When Jesus tells an expert in the law, Go and do likewise (Luke 10:37), the word likewise sends us back to the Samaritan who stopped on the road. Matthew and Luke both tell what happened in the wilderness, with the last two tests in a different order (Luke 4:1-13). There the enemy did more than bend God's words. He quoted them.

Now read the psalm the devil quoted, in its own words.

Street Level

The Crop Was the Lie

Your phone lights up with a screenshot from someone you barely know. It shows one line your closest friend typed in a group chat you are not part of. Honestly, she's too much. Your face goes hot. You start a reply that would end the friendship, then stop, delete it, and ask your friend to send you the whole conversation.

She does. Just above that line, someone had asked whether it was true that you drove four hours to help her move and brought dinner. Just below it, she had written, I don't deserve her. The same four words that looked like an insult were the kindest thing anyone had said about you in months. Every word in the screenshot was hers. The crop was the lie.

On the high edge of the temple, the devil handed Jesus something like that screenshot. The promise he quoted was real. He dropped the phrase about guarding God's people along their way and turned a promise of care into a dare to jump. Jesus did not answer with a cleverer line of His own. He went back to more of what His Father had said. It is also written. Cropped, the promise was aimed at the One who made it. Read with the rest of the thread, it was a promise again.

Christ at the center

Matthew lets the devil's words come back. At the cross, people passing by used the same phrase, If You are the Son of God, and told Him to come down (Matthew 27:40). The religious leaders added that He trusted in God, so let God rescue Him now (Matthew 27:43). It was the wilderness dare again, a demand that God prove Himself on cue. Jesus refused it again. He would not jump from the temple to force His Father's hand, and He would not come down from the cross to prove who He was. He kept trusting His Father through the darkness, and on the third day God raised Him (Matthew 28:5-6). The proof came after the trust, not before it.

Application

Take two minutes with a verse you love. Read the verses just before and after it. Ask what the promise meant there, and to whom. Then thank God for it in its own setting.

Discussion Question

The devil quoted a promise and left part of it out. Why can a verse used out of context do more harm than an obvious lie? Where have you seen a verse pulled from its setting (online, in a conversation, or in your own thinking)?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that Your word holds together, and that Your Son answered every twisted use of it with more of it. Keep us from bending Your words to get what we want, or treating Your promises as dares. When we read a verse, give us patience to read the lines around it. When we are tempted to test You, teach us to trust You instead, as Jesus did in the wilderness and on the cross. In Jesus' name, Amen.
