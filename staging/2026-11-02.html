Literary Genres and Styles

The Prophetic WritingsLesson 6Day 3 of 7

In brief

In one chapter of Jeremiah, God takes His people to court, and the question He opens with is what fault they found in Him. Elsewhere the Bible gives us a travel record, headings over heavy messages, poems that say things twice, and letters that mark which words came from Jesus. God's word comes in many kinds of writing, and each carries His voice in its own way. Noticing the kind is how we hear what He is saying.

Yesterday

Yesterday God told Jeremiah to gather years of messages onto one scroll, so people might turn and be forgiven. But a scroll can hold many kinds of writing. Today looks at the shapes God's word took, and why the shape matters.

Historical Context

Treaties Began With What the King Had Done

Long before Jeremiah, great kings in the ancient Near East made treaties with smaller kingdoms. Hittite treaties from the 1300s and 1200s BC, in what is now Turkey, follow a common pattern. First the great king named himself. Then he recounted what he had already done for the smaller king. Only after that came the duties. Then came a clause about keeping the text and reading it aloud, a list of gods called as witnesses, and the curses and blessings.

Many scholars see a similar shape in Israel's covenant with God, and they hear an echo of it when the prophets bring God's case against His people. Those passages call witnesses, recount what God has done, and then state the charge. Moses' song had already called heaven and earth to listen (Deuteronomy 32:1). In that kind of covenant, the complaint does not come first. Kindness does.

Into the Word

Someone reading straight through the Bible for the first time comes to a list of more than forty place names in a row (Numbers 33:1-49). Is a travel record God's word too? Keep reading and the variety only grows. There are poems that say things twice, headings over visions, a courtroom complaint, and letters. The Bible often tells us what kind of writing we are holding, beginning with a single heading in Isaiah.

Not every page is that dramatic. The list of campsites was recorded at the LORD's command, and each stop held a memory. Elim, with twelve springs and seventy palm trees. Rephidim, where there was no water. Mount Hor, where Aaron died. Read slowly, it is a record of God leading a people one stop at a time. Jeremiah's messages were gathered into a book (Jeremiah 36:2). And much of the New Testament is letters, which have features of their own.

Street Level

Every Memory Was Drawn in Gray

A retired mail carrier joins the library book club the month it picks a graphic memoir, a true story told in drawings. He arrives annoyed. The story makes no sense, he says. The author's grandmother dies in chapter two. Then she turns up on page forty, teaching the author, then a little girl, to ride a bike, and again near the end, braiding her hair for the first day of school. He figures the author lost track of her own story.

A college student across the table opens her copy and lays two pages side by side. Look at the color, she says. Everything happening now is drawn in full color. Every page with the grandmother is drawn in gray. Those are memories. He goes home and starts the book over, and this time he slows down at every gray page. The author is not jumping around. She is grieving, and she has been showing it the whole time.

Nothing in the book had changed. The marks had been there from the first page, and the gray pages turned out to be the heart of the story. Scripture carries marks like that too. Its headings, its poetry, and its labels are there on purpose. None of them makes a word count for less. Each one tells you how to listen. He had been reading the most tender pages in the book as mistakes.

Today’s World

The Same Words Were Read Differently as a Poem

In a study published in 2022 in the journal Discourse Processes, a team led by Stefan Blohm asked 32 college students to read literary texts aloud. An eye tracker followed their eyes, and their voices were recorded. Each text came in two versions with exactly the same words. One was labeled and laid out as prose, the other as a poem.

The form changed the reading. The students read and spoke at a different pace depending on which form they had. As they moved through the prose, they skipped more and more words. In the poems, their voices kept a stronger rhythm. The authors say the results support the idea that readers first sort a text into its kind and then read it in a way that fits.

Thirty-two students are a small group, and the study tracked how they read, not what they understood. Still, the kind of text shaped the reading, and that matters for a verse like Isaiah 17:7. It makes its point in two matching lines. Skim it like prose, and the second line can sound like filler. Read it as the poem it is, and the second line answers the first.

Christ at the center

Jeremiah's courtroom ends with a case still open against the people and their grandchildren. Paul pictures the same kind of court and asks who could bring a charge against God's chosen people. He answers that God Himself declares us right with Him, and Christ Jesus, who died and was raised, now speaks for us at God's right hand (Romans 8:33-34). The Judge with every reason to press His case gave His own Son instead (Romans 8:32). Travel record, burden, poem, lawsuit, and letter all belong to that one story. The case God brought against His people, He settled Himself.

Application

Take two minutes with Isaiah 17:7. Read the first half, then the second, and notice what the second adds. Then pray the verse back to God in your own words, turning your eyes to Him about one thing you are facing today.

Discussion Question

The Bible holds lists, poems, courtroom speeches, and letters. Which kind do you find hardest to read, and why? How might knowing what kind of writing you are reading change what you hear from God in it?

Closing Prayer

Lord, thank You for speaking to us in so many kinds of writing, in the record of a long journey, in burdens and poems, in a courtroom and in letters. Forgive us for skimming the pages that seemed dull and for reading heavy words lightly. Teach us to notice what kind of word You are giving, so that we hear it as You meant it. When we hear Your case against us, help us hear Your mercy too. Thank You that Jesus speaks for us. In Jesus' name, Amen.
