God Seeks Humanity

The Creator SpeaksLesson 1Day 4 of 7

In brief

God walks into the garden He made, stops among the trees, and asks a question He already knows the answer to. Where are you? Today listens to that question and to the dodging that follows, a husband blaming his wife and a wife blaming a snake. Then it hears the same seeking heart in a father running down a road, in a look across a courtyard, and in Paul's cry for rescue. The first question God asked after the first sin was not a trap. It was an open door.

Yesterday

Yesterday ended in the bushes. A serpent twisted what God had said, two people ate, and the sound of God walking sent them running for cover. They could not get out of His sight, only out of the conversation. Today God restarts it.

Historical Context

In Babylon's Story the Gods Wanted Quiet

Israel's neighbors told their own stories about why people exist. In the Babylonian epic of Atrahasis, copied onto clay tablets many centuries before Christ, the lesser gods grow tired of digging canals and refuse to go on. So the gods make humans from clay and the blood of a slain god to do the work for them. People multiply. Their noise keeps the chief god Enlil awake. His answer is plague, then drought, then a flood meant to wipe them out.

Set the garden scene beside that. Here the people have actually done wrong. They have eaten from the one tree God told them to leave alone. If ever there was a moment for God to answer with silence or with a flood, this is it. What He sends is Himself, walking through the trees and calling out to the man. In the old stories the gods wanted less noise from humans. The God of Genesis comes looking for two voices that have gone quiet.

Into the Word

The breeze moves through the garden, and somewhere behind the leaves two people are holding their breath. They are afraid. Adam will say so himself. What comes through the trees is a voice, and the voice is asking a question. Pay attention to who moves first in this story, because nobody in the bushes was planning to come out. How does God treat people who are hiding from Him?

Jesus told a story about this same God. A son takes his inheritance early, wastes it in a far country, and heads home rehearsing a speech about becoming a hired hand (Luke 15:11-32). He never gets to finish it. While he is still in the distance, his father sees him, runs to him, and holds him. The same chapter has a shepherd searching for one sheep and a woman sweeping the house for one coin (Luke 15). In every story, the one who lost something does the looking.

Sometimes all Scripture shows us of the seeking is a look.

Why does God have to be the one who moves? Paul answers from the inside. He describes a person who wants the good and keeps doing the opposite (Romans 7:15), who agrees with God's law in his mind and finds another force at work in his body (Romans 7:15-24). He ends with a cry, not a plan. Who will rescue me?

Street Level

He Had a Key and He Knocked

Imagine a man who borrowed eight hundred dollars from his oldest friend in the spring and promised it back by June. It is now late September. He has not paid, and somewhere along the way he stopped answering texts, because every reply would have to begin with the money. The blinds stay shut. He tells himself he will reach out once he has the cash.

One evening there is a knock. It is the friend, and here is the detail that matters. He has a key to this apartment. He has watered the plants here. He could let himself in, and he knocks anyway. Then he asks a question he does not need answered, since the car is in the lot and the light is on. Hey, where have you been?

The door opens a few inches, and out it comes. Work cut my hours. The landlord raised the rent. And you did say there was no rush. Each sentence is true enough, and each one points somewhere else. The last one points at the man on the step. The friend lets him finish. He does not leave, and he does not pretend the money is nothing. He says he did not drive across town for eight hundred dollars. A man with a key who knocks wants more than to get in. He wants to be let in. God's question among the trees was that kind of knock. He knew where Adam was. He wanted Adam to open the door.

Today’s World

They Walked Into the Church on Their Own

For four days in August 2005, people with open arrest warrants walked into Mount Sinai Baptist Church in Cleveland to turn themselves in. It was the first Fugitive Safe Surrender, a program started by the local U.S. Marshal with judges, prosecutors, public defenders, and clergy. The notices said plainly that this was not amnesty. Anyone who came would still answer for the warrant, with favorable consideration for coming in. In those four days, 838 people came.

Researchers Daniel Flannery and Jeff Kretschmar followed the program through 22 events in 20 communities from 2005 to 2010. In all, 35,103 people who believed they were wanted surrendered at a church, and fewer than 2 percent were arrested. Among those who filled out a survey, nearly three in four said it mattered that the place was a church. The study had no comparison group, so it cannot say how many would have come forward anyway.

What it does record is thousands of people who believed they were wanted walking in on their own, through a church door, on an invitation that was honest about what it could not promise. God's question in the garden erased nothing Adam had done. It gave a frightened man a way to come out of the trees.

Christ at the center

God did not stop asking where are you when the garden closed behind Adam. He asked it through prophets for centuries, and then He came and asked it in person. Jesus spent His days finding people in their hiding places. A woman alone at a well at noon (John 4:6-7). A man living among the tombs (Mark 5:2-3). A disciple in a courtyard. He was not sent to condemn the world but to save it (John 3:17), and the search took Him all the way to a cross. Paul's thanks and the prodigal's welcome both run through that hill. You are not being hunted. You are being looked for, by Someone who has already paid to bring you home.

Application

Answer God's first question today. Find a private moment and tell Him where you actually are, not where you think you should be. Try one sentence each for your faith, your closest relationship, and the thing you most want to blame on someone else. Begin each one, Lord, here is where I am.

Discussion Question

When God asked what happened, Adam pointed at Eve and Eve pointed at the serpent. Where do you tend to point when you are in the wrong (a person, your past, your stress)? What would a plain and I ate sound like in that situation?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for coming to look for us. We would have stayed in the trees. You asked where we were when You already knew, and You kept asking when our answers were bad. We confess our excuses, the people we have blamed, and the half-truths we have told You about ourselves. Here is where we are. Find us the way the father found his son on the road and the way Jesus found Peter in the courtyard. We cannot rescue ourselves, so we say what Paul said. Thanks be to You, through Jesus Christ our Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.
