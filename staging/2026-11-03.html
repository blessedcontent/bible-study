Literary Assistants

The Prophetic WritingsLesson 6Day 4 of 7

In brief

Officials in Jerusalem hear a frightening scroll and ask the scribe one question. Did these words come from Jeremiah's mouth? Centuries later another scribe adds a line of his own to Paul's longest letter, and in other letters Paul takes the pen at the end to sign. God's word often passed through a helper's hand and stayed tied to its source. The helpers were faithful, the authors stood behind every word, and God saw the people holding the pen.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul labeled which words came from Jesus and which were his own instruction. Those labels sat inside a letter. But Paul did not always hold the pen himself. Today meets the people who did.

Historical Context

The Last Lines Were in Another Hand

In the Roman world, many people could not write, and many who could still used a scribe. The statesman Cicero mentions dictating a letter while riding in his carriage on the way to camp. A trained scribe could take down the words, write them out cleanly, and send the letter on its way.

Letters found in Egypt's dry sand show what often happened at the end. The body is in one practiced hand. The closing greeting or signature is in another, the sender's own. Cicero writes of letters from friends that ended with a few lines in their own handwriting. That last line could assure the reader that the letter was genuine and that the sender stood behind it.

Into the Word

In a room in the king's palace, a group of officials has just heard a scroll read aloud, and the words have frightened them (Jeremiah 36:11-16). They know they must report it to the king. But first they turn to the man who wrote it out and ask a careful question. Where did these words come from?

God did not overlook the scribe. In the year Baruch wrote that scroll, God sent him a message of his own. Baruch had said he was worn out with groaning. God told him not to seek great things for himself, and He promised to keep him alive wherever he went (Jeremiah 45:1-5). One short chapter of Jeremiah is addressed to the man holding the pen.

Paul used scribes for other letters too, and several of them end in his own hand. He signs the greeting himself in 1 Corinthians (1 Corinthians 16:21). In Galatians he points to the large letters he is making as he takes over the pen (Galatians 6:11). In one letter he explains why he does it.

Street Level

The Handwriting Was the Florist's

On a college student's birthday, flowers arrive at her dorm with a small card. Her father drives a truck, and that week he is somewhere in Nebraska. He could not be there, and he could not write a card from the cab, so he called a flower shop near campus and told them what to say. The florist wrote it out in her neat, round hand. Then she read it back to him twice, because he asked her to.

Her roommate reads it over her shoulder. Your dad has nice handwriting, she says. The student laughs. That isn't his handwriting. But the words are his, every one, down to the last line. Proud of you, Bug. At the bottom of the card, in the same round hand, the florist has added a line of her own. Written by Tasha at the shop. He made me read it back twice.

The pen belonged to a stranger, and the card was still her dad's. Parts of the Bible reached the page the same way. Baruch wrote for Jeremiah, and Tertius wrote for Paul. Their hands never made the words their own. Nobody else calls her Bug.

Today’s World

Where the Words Should Have Been

A transcript is how an appeals court hears a trial it never attended. In one New York case, that record failed. A man was appealing his convictions when his lawyers found that the trial's main stenographer had often typed words like blah, omitted, and untranscribable instead of what was said. Whole days were missing, including three days of jury selection and the day the jury reached its verdict. So were parts of the testimony.

Instead of ordering a new trial, an appeals court sent the case back for a four-day hearing to rebuild the record. The trial judge testified, along with the judge's law clerk, the lawyers on both sides, and two court clerks. The judge's 124 pages of notes went into the record. In May 2026, New York's highest court called the original transcript utterly inexcusable. It held that, on the unusual facts of the case, the rebuilt record was enough to protect the man's right to appeal.

The opinion does not explain why it happened. It does show what a record of someone's words has to answer. Whose words are these, and are they all here? The officials put the first question to Baruch before they reported to the king. Baruch could answer both. The words were Jeremiah's, all of them, and every one had gone onto the scroll.

Christ at the center

Paul's own handwriting shows up once more, in a short letter to a man named Philemon about his slave Onesimus. If Onesimus has wronged him or owes him anything, Paul says, put it on Paul's account, and Paul promises in his own hand to repay it (Philemon 1:18-19). The same Paul told the Colossians what God did with the record of debt that stood against us. He canceled it and nailed it to the cross (Colossians 2:13-14). Paul's hand promised to pay a debt that was not his. Jesus' hands were nailed to the cross, and the record against us was nailed there with them.

Application

Take two minutes. Think of one person whose quiet work has carried God's word to you, a teacher, a parent, a pastor, or someone who once sent you a verse. Thank God for that person by name. If you want to do more later, tell them.

Discussion Question

Baruch and Tertius held the pen, but the words belonged to someone else. Why might it matter that the Bible names its helpers? Where do you serve quietly behind someone else's work, and what does God see there?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for the people who held the pen, for Baruch in a dangerous year and Tertius at Paul's side. Thank You that Your word stayed Yours through every hand it passed through, and for the people who carried it to us. Make us faithful with whatever You have given us to carry, even when no one sees. And thank You for the hands of Jesus, nailed to the cross, where the record against us was canceled. In Jesus' name, Amen.
