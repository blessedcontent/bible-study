The Elijah Message

The Call of a ProphetLesson 2Day 3 of 7

In brief

One man stands on a mountain facing 450 prophets of Baal, a hostile king, and a silent crowd. He asks one question. How long will you limp between two opinions? By evening a soaked altar is burning and a nation is on its face. Centuries later God promises to send Elijah again, aimed this time at the hearts of parents and children. The Elijah message was never mainly fire from the sky. It is a call to stop limping and come home.

Yesterday

Yesterday Abram believed God, and God counted it as righteousness. God alone walked between the pieces. More than a thousand years later, Abraham's children were splitting their worship between the LORD and a storm god. Today God sends one prophet to call them back.

Historical Context

Baal Was Supposed to Own the Rain

In the late 1920s a farmer's plow struck an old tomb on the Syrian coast, and archaeologists soon uncovered the lost city of Ugarit and a library of clay tablets. The tablets tell the stories of Baal, the god Queen Jezebel brought with her from Sidon (1 Kings 16:31-33). They call him the Rider on the Clouds. Rain, storm, and lightning were his department. A carved stone from Ugarit shows him striding forward with a weapon raised, a storm god ready to strike.

That is why Elijah's first word to King Ahab was a weather report. No dew or rain except at my word (1 Kings 17:1). Three dry years struck at the one job Baal was supposed to do. Then Elijah chose a contest on Baal's own ground. If any god could throw fire from the sky, it should have been him.

Into the Word

Imagine standing in the crowd on Carmel that morning. You have prayed to the LORD, and to Baal when the fields needed rain, and nobody ever made you pick. Now a prophet the king has hunted for three years steps forward and says the time for both is over. The scene is long and worth reading slowly. Watch who does the shouting and who does the waiting.

Look at how a prophet works. Elijah does not perform. He says he has acted at God's command, and then he steps back and lets God answer for Himself. The crowd's cry, the LORD, He is God, is very nearly Elijah's own name, which means my God is the LORD. For one evening a whole nation said what one man had been saying alone. About four hundred years later, the last page of the Old Testament picks his name up again.

Street Level

She Never Deleted the App

Picture a woman four months from her wedding. She loves the man, and she means to marry him. She also still has a dating app on her phone, in a folder on the last screen. She never meets anyone. She answers a message now and then. It does not feel like cheating to her. She has been left before, and the app feels like a spare tire.

Her sister notices the icon one afternoon and does not lecture. She asks one question. When your car died out on the county road in March, who came? The woman says nothing, because she remembers that night. Her fiancé was at work, so she messaged two of those friendly strangers first. Neither one wrote back. Then she called him. He picked up on the second ring, left his shift, and drove forty minutes in the rain with jumper cables.

That is the question Elijah put to the crowd on Carmel. The story reports their silence, not their reasons. Divided loyalty can work the way her app did, as cover in case God does not come through. Elijah asked them to look at who answers. She deleted it that night, and then she told her fiancé the truth. It was the first evening she had been all the way engaged.

Today’s World

Most Did Not Stay Estranged

How often do grown children and their parents drift out of each other's lives? Rin Reczek, a sociologist at Ohio State University, and two colleagues went looking. They used a national survey that followed American families from 1994 to 2018. It covered more than 8,000 ties with mothers and more than 8,000 with fathers. The researchers tracked how often grown children saw or spoke with each parent and how close they felt. By that measure about one in four reported a stretch of estrangement from a father. With mothers it was 6 percent.

Then came the part Reczek called surprising. Among people who had reported estrangement from a mother and stayed in the survey, 81 percent no longer reported it in a later round. For fathers it was 69 percent. The study, published in the Journal of Marriage and Family in 2023, cannot say who reached out first, or why, or whether trust returned or the change lasted. Some distance keeps a person safe, and the numbers judge none of it. They do show that a long silence is often not the end of the story. Malachi aims Elijah's work at that very gap.

Christ at the center

On Carmel the fire fell on the sacrifice, and the people were spared. Hold that picture. A guilty nation stood a few steps from the altar. The fire consumed the offering, the wood, the stones, even the dust, and touched none of them. Centuries later, outside Jerusalem, judgment fell on a sacrifice again while guilty people stood watching, and this time Jesus was the offering. He took the fire so that divided hearts could be turned and not burned. Elijah asked how long people would waver. Jesus says no one can serve two masters (Matthew 6:24), and then He says come to Me (Matthew 11:28). The God who answers by fire gave His final answer on a cross.

Application

Take two minutes and ask yourself one honest question. What do I lean on, besides God, to make my life work? Name the first thing that comes to mind. Money, approval, a screen, being needed. You need not fix it today. Say it to Him plainly, and ask Him to turn your heart.

Discussion Question

The crowd on Carmel said nothing when Elijah asked them to choose. Why can a divided heart feel so comfortable (think of something you lean on the way Israel leaned on Baal for rain)? What would it look like to stop limping there?

Closing Prayer

LORD, You are God. We say it with the crowd on the mountain, and we admit how often we have limped between You and whatever else promised rain. Thank You for not sending the fire on us. Turn our hearts back. Turn the hearts of parents toward their children and children toward their parents, in our own homes first. Repair what is torn down in us, stone by stone. Make us people who point past ourselves and let You answer. In Jesus' name, Amen.
