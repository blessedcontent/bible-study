Apostles and Prophets

New Testament ProphetsLesson 4Day 3 of 7

In brief

Paul is dictating the first line of a letter to Rome. He gives his name and his calling, an apostle, and before the sentence ends he has brought up the prophets. His gospel was promised through them. Writing to the Ephesians, he puts both groups under one building. Apostles and prophets are the foundation. Christ Jesus is the cornerstone. Everyone else is built on top. Apostles and prophets had different callings and one task. Their witness to Jesus is the foundation, and Jesus is the stone that sets every line.

Yesterday

Yesterday the crowds called Jesus a prophet, and Peter went past the whole list. You are the Christ, the Son of the living God. John called Him the Word. Today we ask about the people He sent out to carry that news.

Historical Context

The Temple Stood on Charcoal and Wool

People who lived near Ephesus had a famous lesson in foundations. The city's pride was the temple of Artemis, counted among the seven wonders of the ancient world. Luke records a riot there in her honor (Acts 19:23-34). The Roman writer Pliny says the temple had 127 columns, each sixty feet tall, and took 120 years to build. He also describes the part no visitor saw. He says the builders chose marshy ground so that earthquakes would do less harm. Then they had to keep all that marble from sinking. So under the foundations they packed a layer of trodden charcoal, and over it a layer of sheepskins with the wool still on.

Nobody traveled to Ephesus to admire charcoal. But every column stood because of it. The letter that carries the name of Ephesus describes the church in a builder's words. Foundation. Cornerstone. A building that rises into a holy temple (Ephesians 2:21-22). Paul is telling people who knew a famous temple that God is raising a better one, made of people. And the first thing he tells them about it is what lies underneath.

Into the Word

Picture Paul dictating the opening line of a letter to Rome, a church he has not yet visited. How does he introduce himself? A servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle. Then, before the sentence ends, he adds that God promised this gospel beforehand through His prophets in the Holy Scriptures (Romans 1:1-2). The word apostle means someone sent out with a commission. Paul's is specific. He is the apostle to the Gentiles (Romans 11:13), and he holds that post by the will of God (1 Corinthians 1:1). Not everyone accepted it.

So what are apostles and prophets for? Paul answers with a building.

Which prophets does Paul have in mind? The next chapter gives a clue.

Peter sets two groups side by side as well, and he reaches back to the older prophets to do it.

Street Level

They Wrote Down What Her Hands Did

Picture a grandmother whose Christmas bread is the pride of her family. She has never used a recipe. A handful of this, a splash of that, knead it until it feels right. One December two relatives decide to catch it on paper. A granddaughter stands at her elbow with measuring cups. She catches each handful before it reaches the bowl and writes down the amount. An aunt stands on the other side and writes what cups cannot measure. How long the kneading goes on. What the dough looks like when she stops. They have different jobs, and they end up with one card, because both were watching the same pair of hands.

Thirty years later the family is spread over four states, and the bread has grown with it. There are rolls now, a braided loaf, and a cranberry version a cousin invented. Nobody serves the card. It stays taped inside a cupboard door where no guest will see it. But every loaf on every table goes back to it. When a batch comes out flat, nobody guesses, and nobody writes a new card. They read the old one again. And when the cousin wondered whether her cranberry loaf belonged, the family asked one question. Does it still taste like hers?

That is how Paul's foundation works. The apostles and prophets did different work, and they were all looking at the same Lord. What they set down was never their own. Everything since is built on it and checked against it. The card is not the point of the kitchen, and it was never about the two who held the pencils. Every word on it is hers.

Today’s World

The Name Stayed With the First Frog

A small frog is common in the wet forests of western Ecuador and southern Colombia. For decades it went by a scientific name that turned out to belong to another animal. In zoology a species name is not tied to a description in a book. It is tied to a preserved animal called the type. The type is usually picked when the species is first named. Every later animal that gets the name is supposed to match it.

The type for this name was collected in Ecuador in the 1800s and is kept at the Natural History Museum in London. Santiago Ron, of the Pontifical Catholic University of Ecuador, traveled to England to look at it. It was not the same kind of frog. It belonged to a species that had been named four years earlier. So the old name had to follow its specimen, and the familiar frog was left with no name at all. In July 2026 Ron's team gave it one, Pristimantis milpe. They rested the case on body form, calls, and DNA.

Notice which way the correction ran. Decades of habit did not get to redefine the name. The name stayed with the first specimen, and later usage was brought back into line with it. A preserved frog is not Scripture, and a museum is not an apostle. When later labels drift, the first witness is the one that does not move.

Christ at the center

Before there were apostles, there was the Apostle. Hebrews tells us to fix our thoughts on Jesus, the apostle and high priest whom we confess (Hebrews 3:1). He is the first Sent One. The Father sent Him, and on the evening of His resurrection He passed the sending on. As the Father has sent Me, so also I am sending you (John 20:21). That is why the apostles' preaching and letters keep circling one subject. His life, His death, His rising, His work as our priest, His return. Long before, a psalm had spoken of a stone the builders rejected, which became the cornerstone (Psalm 118:22). Peter says that stone is Jesus, and that whoever believes in Him will never be put to shame (1 Peter 2:6-7). A foundation lies under the floor so that something else can be seen. What rises on this one is His house.

Application

Take two minutes and read Ephesians 2:19-22. Then ask yourself one question. When I want to know who Jesus is, what do I reach for first? A feeling, a favorite speaker, or the witness of His apostles and prophets? If you want to go further, pick one short Gospel passage to read tomorrow before any other voice.

Discussion Question

Paul says God's household is built on the apostles and prophets, with Christ as the cornerstone. Which voices shape your picture of Jesus most (sermons, songs, social media, habit)? How would you check one of them against that foundation?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for those You sent with the news about Your Son. Thank You that eyewitnesses saw Him and heard Him, and that their witness was written down, so that we are not left guessing. Forgive us when we build on thinner ground, on moods and loud voices. Set our lives square to the cornerstone. Where we have drifted out of line, move us back. And make us, together with all Your people, a house where You are glad to live. In Jesus' name, Amen.
