Literary Sources

The Prophetic WritingsLesson 6Day 5 of 7

In brief

An old teacher ponders, searches out, and arranges many sayings. A royal historian points his readers to the archives. Jude quotes an old prophecy, and Paul quotes a Greek poet. God's writers did research as well as receive visions. They gathered, weighed, and set in order what others had said and written, and God guided what they kept.

Yesterday

Yesterday Baruch and Tertius held the pen, and Paul signed his letters in his own hand. Those helpers wrote down what they heard. Some writers also worked from what others had already written. Today looks at the sources they used.

Historical Context

Part of Ezra Is Written in the Empire's Language

The Persian Empire was run through writing. Orders, reports, and letters moved between the king's court and distant provinces, and much of that writing was in Aramaic, the shared language officials used across the empire. Ezra's book shows this plainly. Where it begins to quote letters between local officials and the king, the book itself switches from Hebrew to Aramaic (Ezra 4:7-8). It stays in Aramaic through Ezra 6:18 and returns to it for a royal letter (Ezra 7:12-26).

One of those chapters turns on an archive search. When officials questioned the rebuilding of the temple, King Darius had the royal records searched. A scroll turned up at Ecbatana with the decree of Cyrus allowing the temple to be rebuilt (Ezra 6:1-5). Ezra presents such documents as copies (Ezra 4:11). He let them speak in their own language.

Into the Word

Many of us picture Bible writers receiving every word in a vision. Some did see visions. But near the end of one book, a writer describes a different kind of work, the kind done at a table, with time and care.

The book of Proverbs shows the same kind of gathering. It includes sayings of the wise (Proverbs 22:17). It includes proverbs of Solomon that King Hezekiah's men copied out more than two centuries later (Proverbs 25:1). And one chapter opens with the teaching King Lemuel's mother gave him (Proverbs 31:1 and Proverbs 31). Historians worked with sources too.

Ezra copied letters and royal decrees into his book (Ezra 4-7, chapters 4, 5, 6, and 7). Nehemiah found the register of the first families to return from exile and copied it in, name by name and number by number (Nehemiah 7:5-72). Luke says his Gospel rests on what eyewitnesses handed down, and he wrote it so that Theophilus could be sure of what he had been taught (Luke 1:1-4). One New Testament writer even quoted a line found outside the Bible.

Street Level

She Took One Line From the Almanac

Every morning before the coffee shop opens, the barista writes one line on the chalkboard by the register. She takes her time choosing it. All week she collects candidates from old books, song lyrics, and things customers say at the counter. A line has to pass two tests. It has to be true, and it has to be worth reading at six in the morning. Then she writes where it came from underneath.

One morning the line is from an old farm almanac. A wheat farmer in his seventies stops with his cup halfway to his mouth. That almanac has been wrong about the weather my whole life, he says. She has read it cover to cover, and she is not about to defend its forecasts. But one sentence in it is plain truth. Plow the field you have, not the one you wish you had. She tells him she is not asking anyone to plant by the almanac. Only to read the line. He reads it twice on his way out.

The board never told anyone to trust the almanac. It said one line was true. Bible writers used sources with that kind of care, and the Spirit was in the choosing. When Jude quoted a prophecy whose words closely match a book outside the Bible, that one line came into Scripture. Quoting that line passed no judgment, for or against, on the rest of the book. The forecasts stayed in the almanac.

Today’s World

The Saying Had the Wrong Name on It

A popular saying describes the day when staying tight in a bud became more painful than the risk it took to blossom. For decades it has been passed around under the name of the writer Anaïs Nin. In October 2026, a website called Quote Investigator published what it found. The site traces famous sayings back to the earliest places they appear.

It found no solid evidence that Nin wrote it. The earliest close match was in a 1984 business book, as an epigraph credited to Lassie Benton, in slightly different words. In 2013 the blog of a press that publishes Nin's work posted a note from Elizabeth Appell. She said she had been known as Lassie Benton and had written the line in 1979, while she was director of public relations for a university in California. The earliest credit to Nin the site found came in 1988. Its conclusion is careful. Appell probably wrote it, and the Nin credit is probably a mistake.

The wording barely changed in all those years. The name attached to it did, and it took patient searching through dated pages to sort that out. Proverbs opens under Solomon's name, yet it keeps other names on its pages too. There is Agur (Proverbs 30:1), and a king named Lemuel, whose words begin with what his mother taught him. Getting the name right is part of telling the truth.

Christ at the center

Near the end of Nehemiah's register you find a sad line. Some priests searched for their family records and could not find them, so they were excluded from the priesthood (Nehemiah 7:63-65). Matthew opens his Gospel with a family record too, the line of Jesus from Abraham through David (Matthew 1:1-17). And through that one Son of David, people with no record of belonging are welcomed in. To all who received Him, He gave the right to become children of God (John 1:12). Peter calls believers a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9). A register once left some at the door. The Son of David opened it.

Application

Take two minutes with Luke 1:1-4. Notice why Luke wrote, so that his reader could be sure. Name one thing about Jesus you want to be surer of, and ask God for that. Then thank Him for the people who handed the story down to you.

Discussion Question

A quotation like Jude's does not, by itself, endorse a whole book. What is the difference between quoting a source and approving everything in it? Where in your own reading or listening do you need that kind of care?

Closing Prayer

Lord, thank You for the writers who pondered, searched, and set things in order, and for the records, letters, and eyewitness accounts You used. Thank You that nothing they gathered was outside Your care. Give us patience to read carefully and wisdom to tell a true line from a false one. And thank You that in Your Son we belong to Your family, even when we had no record of belonging. In Jesus' name, Amen.
