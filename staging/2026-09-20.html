The Grace of Jesus

Grace, Love, and FellowshipLesson 13Day 2 of 7

In brief

A secretary dips his pen somewhere in Macedonia, and the first real word Paul gives him for Corinth is grace. Thirteen chapters later the last word is grace again. Today follows that word from the top of the letter to the bottom, through a trade that left the rich One poor, into a promise that receivers will reign, and out to the sign-offs where grace is the last thing a church hears. Grace is not Paul's way of being polite. It is the only word big enough to open and close a letter to people like us.

Yesterday

Yesterday a reader in Corinth reached the last line of Paul's hardest letter and found a blessing with three names in it instead of one more warning. Jesus was named first. Today we start where Paul started, with the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ. Why is it the first word and the last word of everything he wrote?

Historical Context

The Next Letter Was Written From a Corinthian Guest Room

Picture the winter after this letter arrived. Paul made the visit he had promised. Luke records three months in Greece (Acts 20:2-3) without naming the city. Paul's own greetings fill in the gap. During that stay he dictated his longest surviving letter, Romans. He sends greetings from his host, Gaius, and from a city official named Erastus (Romans 16:23). He introduces the woman carrying the letter as Phoebe from Cenchreae (Romans 16:1-2), the harbor town on Corinth's eastern shore. Those clues are why most scholars place the writing of Romans in Corinth.

If that is right, look at how Romans begins and ends. It opens with grace and peace to everyone in Rome (Romans 1:7). It closes with grace, right after a promise that Satan will be crushed (Romans 16:20). Both lines were likely written a short walk from the people who had given Paul the most trouble. He used the same greeting for Corinth itself (1 Corinthians 1:3), for the Galatians (Galatians 1:3), and for Philippi (Philippians 1:2). The grace he sent to Rome probably had a Corinthian return address. It was the same grace he had already spent on Corinth.

Into the Word

Every one of Paul's surviving letters starts the same way, and it is not the way letters started. A Greek letter opened with the writer's name, the reader's name, and a one-word hello. Paul keeps the names and swaps the hello for a blessing, then ends with the blessing he began with. A word a writer cannot get into or out of a letter without is a word he thinks you cannot live without. So what is this grace, and what did it cost to make it the first and last thing Paul had to say?

Paul had defined the word for Corinth a few chapters earlier, in the middle of asking for money, with a sentence about a trade.

Grace given is one thing. What does it do to the people who receive it? Paul answered that in the letter he wrote next, from a Corinthian's guest room.

Then watch how Paul says goodbye. The first of his four closings has a battle in it.

Street Level

The Receipt Said One Dollar

Imagine a hardware store in a small town, forty years under one man's name. A chain is opening out by the highway, and a developer's offer on the building would fund the rest of his life. Instead he calls the woman who has run his register for six years into the back office and slides a folder across the desk. A bill of sale. Purchase price, one dollar. She laughs. She has done the math on that store the way you do the math on a house you will never afford. Six years at a register do not add up to it. Then she sees his signature already on the line.

A dollar sale is not a joke, and it is not a loan. It is a sale, so the whole thing transfers. On Monday she unlocks the front door with the same key she has carried for six years, and the key is not what changed. She sets the hours now. When a customer asks for the owner, she is the owner. The man who signed is living on less than he could have, on purpose. He still reads her the books straight in the months she loses money. He does not come for the store. It was hers the day he signed it.

That is the direction Paul built into the word grace. He was rich, and for your sake He became poor, so that you would end up rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). The loss runs toward the One who had everything. The standing runs toward people who put in nothing but their name. Grace does not keep you on at the register, hoping. It puts your name on the deed.

Today’s World

The Keys Came Before the Test

Housing First runs on one reversed rule. The apartment comes first. Getting it does not depend on being sober, finishing treatment, or passing any other readiness test. Urban Institute researchers tested that order in Denver on 724 people. They had spent years cycling between the street, the emergency room, and the jail. Half were offered supportive housing with no readiness requirements. Half got the usual services.

Of those who moved in, 86 percent were still stably housed a year later and 77 percent after three years, counting only those still living. The group offered housing also used shelters 40 percent less. The study did not find every life repaired. It found that housing given first held, even for people whose lives were not fixed yet. The keys stayed in their hands.

That is the order Paul used with Corinth. Grace was the first word of the letter, before anything had been put right, and the last word too. Help that arrives before you earn it, and stays when you stumble, is not softer help. It is the kind that holds.

Christ at the center

Be honest about the other half of the ledger for a moment. Take the things you have said and done this year, the ones you would least like read aloud, and ask what they deserve. Scripture's answer is not flattering. Sin earns death (Romans 6:23), and no amount of later kindness pays that bill down. Grace does not argue with the verdict. It moves the sentence onto Someone else. The rich One became poor, the sinless One took the cross, and He did it for your sake, on purpose, before you had done a single thing to make it reasonable. That is why grace is a Person before it is a doctrine. You cannot receive it from a distance any more than you can receive a rescue from a distance. Jesus is the grace of God with a face and hands and scars, and what He offers instead of what you deserve is Himself. Take it, and start living like someone who was paid for.

Application

Borrow Paul's habit for one day. Write the word grace at the top of the first thing you write this morning and at the bottom of the last thing you write tonight. In between, each time you catch yourself keeping score against someone, including yourself, say one sentence under your breath. He was rich, and for my sake He became poor. Notice by bedtime whether the score still feels worth keeping.

Discussion Question

What do the things you have said and done this year deserve, and what does it do to you that grace was Paul's first and last word to people exactly like that (think of the one thing you would least want read aloud)? Where this week does grace need to become the first word instead of the last resort?

Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, You were rich and You became poor, and You did it for us, on purpose, before we had done anything to deserve it. Thank You. We confess that we still treat Your grace like a last resort, something we reach for after our own efforts run out. Make it our first word instead. Let it be the ground we stand on when we open our mouths, and the sound we leave behind when we close them. Teach us to receive without bargaining, and then stand us up. Where we are tired and the thorn has not gone away, say to us what You said to Paul, and let it be enough. In Jesus' name, Amen.
