The Historical-Cultural Context

Interpreting Prophetic WritingsLesson 8Day 3 of 7

In brief

Picture a family in ancient Judah who have buried a baby, as many families then had. Isaiah tells them that in God's new heavens and new earth, no nursing infant will live only a few days, and someone who dies at a hundred will be counted young. Read that line alone today and it can sound as if death still happens there. Reading Scripture in its own historical and cultural setting does not shrink its promises or cancel its commands. It lets us hear what God actually said.

Yesterday

Yesterday Jesus said He came to fulfill the Law and the Prophets, and we watched Scripture explain Scripture. Some keys sit on another page. Others sit in the world the words were first written to. Today reads a promise from Isaiah and a greeting from Paul in their own world.

Historical Context

Many Families Had Buried a Child

Many families among Isaiah's first hearers knew the grief of burying a young child. In Judah's family tombs from that era, archaeologists have found the bones of infants and children alongside the adults. Scripture does not hide that grief. David's infant son died on the seventh day (2 Samuel 12:18). Even a full life was short by our standards. A psalm puts it at seventy years, or eighty for the strong (Psalm 90:10).

So when Isaiah promised that no nursing infant would live only a few days, he was naming a grief his hearers knew. When he said someone who died at a hundred would be counted young, he was describing a life far beyond the one they knew. The promise was written to land among real families.

Into the Word

Near the end of Isaiah, God promises to create new heavens and a new earth. Then comes a line that has puzzled many readers. If God is making the world new, why is anyone dying at a hundred?

Those colors were chosen with care. The next lines promise that God's people will live in the houses they build and eat from the vineyards they plant (Isaiah 65:21-22). Israel's covenant had warned of the reverse, houses lived in by others and harvests eaten by strangers (Deuteronomy 28). Isaiah describes the curse undone in language that echoes the covenant.

The same habit of reading helps with a command that many modern readers find strange.

Street Level

He Saved the Peel

The best Christmas of his life, the old man tells the aide, was the year he got an orange. She is taping paper snowflakes to the window of his room, and she laughs and waits for the rest of the joke. There isn't any. He grew up on a farm where nobody had money in December, and an orange in winter was something you looked at in a store window and did not buy. He found it in the toe of his stocking. He made it last three days, a section at a time, and kept the peel in his dresser drawer because it still smelled like the orange.

The aide had heard an orange and pictured a sad little gift, the kind someone grabs at the last minute. He meant the best thing a boy in that farmhouse could imagine finding. The word was the same. She had heard it from her world instead of his.

Isaiah's hundred years can land on us the way the orange landed on her. Most children around us grow up, and plenty of people reach their eighties, so a promise about dying at a hundred can sound like a ceiling. Isaiah's first hearers lived where many families had buried a child, and hardly anyone they knew had seen a hundred. To them a hundred years was the orange, the most life they could picture anyone getting. Through Isaiah, God also promised an end to death itself. A hundred years was never the edge of His promise. It was the edge of what they could imagine.

Christ at the center

Isaiah's hope does not rest on longer lives or better medicine. Paul ties it to an empty tomb. He calls the risen Christ the firstfruits, the first of a harvest that will include everyone who belongs to Him (1 Corinthians 15:20-23). When that harvest comes, Paul says, Isaiah's words come true, and death is swallowed up in victory (1 Corinthians 15:54-57). A hundred-year-old youth was a picture. Jesus' resurrection is the first piece of the real thing. The holy kiss grows from the same root. Through the cross, people who were strangers become members of God's household (Ephesians 2:13-19). We greet one another as family because He made us family, at the cost of His own life.

Application

Take two minutes. Read Isaiah 25:8 slowly and name one grief you are carrying. Thank God that He has promised to wipe away tears. If you gather with other believers this week, plan to greet one person in a way that says you belong here.

Discussion Question

Isaiah described the new creation in the colors of his hearers' world. Why can knowing a passage's setting strengthen our trust in it? What part of the Bible made more sense once you learned its setting (a custom, a place, or a promise)?

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You for speaking into real families and real grief, in words Your people could understand. Thank You that You will swallow up death forever and wipe away the tears from every face. When a passage sounds strange to us, give us patience to learn its world before we decide what it means. Keep us from using its setting as an excuse to ignore it. Teach us to greet one another as family, because in Jesus You have made us one. In Jesus' name, Amen.
