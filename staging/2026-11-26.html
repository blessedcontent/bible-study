Fulfilled Prophecies

Testing the ProphetsLesson 9Day 6 of 7

In brief

In the temple courtyard, the prophet Hananiah promises that Babylon's power will break within two years. Jeremiah, wearing a wooden yoke, says he hopes so and gives a test. Then Hananiah breaks Jeremiah's yoke. Before the year is out, Hananiah is dead. Yet Jonah's warning to Nineveh did not come true, and God was behind that too. God's predictions show whom He has sent, and His warnings depend on how people respond, so a warning that leads people to turn has done what He meant it to do.

Yesterday

Yesterday, Jesus said a tree is known by its fruit, and Scripture did not hide the failures of God's messengers. A life shows what kind of tree it is. Today asks what happens when a prophet's words meet the future.

Historical Context

Five Kings Sent Envoys to Jerusalem

Early in Zedekiah's reign, envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon came to Jerusalem (Jeremiah 27:3). Babylon ruled all of them, and many scholars think they had come to talk about breaking free. God told Jeremiah to put a yoke on his own neck and send word to their kings. Zedekiah heard the same message. Serve the king of Babylon and live (Jeremiah 27:2; Jeremiah 27:12).

In that setting, a word of peace was not a private comfort. It was advice about war. If Judah believed that Babylon's power was about to break, it would join the revolt. Zedekiah did rebel in the end, and Babylon's army came back to Jerusalem (2 Kings 24:20; 2 Kings 25:1). Whether a prophet of peace was right would be counted in lives.

Into the Word

Two prophets stood in the temple in front of the priests and the people. Jeremiah wore a wooden yoke on his neck. Hananiah announced that within two years God would break Babylon's yoke (Jeremiah 28:1-4). Jeremiah did not argue about who sounded more certain. He said amen and wished it were true (Jeremiah 28:6). Then he gave a test. Hananiah answered by pulling the yoke off Jeremiah's neck, breaking it, and saying that within two years God would break Babylon's power the same way (Jeremiah 28:10-11). The wood broke in a moment. The test would take longer.

If fulfillment were the whole test, though, Jonah would have failed it. He walked into Nineveh announcing that in forty more days the city would be overturned (Jonah 3:4). Forty days came and went, and Nineveh still stood. At a potter's house, God gave Jeremiah the rule that explains it (Jeremiah 18:1-6).

Street Level

He Said It So It Would Not Come True

You told me I wouldn't see sixty. The man says it with a grin, buttoning his shirt at his yearly checkup. He is sixty-two. Fourteen years earlier, in this same small room, with a pack of cigarettes in his shirt pocket and numbers on the screen that made his doctor go quiet, he heard the sentence that changed his life. If nothing changes, I don't think your heart makes it to sixty.

Something changed. He threw the cigarettes away in the parking lot that afternoon. He started walking after supper, one block at first, then three miles. He learned to cook without the salt shaker and, eventually, to like it. Now he tells the story at cookouts as the time the doctor got it wrong. In the exam room, the doctor laughs and shakes his head. I said if nothing changed, he says. I said it the first day. You are the reason I would say it again.

Jonah walked into Nineveh with a forty-day warning, and forty days later the city still stood, because its people had turned (Jonah 3:4-10). Through Jeremiah, God spelled out how His warnings work. When a nation He has warned turns from its evil, He relents (Jeremiah 18:7-10). The doctor did not add his if after the fact, and neither did God. It was there from the start, and the turning was the hearers' to do. Some predictions must come true before anyone can trust the one who made them (Jeremiah 28:9). The doctor's warning was meant to be outlived.

Christ at the center

Jesus also spoke about the future before it arrived. He told His disciples that He would suffer, be killed, and be raised on the third day (Matthew 16:21). That word came true in the most unlikely way a word could, with an empty tomb. On the night before He died, He told them He was going away to the Father and coming back to them. He was telling them before it happened, He said, so that when it happened they would believe (John 14:28-29). His warnings carry the same heart as God's word through Jonah. As He came near Jerusalem, He wept over the city because it did not recognize the time God came to it (Luke 19:41-44). His predictions show who He is. His warnings show what He wants. He wants people to turn and live.

Application

Read Jeremiah 18:8 and think of one warning in Scripture you tend to skip. Ask what turning it invites, and name one small way to turn toward God today.

Discussion Question

Why does it matter that God's warnings depend on how people respond while His character never changes? How might reading warnings as invitations change the way you read hard passages (or the way you warn others)?

Closing Prayer

Father, You do not change, and You take no pleasure in anyone's ruin. Thank You for warning us before trouble comes and for relenting when people turn to You. Forgive us for the times we heard Your warnings and shrugged, or wanted mercy for ourselves and judgment for others. Make us quick to turn when You speak. Keep us from false promises of peace, and give us Your heart for anyone who needs to hear the truth. In Jesus' name, Amen.
