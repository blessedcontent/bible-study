Sufferings for the Sake of the Gospel

Dealing With False TeachersLesson 12Day 5 of 7

In brief

Paul finally agrees to play the newcomers' game. Receive me as a fool, he says, and then he boasts, except the boast is a police record. Five floggings, three beatings with rods, a stoning, three shipwrecks, a night treading water, hunger, cold, and the daily weight of every church he loves. He wins the contest and immediately throws the trophy away, because a thorn taught him where his real strength was. Power lands in weakness, so the scars were never the boast. The grace that carried him through them was.

Yesterday

Yesterday Paul pulled the mask off the newcomers. They had changed into apostles' clothing, and the church that could spot a costume change in the theater could not spot this one in the pew, so Jesus' fruit test became the only test that worked. Today Paul does something stranger. He puts on their costume himself, for one paragraph, to show how ridiculous it looks.

Historical Context

Thirty-Nine Lashes Meant He Kept Coming Back

Every synagogue kept a strap, and every Jewish man knew what thirty-nine meant. The law in Deuteronomy allowed a judge to have a guilty man beaten, but it capped the blows at forty so that a brother would not be degraded in front of his own people. To stay safely under the limit, the practice was to stop at thirty-nine. The blows were counted, the man was bound, and the community that had raised him watched. It was a punishment for insiders. A Gentile would never receive it, and neither would a Jew who had walked away from the synagogue, because the synagogue had no claim on him anymore.

That is what makes Paul's number so striking. Five times. He could have avoided every one of them by doing what the newcomers would have called sensible, staying out of synagogues and preaching only where he was welcome. Instead he kept walking back into the one room where he knew the strap was waiting, because his own people were in it. The Roman rods were a different matter, a magistrate's punishment for troublemakers who could not claim citizen protection, the kind Paul took in Philippi. Between the two, his back carried the marks of both worlds he was trying to reach. When he lists them for Corinth, he is not asking for pity. He is showing them what it looks like when a servant refuses to stop coming back.

Into the Word

Imagine a man who hates bragging being forced into a bragging contest to save his children. That is Paul in 2 Corinthians 11:16-28. He has just exposed the newcomers as counterfeits (2 Corinthians 11:1-15), and now he does the thing he has refused to do for ten chapters. He boasts. But he announces first that it is foolishness, he keeps apologizing while he does it, and the résumé he produces would get anyone else laughed out of Corinth. Today's question is what a boast looks like in the hands of a man who has decided the boast is not the point.

Then he plays. He matches the newcomers' pedigree point for point (2 Corinthians 11:22-23), the same heritage they were so proud of (2 Corinthians 11:22), and on the one line that matters, servants of Christ, he claims more (2 Corinthians 11:23). Here is his proof of more.

If Paul stopped there, he would have won the contest on the newcomers' own terms, the toughest man in the room. He does not stop. He tells them about a thorn he begged God three times to remove (2 Corinthians 12:7), and about the answer he got instead.

Street Level

You Woke Up in Your Own Bed

Almost everybody has one of these memories. You fell asleep in the back seat on the way home from somewhere, a long drive, a late night, your head against the window. The next thing you knew, it was morning and you were in your own bed with your shoes off. No memory of the driveway. No memory of the stairs. Somewhere in there a parent unbuckled you, got you up onto a shoulder, opened the front door with a hip, and climbed the stairs in the dark carrying dead weight that kept sliding, then went back down for the bags. You never saw any of it. You just woke up home.

Ask the parent about it years later and they remember the cost. The arm that went numb halfway up. The back that complained the next morning. The night they nearly dropped you on the landing. None of it got mentioned at breakfast, and none of it was the point. The point is who got carried. The kid who insisted he was awake and could walk stumbled in on his own and remembers every step. The kid who was out cold, who could not have taken one step, arrived in bed without lifting a finger. The parent's strength did all of it, and it only did all of it for the one who could not.

Paul's scars are the parent's numb arm, the price of getting people home, and love does not mention them until it is made to. But the boast lands somewhere else. Grace covers what is missing, and Christ's power rests on the person who has stopped pretending to walk. "When I am weak, then I am strong" is a sleeping child's sentence. He did not get himself to bed. He was carried.

Today’s World

The Scientist Who Admitted Limits Was Trusted More

A team of psychologists asked what happens to a scientist's credibility when she admits the limits of what she knows. Across five preregistered studies with 2,034 participants, published in Nature Human Behaviour in late 2024, readers met a scientist named Dr. Moore in an article. In one version a colleague says she "is not afraid to assert what she knows." In the other, she "is not afraid to admit when she doesn't yet know something." Readers trusted the second Dr. Moore more, believed her research more, and in a later study were more willing to follow a scientist's advice. The effect held whether the scientist was a man or a woman and across racial backgrounds. Certainty did not buy credibility. Admitted limits did.

One more finding keeps this honest. In the final study the researchers had the scientist signal humility in different ways. When she spoke about herself, admitting she does not yet know everything, readers saw her as humble and still believed her research. When she instead listed the weaknesses of her own results, readers rated her as humble too, but belief in the research fell below that of a scientist who said nothing at all. Humility landed when it belonged to the person. Attached to the work as a disclaimer, it cost her.

The study measured trust in research, not faith, and says nothing about grace. What it shows is the instinct Paul was counting on in Corinth. A voice that admits what it cannot do is more believable than a voice that never loses, and the admission has to be real. Paul's weakness was never a speaking tactic. It was the plain report of a man who knew where his own strength ran out and had met the One whose power begins there.

Christ at the center

The voice that told Paul "My power is perfected in weakness" was not speaking in theory. It belonged to a man who had been flogged, mocked, and nailed up outside a city while people measured Him by appearances and walked away. By every standard Corinth used, the cross was the weakest moment in history, and it was where God did His mightiest work. That is why Jesus can answer a thorn with grace instead of removal. He knows weakness from the inside, and He knows what His Father accomplished through it. So when Paul boasts in his weaknesses, he is not being brave. He is standing where his Lord stood and expecting the same thing to happen, resurrection power showing up in a body that cannot save itself. That is the gospel in one sentence. We are not saved by getting strong. We are saved by a crucified Savior whose grace is enough, and the places we cannot fix are the places He most likes to work.

Application

Write your own list today, but not of things you have survived. Write the three places you feel weakest right now (a marriage that is strained, a body that keeps failing you, a job that is not working, a temptation you keep losing to). Beside each one, copy out the sentence Paul heard, "My grace is sufficient for you," and pray that line over that place before you go to bed. Do not ask for the weakness to be explained. Ask for Christ's power to rest on it, and leave the list where you will see it tomorrow.

Discussion Question

When you have to prove yourself (a job interview, your spouse's family, a new church), what do you put forward first and what stays hidden? Paul led with his scars and then with his weakness, so what would change in one relationship this week if the weak place became the first thing you were willing to show?

Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, we have spent a lot of energy looking strong. We have hidden the thorns, dressed up the failures, and hoped nobody would notice the places where we cannot do it ourselves, and we confess that we have sometimes trusted teachers for the same reason, because they looked like they never lost. Teach us to read the list of scars the way You read it, as the marks of love that kept walking toward people. Then teach us the harder thing, to stop boasting about what we survived and start bringing You what we cannot fix. Rest Your power on our weak places today, on the marriage, the body, the job, the habit, and let people see a strength in us that is plainly not ours. Make us servants who keep coming back to the people who need You, whatever it costs. In Jesus' name, Amen.
