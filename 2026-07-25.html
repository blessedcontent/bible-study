All to the Glory of God

All to the Glory of GodLesson 5Day 1 of 7

In brief

A bull walks up a temple ramp in Corinth, and by week's end its meat is on a dinner table with no label and no backstory. Around that table sit new believers with old memories, asking Paul what they are allowed to eat. This week Paul answers a menu question with a compass. Knowledge matters less than love. Rights exist to be laid down. Israel's wilderness graves are a warning, and two tables are set in every city. One line steers it all. Whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God.

Last week

Last week ended on a doorstep. The Owner of the house, having paid for every room, asked gently for the whole thing, and we learned that holiness comes by occupancy, the Spirit filling the space until the old appetites lose their seat. Now Paul walks that ownership claim out of the bedroom and into the dining room. The slogan he has already quoted twice, everything is permissible for me (1 Corinthians 6:12), is still hanging in the air, and this week he finally finishes the argument. If your body belongs to God, so do your calendar, your menu, and your grocery cart. The question of the week is what a purchased life is actually for.

Historical Context

Dinner in Corinth Came With a God Attached

Follow one bull through a Corinthian morning. It climbs the temple ramp with garlands on its horns, is dedicated to a god, and dies at the altar. A portion burns. A portion goes to the officiating priests. And then comes the detail that quietly ran the city's economy. The priests could never eat it all, so the surplus walked out the temple's back door and into the macellum, the public meat market, where it sold at an ordinary stall beside the fish and the figs. By the time it reached anyone's table, the god's name was not written on it anywhere. In Corinth, almost every cut of meat had a backstory, and the backstory was an altar.

Now put a new believer in that city. He passes the butcher's stall and remembers the festival where he last tasted meat like that, back when the god still owned him. His wealthier brother gets an invitation to a banquet in a temple dining room, the kind archaeologists have actually unearthed at Corinth's shrine of Asklepios, three stone couches to a room, dinner served under a god's roof. Say yes and you recline in an idol's house. Say no and you insult a patron, a business partner, a friend. Multiply that dilemma by every wedding, contract, and birthday in the city, and you understand the letter the Corinthians finally sent Paul. They asked him for a rule about food. He handed them a compass instead, and it points at the glory of God.

Christ at the center

Here is the seed to carry into the week. The verse at the center of everything calls us to live for the glory of God, and only one Person has ever done it without a gap. Jesus ate and drank His way through Galilee, at wedding feasts, at tax collectors' tables, in a borrowed upper room, and every meal made the Father look like exactly who He is. He could say out loud, I do not seek My own glory (John 8:50), and no one could produce a counterexample. Now watch where this week's argument finally lands, in a sentence most of us would be afraid to say. Imitate me, as I also imitate Christ (1 Corinthians 11:1). Paul learned his freedom from Jesus, the freest man who ever lived, who spent every ounce of that freedom on other people. By Friday, the question will not be what a Christian is allowed to touch. It will be what our choices make the watching world think of our God.

Application

Before Sunday, pick the most ordinary repeating act in your week, the one you have never once thought of as spiritual. The morning coffee. The commute. The grocery run. The lunch order. Choose one, and decide now what doing it for the glory of God will look like this week, concretely. Maybe that means gratitude said out loud, patience in the slow lane, generosity at the register, or simply doing the thing with full attention instead of a running complaint. Write the act and the plan somewhere you will see them. By Friday, check whether the most ordinary hour of your day has started pointing somewhere.

Opening Question for the Week

"When you think about the parts of your week that feel too small to be spiritual (the commute, the lunch order, the scroll before bed), which of them have you never once connected to God? And if that ordinary hour had one job this week, to make God look as good as He actually is, what is the first thing you would do differently?"

Opening Prayer for the Week

Father, we have kept a religious corner of our lives swept for You, and this week You are asking about the rest of the house. Paul is about to talk about eating and drinking, rights and freedoms, dinner tables and grocery lists, the parts of our days we have always filed under ordinary. Walk into those rooms with us. Teach us what it means that we were bought at a price, not only in what we avoid but in what we do on purpose. Where our freedom has been quietly costing someone else their footing, show us. Where our habits have been feeding on glory that belongs to You, name it, and we will not argue. Give us eyes to see the weight of Your name resting on the smallest verbs we own. We want the whole week, even the boring parts, to make You look like who You are. In Jesus' name, Amen.
