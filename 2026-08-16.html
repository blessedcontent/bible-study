Proclaiming the Resurrection of Christ

The Power of Christ's ResurrectionLesson 8Day 2 of 7

In brief

A letter is being read aloud in a crowded Corinthian living room, and Paul has just reached the words everyone learned first. Christ died for our sins. He was buried. He was raised on the third day. Then comes the astonishing move. Paul calls the witnesses, Peter, the Twelve, five hundred at once, and adds that most of them are still living. In plain terms, go ask them. The gospel is not good advice to live by. It is good news that happened, and it still saves everyone who holds on to it.

Yesterday

Yesterday you slipped a piece of paper into your Bible, a name now resting in the middle of the resurrection chapter, and we walked into Corinth past tombstones that shrugged. I was not. I was. I am not. I do not care. Today Paul begins answering the stones, but notice where he starts. Not with comfort, and not yet with your name. With facts. Before this chapter can hold your grief, it has to hold weight, so Paul lays the foundation first. The gospel he preached. The Scriptures behind it. The living eyewitnesses who saw the risen Lord with their own eyes.

Historical Context

The Words Paul Learned by Heart

In a world where most people would never own a book, memory did the work paper does for us. A student sat at his teacher's feet and learned the master's words by exact repetition, until he could recite them without a slip and hand them to students of his own. That hand-off had formal vocabulary. A teacher passed on the tradition. A student received it. When Paul writes that he passed on what he also received, Corinth heard the language of a chain of custody. He is not composing fresh sentences here. He is reciting a fixed formula that was placed in his hands, word for word, by the people who had it before him.

Now do the math that historians love about this passage. Paul is writing around twenty-five years after the crucifixion, but he received this creed at the beginning of his Christian life, within just a few years of the events themselves, from the very men named inside it. That timing changes everything. The resurrection was not a legend that crept in generations later, after the eyewitnesses were safely dead. It was the church's founding recitation, already fixed and memorized while the tomb was fresh news. The claim was not born in the dark. It was proclaimed in public while many named and unnamed witnesses were still living and could be questioned.

Into the Word

When the courier finally reaches this part of the letter, Paul does something disarming. He argues nothing. He rewinds. Let me remind you, he says, of the gospel I preached to you, the one you received, the one you are standing on right now. Some in Corinth had begun treating resurrection as negotiable, a topic for the seminar room. So Paul takes them back to the words that saved them, recites the message in its oldest form, and then calls a witness list long enough to fill a courtroom. Underneath it all sits one quiet, enormous question. Do you remember what you believed?

Before calling his witnesses, Paul pauses over a pairing he refuses to separate. What is preached and what is believed are the same thing, or they are nothing. By this gospel you are saved, if you hold firmly to the word that was preached (1 Corinthians 15:2), and whether the voice belonged to Paul or to anyone else, so we proclaim and so you believed (1 Corinthians 15:11). Preaching hands over a fact. Faith closes its hand around it. Neither works alone.

Street Level

You Know Your Birthday by Faith

Nobody remembers being born. Think about how strange that is. Your birthday is the most settled fact you own. You type the date into every form without a flicker of doubt, and yet you were in no condition to witness it. Everything you know about the day you arrived came to you secondhand. Your mother tells the story with details no form ever asks for, the storm that night, the nurse with the cold hands, the exact wrong thing your father said in the waiting room. The relatives retell it at every gathering, and the versions hold together. For most of your life you could test the story any afternoon you wanted, because the witnesses were sitting in the kitchen. You never doubted it. You could always ask.

That is the kind of knowing Paul offers Corinth, and notice what he never asks for. He does not ask anyone to believe into the dark. He recites testimony from people who were in the room, fixed early and told the same way from the beginning, and then adds the astonishing detail that most of the five hundred are still living. That is a man handing out addresses. Go ask them. Faith in the resurrection has never meant believing without evidence. It means trusting witnesses who have held up under a lifetime of asking, the same way you trust the people who were present at the one event your whole life stands on. You have run on tested testimony since you learned your own birthday. The gospel simply asks you to do it about a better morning.

Today’s World

The First Test Is the One That Counts

For decades the standard line, in courtrooms and psychology departments alike, was that eyewitness testimony is the weakest evidence there is. In August 2025, two leading memory researchers published a paper that complicates that story. Writing in the journal Memory, Laura Mickes of the University of Bristol and John Wixted of UC San Diego argued that many of the famous wrongful convictions were not so much failures of memory as failures to heed a witness's first answer. A memory, Wixted explains, works like the other trace evidence at a crime scene. DNA can be contaminated. Fingerprints can be contaminated. So can a remembered face, every time it is retested, rehearsed, and coached toward trial. On the first properly run test, before the contamination starts, the evidence suggests a confident identification is far more dependable than its courtroom reputation. In many of the DNA exoneration cases, the witness's earliest response actually pointed away from the man who ended up in prison. The first answer was often sound. The system stopped listening after it.

Set that beside what Paul does in today's passage. He does not hand Corinth a story that had simmered privately for decades. He recites the church's first answer, a formula fixed within a few years of the events and received from the named men inside it, then lists the witnesses and adds that most are still living and easy to ask. Scripture preserved the very thing the memory researchers now prize, the earliest record of what the witnesses said, set down before time or pressure could bend it. The parallel deserves honest limits. A study of police lineups cannot test a first-century identification, and no journal article can prove the resurrection. What the research does offer is a reason to take early, well-preserved testimony seriously instead of dismissing witnesses on principle. That is precisely the kind of testimony the church has been carrying for two thousand years, cross-examined by people certain the witnesses had to be wrong, and never revised. Christ died, was buried, was raised, was seen. The first answer is still on the record, and it still deserves a hearing.

Christ at the center

Every thread of today ties back to a Person who insists on being alive. The gospel Paul recites is not the portrait of a martyr we are asked to admire. It is the announcement that the Man who carried our sins into a grave carried them out again, emptied of their power. He was seen. That changes what kind of faith Christianity is allowed to be. We are not curators of a beautiful memory. We are witnesses of a living Lord who still forgives, still calls, and still walks into locked rooms. Look at the witness list itself. Peter, who denied Him, restored by Him. James, who doubted Him, convinced. Paul, who hunted His church, arrested by grace on an open road. The risen Christ has a habit of turning His witnesses into His heralds, and He has not lost it.

Application

Someone once handed you the message Paul hands Corinth. A parent, a friend, a stranger with more courage than you knew. Today, be one link in that same chain. Tell one person one specific thing you have seen God do, an answered prayer, a change in you, a rescue you cannot otherwise explain. Say it plainly, no sermon attached. If speaking feels too big, write it instead, one text message, sent today. Witnesses report what they saw. Start there.

Discussion Question

"What actually holds up your confidence that Christ is risen (the eyewitness record, answered prayer, a change you have watched God work in someone, your own walk with Him)? And what else do you treat as completely true even though you never saw it yourself, and what does that tell you about how faith and evidence work together?"

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that the gospel is news before it is anything else, news of something You did in the daylight of history. Thank You for the chain of faithful hands that carried it from an empty garden tomb across twenty centuries to us. Forgive us for treating the resurrection like a decoration on our faith instead of the ground underneath it. Steady those of us who believe and still carry questions, and let the witnesses' testimony give our confidence something solid to stand on. Then make us witnesses too. Put one name on our hearts today, and give us plain courage to say what we have seen You do. We do not want to guard this news. We want to hand it on. In Jesus' name, Amen.
