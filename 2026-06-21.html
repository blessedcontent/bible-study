Living Today

Into EternityLesson 13Day 2 of 7

In brief

You scroll the morning headlines and feel it in your chest: wars, outbreaks, the ground itself shaking. Jesus said these would come. James adds a quieter alarm. Your own life is a wisp of steam off a kettle, here and gone. None of it is meant to make us frantic. It is meant to make us awake. An ancient songwriter watching his nation collapse prayed one thing on repeat: turn back to us, make Your face shine, save us. The shortness of life is not a reason to panic but a reason to make sure of God today.

Yesterday

Yesterday we stood among the old Roman tombstones with their four-letter shrug at the grave and heard the Bible answer with a stubborn hope: we will see Him, and seeing Him will finish us. But a promise about the far end of the story always raises a question about the middle of it. If that is where we are headed, how do we live in the meantime, in a world that still heaves and groans and buries its dead? Today turns from the destination to the road. The same hope that lets us face a graveside without despair is supposed to change the way we wake up tomorrow morning.

Historical Context

The Prayer of a Nation Watching Its Walls Come Down

This psalm was written by someone watching the worst happen in slow motion. The songwriter, Asaph, looks back to the day God carried a young vine out of Egypt, cleared the ground, and planted it so it could spread until its branches reached the sea. That vine was Israel. Now, generations later, the singer stares at the same vineyard with its protective walls torn open, animals trampling through, the branches cut and burned. This is almost certainly a cry from the years the northern kingdom was being chewed up by the Assyrian empire, a real political collapse playing out one ruined town at a time.

What makes the psalm remarkable is what the singer does with the catastrophe. He does not draft a military strategy or a list of who to blame. He reaches past every visible cause to the only One who can actually fix it, and he asks the same thing three times like a chorus he cannot stop singing: turn us back, make Your face shine, save us. He has figured out that the deepest problem behind a collapsing world is a people who have drifted from the face of God, and the only real solution is to have that face turned toward them again. That instinct, to answer a frightening age with a prayer for revival rather than a panic, is exactly what today's reading is teaching us.

Into the Word

Look honestly at the times and you can feel two pressures at once. There is the big, global kind of dread that Jesus named in advance, and the small, personal kind that James names so bluntly it stings. Today's reading sets those two right next to a 3,000-year-old prayer, because the people who handle a serious age best are not the ones who pretend it is not serious. They are the ones who let the seriousness drive them straight to God's face.

Street Level

The Diver Who Slows Down

Ask anyone who teaches scuba diving what actually kills people underwater, and the answer is rarely the shark or the broken gear. It is panic. A diver feels their air running low or their mask floods, and the body screams one instruction, and it is the wrong one. Thrash. Bolt for the surface. Fight. That instinct is almost always what turns a manageable problem into a fatal one. So the first thing instructors drill, over and over in the shallow end, is the opposite reflex. When something goes wrong, you stop. You breathe slow. You think. Then you act. The calm is not a personality trait you hope to have on the bad day. It is a habit you build on a hundred easy dives so it is already in you when the real trouble comes.

That is almost exactly what Peter and James are teaching about living in a serious age. The headlines flood the mask. Your own life is a vapor and you can feel it. Every nerve says to panic, or to numb out and stop thinking about it at all. The Bible says do neither. Be clear-headed. Pray. The way you make it through a frightening world is not by bracing harder for the emergency but by being so used to turning your face toward God on the ordinary days that the turning is already a reflex when the water rushes in. You do not wait for the crisis to make sure of Him. You make sure of Him now, in the shallow end, while it is calm.

Today’s World

Eighty-Five Seconds to Midnight

In January 2026, the scientists behind the Doomsday Clock moved its hands to eighty-five seconds before midnight, the closest to catastrophe it has stood since they first built it in 1947. The clock is set each year by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists' Science and Security Board, in consultation with a board of sponsors that includes several Nobel laureates. It is their way of telling the human race how near they think we are to wrecking ourselves, and this year they pointed at nuclear arsenals, runaway artificial intelligence, engineered disease, and a warming planet all at once. You do not need a physicist to feel it. You feel it scrolling the news before you are even out of bed. The reading was not wrong. The world really is that serious.

Notice, though, what a clock like that can do to a person. It can push you to panic, or it can push you to numb out and stop looking altogether. Peter and James, who lived in a genuinely dangerous age of their own, point to a third door. Be clear-headed. Pray. The nearness of midnight is not a reason to lose your head. It is the reason to keep it, and to turn your short, vapor-quick life toward the face of God while there is still time on the clock. The scientists are right that it is late. They just do not know the One who holds the hands.

Christ at the center

The songwriter prayed "cause Your face to shine upon us" without knowing how God would finally answer it. The answer has a name. In Jesus, the face of God turned toward us in person, and not from a safe distance. He walked into the heaving, groaning, dying world James was describing and lived a real, vapor-short human life inside it, thirty-three years that ended on a Friday afternoon. He did it so His unbroken record could be credited to us, so that the favor the songwriter begged for would no longer depend on our performance. On the cross the face of God did the opposite of shine; it was turned away from Him, so that it could be turned permanently toward us. That is why a believer can read the worst headlines and the shortest obituary and still be steady. The face is already shining. It was secured at Golgotha and it is never turning away again.

Application

Pray Psalm 80:18-19 in the first person today. Take the refrain, "Restore me, make Your face shine on me, so that I may be saved," and pray it slowly three times: once over the state of the world, once over someone you love who is far from God, and once over your own drifting places. Then leave the panic where you found it and go live the rest of the day as someone whose God has already turned toward them.

Discussion Question

"When the world feels like it is coming apart, what does your gut reach for first (the news feed, the worst-case scenario, a way to numb out)? What would it look like this week to let that same moment of dread send you to God's face instead, praying for revival rather than just bracing for impact?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we live in serious times, and we feel it. The news frightens us, and our own lives are shorter and less certain than we like to admit. Keep us clear-headed instead of frantic. When the ground shakes, teach us to pray before we spiral. We borrow the oldest prayer of Your people and make it our own: turn back to us, make Your face shine on us, and save us. Thank You that in Jesus that face is already turned our way for good. Help us live today, this ordinary day, like people who are sure of You. In Jesus' name, Amen.
