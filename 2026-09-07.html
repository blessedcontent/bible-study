Motivation

Stewardship and MissionLesson 11Day 3 of 7

In brief

An offering plate is passing down the row, and nobody watching can tell what is happening inside the hands that touch it. One person is paying protection money to a God he fears. One is keeping a family habit. One is managing an image. Paul spends two chapters naming the fuels that burn clean, gratitude for grace, the example of Jesus, blessings meant to keep moving, and love that can be seen. Then the man of many words runs out of them entirely. Thanks be to God for His indescribable gift. God is not funding a budget. He is growing givers who look like His Son.

Yesterday

Yesterday closed with a receipt. Jesus, rich beyond imagining, chose beggar-poverty on purpose so that we could become rich, and Paul restarted Corinth's stalled pledge by simply holding that receipt up. Maybe you took the challenge and gave something that actually cost you. If so, you already know today's question from the inside, because somewhere between deciding and giving, you could feel your own reasons shifting under you. Am I doing this out of love or leftover guilt? Gratitude or image? Today Paul opens the engine compartment of generosity and names the only fuels that burn clean. It turns out God cares less about the amount in the hand than the reason in it.

Historical Context

Pagan Giving Was a Contract With the Gods

A merchant stands before a roadside altar with a problem and a proposition. His ship leaves for Italy in the morning, so he speaks to the god the way he speaks to a business partner. Bring the cargo home safe, and a fine goat is yours. All over the Greek and Roman world, stone altars carried the closing line of that transaction, a standard formula so common that carvers abbreviated it, the vow paid, willingly and deservedly. Religion ran on the same logic as the marketplace. I give so that you will give. Gifts to the gods were opening bids, premiums on divine insurance, invoices settled after services rendered. The dominant logic had little room for gratitude. You gave to obtain, and you kept the receipts.

Now listen to how strange Paul sounds against that backdrop. In two chapters about giving, he never once positions the offering as a way to get something from God. The direction is completely reversed. Grace has already been given, the Gift has already arrived, the debt runs the other way and can never be paid anyway. So the Christian gives the way a rescued man says thank you, not the way a merchant buys a favorable wind. Corinth's converts had spent their whole lives giving-to-get. Today's passages hand them, and us, an entirely new set of reasons, and every one of them starts from something already received.

Into the Word

Read straight through the two chapters of Paul's appeal and a pattern emerges. Giving language saturates every paragraph (2 Corinthians 8:1-9:15). Grace given, selves given, gifts decided in the heart, generosity scattered to the poor, and thanks rising to God at the end of it all. The chapters open with giving and close with giving, and in between Paul quietly answers a question most fundraisers never touch. Not how much, and not even to whom, but why. Trace the reasons with him, one passage at a time, and notice where each one begins.

Gratitude is the first fuel, but watch what the Macedonians actually handed over before a single coin moved.

The third motive answers a quieter worry, the fear that generosity will leave the giver empty. Paul points at where the supply actually comes from.

One motive remains, the simplest and the most exposing. Love. Paul is careful to say he is not commanding anyone, he is testing the sincerity of their love against the earnestness of others (2 Corinthians 8:8), and he asks Corinth to give the watching churches proof of that love in something they can see (2 Corinthians 8:24). Talk is cheap in every century. Giving is love with a receipt. And when all four motives finally converge, they collapse into a single sentence of worship.

Street Level

The Thank-You Note Nobody Made You Write

Most of us wrote our first thank-you notes under guard. A parent stood over the kitchen table the day after the birthday, dictating warmth one line at a time. Dear Aunt Carol. Thank you for the sweater. I love it. You signed your name like a prisoner signing a confession, and the note was true, and it meant almost nothing, because it was really a ransom, the price of being released back outside to play. Now set that note next to the other kind, the one nobody makes you write. Years after a teacher talked you out of quitting, whatever she gave you finally finishes its work, and you find yourself at thirty hunting down an address. The sentences come out nearly identical. Thank you. It mattered. I still have it. Same words, opposite act.

The difference is not the gratitude claimed on the page. It is the direction the letter travels. The kitchen-table note pushes an obligation away so an account can close. The letter at thirty overflows from an account that will never close, and it asks for nothing, because everything was already received. That distance is the distance Paul walks in these two chapters. Corinth's altars ran on the first kind of giving, payment rendered, favor expected, receipts kept. What God wants is the second kind, and He grows it the only way it grows, by giving first and then waiting for the thanks to need somewhere to go. So run today's question through your mailbox. The same gift can be a bill getting paid or a love letter getting answered. One is a transaction. The other is worship, and God has never once confused the two.

Today’s World

The Book Lady Started With a Daddy Who Couldn't Read

When Dolly Parton died in late August 2026 at age eighty, the tributes named the hits and the rhinestones, and then many of them landed on the same quiet machine. In 1995 she started mailing free books to children in her home county in East Tennessee, one book a month, addressed to the child by name, from birth to age five, no fee and no income test. The Imagination Library spread from one county to all fifty states and on to other countries, and by the week of her death it had given away more than 332 million books. The reason behind it never changed. Her father, Robert Lee Parton, one of the smartest men she knew, had never learned to read. She built the whole thing as a tribute to him, and the children who got the books simply called her the Book Lady.

Look at what that motive did to the shape of the giving. There was nothing to collect. Toddlers cannot repay, most of those families would never meet her, and the books kept mailing long after any career could benefit. This was not a transaction, and it was not image management either. She liked to say her daddy was prouder of the Book Lady than of anything she ever did on a stage. It was love for one man, overflowing onto millions of children he never met, which is exactly the current Paul has been describing. Giving that starts from something already received does not need to be squeezed out, and it does not quit when the cameras leave. The mailings did not even stop when she did. That is what a gift looks like when the reason in the hand is love.

Christ at the center

Line up Paul's four motives and notice that they are one motive wearing four coats. Gratitude, for what? For Christ. Imitation, of whom? Of Christ. Blessings shared, from where? From the God who supplies seed to the sower and gave the Seed that fell into the ground and died. Love, proven how? The way His was proven, in something you can see, at a cross. Every clean reason for giving has the same face. And here is the wonder inside today's last verse. When God wanted to move the world to generosity, He did not send an invoice or an argument. He sent His Son, and then let the gift itself do the persuading. The indescribable Gift has no matching donation level. Nobody gives back at that scale. All we can do is what Paul does, run out of words, say thank You, and let the thanks leak into how we live. Gratitude is the currency Paul's whole appeal has been asking for. It just refuses to stay in the wallet.

Application

Run a motive check on your very next act of giving, today if possible. Before the money or the time leaves your hands, stop for ten seconds and answer honestly. Why am I doing this? If the answer smells like guilt, image, or bargaining with God, do not cancel the gift. Re-aim it. Write one sentence first, on the memo line, in your notes app, or out loud, naming something specific God has given you. Then give as a thank-you note instead of a payment. Same gift, new fuel.

Discussion Question

"If you traced your last few acts of giving to their real fuel, what would you find (gratitude, love, habit, guilt, image, a quiet deal with God)? And which of Paul's four motives, gratitude for grace, the example of Jesus, sharing what God supplied, or visible love, would most change your giving if it took the wheel this month?"

Closing Prayer

Father, You see what we do with our hands and You also see why, and today that second part has our attention. We confess the old altar logic still living in us, the giving that bargains, the giving that performs, the giving that pays protection money to a God we forgot loves us. Forgive us for handing You contracts when You handed us grace first. Fill our motives with better fuel. Make us grateful people, so stunned by the Gift we cannot describe that thankfulness needs somewhere to go. Make us imitators of Jesus, who gave Himself before He gave anything else. Make us channels, not reservoirs, for every blessing You supply. And make our love visible enough to audit. Whatever leaves our hands this week, let it leave for the right reason. Thanks be to You for Your indescribable Gift. In Jesus' name, Amen.
