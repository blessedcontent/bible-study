Antidote Against Sexual Immorality

Sin in the ChurchLesson 4Day 5 of 7

In brief

Somewhere in Corinth a believer is quoting slogans over dinner. Food for the stomach, the stomach for food, the body is just appetite hardware, so what you do with it means nothing. Paul answers with the strangest sentence in the whole argument. The body is for the Lord, and the Lord is for the body. Then he stacks three facts underneath it, you were washed, you are members of Christ, you are a temple of the Spirit. Your body is not a shell you happen to use. It is a place God lives.

Yesterday

Yesterday ended with a list and a caption. Sexually immoral, idolaters, thieves, greedy, that is what some of you were, and then the caption, not anymore. You were washed. But anyone who has carried a strong habit into a new life knows the morning-after question. If I am washed, why does the old pull still feel so loud? Today Paul answers with the word underneath this entire week. Sanctification. Not a rule book bolted onto grace, but the Spirit's slow, deliberate work of setting a whole person apart for God. The antidote to a corrupted culture was never going to be willpower. It is belonging, worked all the way down into the body.

Historical Context

The Philosophers Called the Body a Tomb

Somewhere at a Corinthian dinner party, a man with a cup of wine is explaining the universe. The real you, he says, is the soul. The body is a costume, a tomb the soul is locked inside until death finally opens the door. Greek thought had played variations on that tune for centuries, the spirit noble, matter beneath notice. And the doctrine came with a convenient corollary. If the body is just temporary packaging, then what you do with it is spiritually beside the point. Some thinkers took that logic toward harsh self-denial, starving the costume to free the soul. Corinth, being Corinth, mostly took it the other direction. Feed the appetite, visit the brothel, follow the urge. The soul stays clean. The meat does what meat does.

Half the church's slogans in this letter are that philosophy wearing Christian clothes. Food for the stomach and the stomach for food. Everything is permissible for me. The new converts had carried the city's body-shrug right through the baptistry with them, and it was quietly licensing everything from the scandal to the brothel visits Paul is about to name. That is why his counterattack sounds so physical. Bodies washed. Bodies joined to Christ. Bodies indwelt by the Spirit. Scripture refuses to treat flesh as packaging, because God does not save souls out of bodies. He redeems people, and people come embodied. In a city that called the body a tomb, Paul called it a temple. Only one of those gets cleaned for company.

Into the Word

Why did one church generate this many problems? A scandal, lawsuits, brothel visits, and more questions waiting in line. Paul's diagnosis is not that Corinth was uniquely wicked. It is that the church had misread its own freedom. So before he finishes his argument about the body, we step sideways into a letter he wrote to another young church breathing the same Greek air (1 Thessalonians 4:1-8). There Paul lays out the antidote in a single word, and the word is not willpower. It is God's own will for every believer, worked by God's own Spirit. Sanctification.

Now back to Corinth, where freedom had gone feral. Some believers reasoned that since the gospel set them free, they were free to do anything, and they had slogans to prove it. Just as the stomach was made for food, they said, the body was made for sex (1 Corinthians 6:13). Paul calls that a misuse of the gift. Christ frees us from the law of sin (Romans 8:2), and that freedom is not an opening for the flesh but a call to serve one another in love (Galatians 5:13). Set free from sin, Scripture says, we became servants of righteousness, bound to God, with sanctification as the payoff (Romans 6:18, Romans 6:22). Free was never the same as unclaimed. The body is for the Lord, and the Lord is for the body, and the rest of the chapter (1 Corinthians 6) unpacks that one claim three ways.

Street Level

The Collar Was Never the Cage

The gate gets left open one morning, and the dog is gone before anyone notices. For the first twenty minutes it is glorious. No fence, no leash, nobody saying no. He tears down three streets with his ears back, drunk on it. But watch what that freedom does to him by late afternoon. He is miles past anything he recognizes, thirsty, footsore, flinching at traffic. By dark he is shaking under a stranger's porch, and the liberty that felt like flying at nine in the morning feels exactly like being lost. Because that is what it was. Meanwhile, two neighborhoods over, a family is driving slow with the windows down, calling his name into the dark.

Corinth's slogans were an open gate. Everything is permissible for me, the body is only appetite, run wherever it pulls. Paul answers with a collar. You are not your own. You were bought at a price. And before that sounds confining, remember what a collar actually is. Not the cage. The claim. The tag on it tells the whole world that somebody loves this one, somebody paid for him, and if he turns up lost there is a home to call. Nobody looks at a dog with no claim on him and calls him free. They call him a stray. Appetite promises open country and delivers the far porch, shaking in the dark. But the Owner who bought you is already in the street with the windows down, calling your name. Freedom was never the absence of a master. It is belonging to the One who comes looking.

Today’s World

The Revolution Ended in a Famine

For fifty years the culture has been running something very like Corinth's experiment at national scale, dismantling boundary after boundary around sex on one promise, that a body freed from covenant would finally be satisfied. The numbers coming back are strange. Analyzing the 2024 General Social Survey, researchers at the Institute for Family Studies reported in 2025 that the share of American adults having sex even once a week has fallen from 55 percent in 1990 to 37 percent today, a level the researchers call a record low. Among adults under thirty, one in four reported no sex at all in the past year, double the rate of 2010. Whatever else the era of fewer rules has delivered, more sex is not on the list. And buried in the data sits the punchline nobody ordered. The people having the most sex are the married ones, the folks who signed the lifelong covenant the culture kept calling a cage.

Paul heard Corinth's slogan, food for the stomach and the body for sex, and answered that a body is not appetite hardware. It is a temple, built for union and belonging, joined to Christ Himself. The researchers themselves point to prosaic suspects for the decline, fewer marriages, less face-to-face social life, more hours alone with screens. A survey can count the retreat. It cannot rule on the cause, and honesty says so out loud. But read the trend from Paul's side of the argument and it looks like a parable, a culture that learned to treat bodies like hardware, swiped, rated, and returned, quietly losing its appetite for the very thing it deregulated. The design was never a restriction on joy. It was the address of it. The ancient claim is aging very well.

Christ at the center

If the body were really just packaging, God would never have worn one. That is the doctrine that detonates every body-shrug philosophy in Corinth, and it is the heartbeat under this whole chapter. The Word became flesh. Jesus did not beam truth down from a safe spiritual distance. He took on lungs, hunger, fingerprints, and fatigue, and when they demanded a sign from Him, He pointed at Himself and called His own body the temple (John 2:19-21). Destroy it, He said, and in three days I will raise it. He meant it literally. Easter morning did not release Jesus from His body. It raised that body, scars included, and He wears it still. Heaven judged human flesh worth keeping forever. So when Paul tells you that your body is a temple, he is not flattering you. He is tracing your body's dignity straight back to the Man who bought it with His. The cross was a bodily act. So is following Him.

Application

Pick the one bodily arena where your private logic still runs on my body, my business, what you eat or drink, what you look at late at night, how you treat sleep, how you treat touch. Today, deliberately present that exact arena to God as worship. Say it in plain words, this body is Yours, bought at a price, and then live the next hour like it is true. If it helps, write bought at a price where you will see it all week, on the mirror, the lock screen, the dashboard.

Discussion Question

"Where in your life has the world's body-logic quietly set the rules, it is my body, it is natural, everyone does it (what you consume, what you watch, who you let define your worth)? And if that one area really is a temple where the Spirit lives, what is the first piece of furniture that would have to move?"

Closing Prayer

Father, we bring You our actual bodies today, the tired ones, the aging ones, the ones we have criticized in mirrors and misused in the dark. Thank You that You do not deal in souls only, that Your Son wore flesh, that Your Spirit chose these bodies for a home. We confess the places we have lived like tenants with no landlord, feeding appetites and calling it freedom. Wash us again in what You have already declared over us, washed, sanctified, justified. Where the old habits shout, let Your quiet ownership be louder. Teach us to govern our own bodies in holiness and honor, not as a performance for You but as worship with You. And for everyone among us carrying sexual regret, speak the old word over them once more today. Clean. In Jesus' name, Amen.
